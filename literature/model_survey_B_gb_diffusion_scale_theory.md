# Model survey B — Grain-boundary diffusion and oxide-scale kinetic theory

Full-text read of 11 papers, assessed against three questions relevant to the manuscript's coupled RIS–GB-oxidation model (1D oxygen transport along a GB with a phase-composition-dependent D_eff over Cr2O3 / Fe3O4 / FeCr2O4 / SiO2 (+porosity), interfacial reaction rates driven by RIS-modified GB Cr/Fe/Si, and an intergranular oxide penetration depth vs time and dose as the observable):

- **(a)** Does the description assume a *constant* GB (or oxide) diffusivity and give an *analytical* penetration law?
- **(b)** Does transport depend on the *type/composition of the oxide already formed* (phase, composition, porosity)?
- **(c)** Does *alloy/GB composition enter as input* and is an *evolving oxide composition* predicted?

**Method note.** The PDFs could not be rendered as page images (no poppler on this machine), so every page of every PDF was read through its full text layer, extracted with macOS PDFKit (`PDFDocument.page(at:).string`). All pages, appendices and tables were read; reference lists were skimmed only. Figures were not viewed — statements about figures come from captions and body text. Page numbers quoted are the printed journal page numbers. Equation references use the papers' own numbering. Where OCR-style corruption affected formula text, equations are paraphrased in words rather than transcribed.

## 1. fisher1951calculation

**Citation (as printed).** J. C. Fisher, "Calculation of Diffusion Penetration Curves for Surface and Grain Boundary Diffusion," *Journal of Applied Physics* **22**(1), 74–77 (1951). General Electric Research Laboratory, Schenectady. Received July 19, 1950.

**Model type and governing equations.** Analytical (plus finite-difference check) model of tracer diffusion along a thin high-diffusivity slab (the GB) of thickness δ embedded in a semi-infinite low-diffusivity solid, surface held at fixed concentration from t = 0. Eq. (1): Fick flux P = −D grad C with D constant; Eq. (2): Arrhenius D. Eq. (3): concentration in the slab obeys ∂c/∂t = D' ∂²c/∂y² + (2/δ) D (∂c/∂x)|₀, the second term being the leakage from the two slab faces into the bulk. Eq. (4): bulk diffusion ∂c/∂t = D ∇²c, reduced to one-dimensional transverse diffusion when penetration along the slab is much larger. Eq. (5): dimensionless variables t₁ = Dt/δ², x₁ = x/δ, y₁ = y/[δ(D'/D)^{1/2}]. Eqs. (3a,4a) reduced forms; (3b,4b) finite-difference equations (Fig. 2 network). Eqs. (6)–(8): quasi-steady approximation leading to the closed-form concentration C = exp(−2^{1/2} y₁/(π^{1/4} t₁^{1/4})) · erfc(x₁/(2 t₁^{1/2})) [Eq. (8)]. Eqs. (9)–(12): integrated quantity between sectioning planes; Eq. (13): log a = −[2^{1/2}(D/D'δ)^{1/2} log e/(πDt)^{1/4}] y + const.; Eq. (14): slope d log a/dy. Eqs. (15)–(18): extraction of D/D'δ = 21.3 cm⁻¹ and D'/D = 0.94×10⁶ for δ ≈ 5×10⁻⁸ cm.

**Transport — constant diffusivity?** Yes, strictly. "where P is the flux and C the concentration of diffusing material, and D is constant" (p. 74). "D' and D are the diffusion coefficients for diffusion in and outside the slab, and the subscript zero on ∂c/∂x means that the derivative is to be evaluated just outside the slab, at its surface" (p. 75). No dependence on phase, composition, or porosity; the GB is "a thin layer of high diffusivity material sandwiched between large volumes of low diffusivity material, in a manner analogous to the diffusion of heat along a copper foil imbedded in cork" (p. 74).

**Alloy/GB composition as input? Evolving oxide composition?** No. Pure self-diffusion (radioactive Ag in Ag). No oxide, no alloy chemistry.

**Irradiation / RIS.** Not mentioned.

**Prediction and data.** Predicted: concentration in the GB decays exponentially with depth, so a plot of log(specific activity) vs depth is a straight line (Eq. 13) whose slope scales as (D/D'δ)^{1/2}(Dt)^{-1/4} — i.e., the characteristic GB penetration depth grows as t^{1/4} (for fixed bulk leakage) and as (D'δ)^{1/2}. Compared with Hoffman and Turnbull's grain-boundary self-diffusion of radioactive silver into polycrystalline silver, 63 h at 478 °C (Fig. 4, p. 76): "As predicted by Eq. (13), the points lie upon a straight line, within the limit of experimental error" (p. 77). "It is concluded that diffusion penetration curves for grain boundary self-diffusion are adequately approximated by Eq. (13), and that the corresponding concentration of diffusing material in the neighborhood of a grain boundary is adequately approximated by Eq. (8)" (p. 77).

**Authors' stated limitations.** "It is assumed that the slab is so thin that concentration variations across its width are negligible" (p. 75). The reduction of Eq. (4) holds "Whenever the penetration of diffusing material is much greater in the slab than elsewhere" (p. 75). Accuracy of the numerical C-values "is about ±10 percent or ±0.005 C_max, whichever is larger" (p. 76). Extracting D' requires an assumed GB thickness: "In order to obtain a value for D' or for the ratio D'/D, a grain boundary thickness must be assumed" (p. 77). Requires a constant source: "surface diffusion and diffusion in the highly disordered electrodeposited layer of radioactive silver are sufficiently rapid to maintain a nearly steady concentration ... at the exposed edge of each grain boundary" (p. 76).

**Relation to the manuscript's model.** This is the archetype of category (a): a constant-D_gb slab with analytical penetration law (exponential in depth, t^{1/4} scaling); the manuscript's oxygen-transport equation is structurally a Fisher-type GB equation but replaces the constant D' by a phase-composition-dependent D_eff and adds consumption by interfacial reactions, which removes the closed-form solution.

## 2. klinger1999fisher

**Citation (as printed).** L. Klinger and E. Rabkin, "Beyond the Fisher Model of Grain Boundary Diffusion: Effect of Structural Inhomogeneity in the Bulk," *Acta mater.* **47**(3), 725–734 (1999). Technion, Haifa. Received 30 July 1998; accepted 29 November 1998.

**Model type and governing equations.** Analytical (Laplace-transform) extension of the Fisher model in which the bulk is "a stochastic mixture of defect-free crystalline regions and regions of bad material inside the short-circuit paths" (abstract, p. 725) — dislocations, subgrain boundaries, or lamellar interphase boundaries — with volume fraction g and diffusivity D_d. Eq. (1): Frank hollow-core radius (motivation only). Eqs. (2a)–(2c): coupled diffusion equations for c_b (GB), c_g (bulk grain, averaged) and c_d (short-circuit paths), with a phenomenological exchange term m(c_d − c_g); Eq. (3): total concentration c = (1−g)c_g + g c_d; Eq. (4): boundary conditions c_g = c_d = c_b at the GB plane. Eqs. (5a)–(5c): quasi-steady (B₂-regime) reduction. Eq. (6): m for a dislocation network (Wuttig–Birnbaum form, m ∝ D/[r_d² ln(r_m/r_d)]); Eqs. (7)–(11): m for parallel subgrain boundaries, m = Dπ²/(δ·2d) [Eq. (11)]. Eqs. (12)–(15): Laplace solution c̄_b, with ⟨D⟩ = (1−g)D + gD_d [Eq. (14)]. Eqs. (16)–(18): Fisher/Whipple/Le Claire limit for g = 0, Fisher length L₀_y = [δD_b/(D/t)^{1/2}]^{1/2} [Eq. (17)], ln c_b = a + b w^{6/5} [Eq. (18)]. Eq. (19): limit gD_d ≫ D; Eq. (20): layer activity A; Eq. (21): explicit c_b; Eq. (22): new length L_B = [δD_b/(g²mD_d)^{1/2}]^{1/2}; Eq. (23): A(y,t) in closed form; Eq. (24): Whipple–Le Claire formula for δD_b. Eqs. (25)–(27): regime criteria and critical fraction g*. Table 1 (p. 732): sequence of regimes C, B, D1, D2, D3 vs. D/g²D_d and g/g*. Appendices A–C give the Laplace derivations.

**Transport — constant diffusivity?** Yes — all three diffusivities are constants; the "inhomogeneity" is a fixed mixture, not an evolving composition. "In the spirit of the classical Fisher model, we again consider the GB as a high-diffusivity, semi-infinite isotropic slab of uniform width δ [1]. However, the bulk in which the GB is embedded, is not homogeneous anymore, but penetrated by the high-diffusivity paths running in all possible directions (Fig. 1). The diffusion coefficients both along the GB, D_b, and along the high-diffusivity paths (dislocations, subgrain boundaries, coherent or semicoherent interphase boundaries), D_d, are much larger than the bulk diffusion coefficient D, while D_d ≤ D_b" (p. 727). The paper's own characterization of the standard model it extends: "the highly idealized Fisher model [3], in which the GB is represented by a homogeneous slab with the diffusivity D_b embedded in the homogeneous bulk of the diffusivity D" (p. 725). No oxide, porosity, or composition dependence. The effective leakage diffusivity does change *with time* through saturation of the short-circuit network: "with increasing time the effective diffusivity responsible for the leakage from the GB gradually increases from g²D_d to gD_d" (p. 732).

**Alloy/GB composition as input? Evolving oxide composition?** No. Single diffusant in a chemically fixed host; no oxide.

**Irradiation / RIS.** Not mentioned (dislocation densities of annealed vs. deformed material are discussed, p. 725–726, 732).

**Prediction and data.** Three kinetic modes (p. 730): "1. Short times, mt ≪ 1. The penetration depth along the GB followed the Fisher relationship (17), but with the bulk diffusivity D replaced by g²D_d. 2. Intermediate times, 1 ≪ mt ≪ g⁻¹. The penetration depth was a weak function of time. 3. Long times, mt ≫ g⁻¹. The penetration depth along the GB again followed the Fisher relationship (17), but with the bulk diffusivity D replaced by gD_d." The Whipple–Le Claire extraction Eq. (24) "is a very good approximation (accuracy about 1.5%) for the short times, if the substitution D → g²D_d is made, and a good approximation (accuracy about 15%) for the long times, if the substitution D → gD_d is made" (p. 731). Data reanalysed: Au GB diffusion in Cu measured in the C-regime (ref. 6), g* ≈ 1.5×10⁻⁴ at 450 K (p. 732); Co* GB diffusion in W (ref. 22) argued to be a B→D3 transition with inferred dislocation density 2.4×10⁷ cm⁻² (p. 732); W self-diffusion zone width 2–4 µm (ref. 25) "can only be explained by the dislocation pipe diffusion" (p. 732).

**Authors' stated limitations.** "This approach is justified if the average diffusion distance in the bulk, (Dt)^{1/2} (t is the annealing time) is comparable, or exceeds the average distance between the individual elements of the substructure in the bulk" (p. 727). On m: "Though it can be estimated for some simple geometries, in what follows it will be considered as a phenomenological constant. The introduction of this new parameter is the price we need to pay for the refusal to consider the details of the defect substructure in the bulk" (p. 729). The subgrain calculation of m assumes a constant-source subboundary: "Though this condition is not fulfilled for the GB diffusion model considered here, it is somewhat similar to the approximation of Wuttig and Birnbaum [19]" (p. 728). "The GB diffusion experiment should be conducted under the conditions at which the microstructure is stable" (Fig. 1 caption, p. 726).

**Relation to the manuscript's model.** Category (a) with constant D's and analytical (Laplace) solutions; its key idea — that an effective leakage diffusivity is a volume-fraction-weighted property of a two-component medium (g²D_d → gD_d) — is the same kind of mixture rule the manuscript uses for D_eff over oxide phases, but here the mixture is static and lies in the bulk rather than in the GB channel, and nothing is consumed by reaction.

## 3. gao1999cracklike

**Citation (as printed).** H. Gao, L. Zhang, W. D. Nix, C. V. Thompson and E. Arzt, "Crack-like Grain-Boundary Diffusion Wedges in Thin Metal Films," *Acta mater.* **47**(10), 2865–2878 (1999). Received 16 February 1999; accepted in revised form 22 May 1999.

**Model type and governing equations.** Coupled elasticity / grain-boundary diffusion problem (no oxidation): stress-driven transport of matter from the free surface into a GB in a film bonded to a substrate (no sliding, no interface diffusion), modelled as insertion of a continuous array of climb-edge dislocations. Eq. (1): Eshelby traction kernel S(z,ζ) for an edge dislocation near a free surface; Eq. (2): GB traction σ_gb(z,t) = σ₀ − (E*/2π)∫S ∂u/∂ζ dζ; Eq. (3): chemical potential μ = −σ_gb Ω; Eq. (4): GB flux j = (δ_gb D_gb/kT) ∂σ_gb/∂z; Eq. (5): mass conservation 2 ∂u/∂t = −Ω ∂j/∂z; Eq. (6): ∂u/∂t = (δ_gb D_gb Ω/2kT) ∂²σ_gb/∂z²; Eq. (7): integro-differential equation for σ_gb; Eqs. (8)–(10): boundary/initial conditions (σ_gb(0,t)=0; no sliding/diffusion at the substrate; σ_gb(z,0)=σ₀); Eq. (11): eigen-expansion σ_gb = σ₀ Σ c_n e^{−λ_n t/t₀} f_n(z/h); Eq. (12): characteristic time t₀ = 4πkT h³/(E* D_gb δ_gb Ω); Eqs. (13)–(18): eigenvalue problem converted to a Cauchy singular integral equation; Eq. (19): coefficients; Eq. (20): opening displacement; Eq. (21): Irwin K(t); Eq. (22): periodic kernel P(ξ,ζ) for a GB array with aspect ratio a = h/L; Eq. (23): Rice dislocation-emission condition σ₀√(πh) ≥ 4√(μγ_us)... Appendix A: Gauss–Chebyshev numerical schemes (A1)–(A23); Appendix B: closed-form one-mode approximation (B1)–(B9), λ₁ ≈ 13.36 (within 15% of exact 11.74), λ₁(a) fit Eq. (B9).

**Transport — constant diffusivity?** Yes: "δ_gb D_gb is the product of the grain-boundary thickness and the atomic diffusivity in the boundary, Ω is the atomic volume and kT has the usual meaning" (p. 2868); the whole kinetics collapses into the single constant t₀ of Eq. (12). No dependence on oxide, composition or porosity. Oxide appears only qualitatively as a passivation layer that shuts off the process: "For Al films [5, 6], the presence of a strong, adherent native oxide serves as a passivation layer which effectively inhibits diffusional flow between the grain boundaries in the film and the surface" (pp. 2865–2866); "In the present paper we limit our attention to diffusional deformation in unpassivated metal films" (p. 2866).

**Alloy/GB composition as input? Evolving oxide composition?** No. Pure metals (Cu, Ag, Au, Al, Pt discussed); no composition variable, no oxide chemistry.

**Irradiation / RIS.** Not mentioned.

**Prediction and data.** Predicts that the GB normal traction decays exponentially in time, dominated by the first eigenmode, with "Complete relaxation of the grain-boundary traction occurs on a time-scale of order t₀/λ₁" (p. 2869); the wedge opening evolves to a mode-I-crack-like shape with a stress intensity factor approaching that of a traction-free edge crack (Fig. 7, p. 2871); second eigenmode decays 25× faster, third >100× (p. 2869); periodic array relaxes about twice as fast (p. 2870). Comparison with experiment is indirect: "it has been shown [23] that the results described in this paper are qualitatively consistent with the film thickness and temperature dependence of the flow stress of uncapped Ag films, for reasonable values of grain-boundary diffusivity" (p. 2872); qualitative stress–temperature behaviour of unpassivated Cu films [3] (p. 2874). Critical film thickness for dislocation emission from a wedge: 0.1 µm at 0 K and ~20 nm at room temperature for Cu at 200 MPa (pp. 2874–2875).

**Authors' stated limitations.** "Surface diffusion and grain-boundary grooving are neglected in the present analysis" (abstract, p. 2865). "We neglect possible anisotropic elastic properties and possible mismatch between the elastic constants of the film and those of the substrate" (p. 2867). "There may be complex interactions between the grain-boundary diffusion process studied in this paper and other processes such as surface diffusion, grain-boundary grooving and dislocation plasticity. These interactions will be investigated in future work" (p. 2872). In Appendix B: "In this description, the contributions from higher eigenmodes and grain-boundary interactions are neglected" (p. 2877).

**Relation to the manuscript's model.** Not an oxidation model; it belongs to category (a) (constant δ_gb D_gb, closed-form approximate solution), with a *stress-gradient* rather than concentration-gradient driving force. Its only relevance is as a mechanics analogue: a GB "wedge" that produces crack-like stress concentration — a plausible companion picture for how an intergranular oxide of the depth predicted by the manuscript could act as an IGSCC precursor, but it offers no treatment of oxide phase, composition, or alloy chemistry.

## 4. jaseliunaite2020kinetic

**Citation (as printed).** Justina Jaseliunaite and Arvaidas Galdikas, "Kinetic Modeling of Grain Boundary Diffusion: The Influence of Grain Size and Surface Processes," *Materials* **2020**, 13, 1051; doi:10.3390/ma13051051. Kaunas University of Technology. Received 15 January 2020; accepted 24 February 2020; published 26 February 2020.

**Model type and governing equations.** Two-dimensional explicit finite-difference rate-equation model of diffusion into a polycrystal with square grains of varying size (30–138 a.u.) and fixed GB width (6 a.u.), with Langmuir adsorption/desorption at the surface; boundary conditions "correspond with the real situation in the electrolytes of solid oxide hydrogen fuel cells (SOFC)" (abstract, p. 1). Eq. (1): 2D Fick's second law with D_x, D_y; Eq. (2): explicit discretisation with D = D_gb in GBs and D = D_g in grains; Eq. (3): Langmuir dc/dt = αp(c* − c) − βc; Eq. (4): first-layer equation combining adsorption/desorption with diffusion; Eqs. (5)–(6): contour levels; Eq. (7): percentage difference Δc_g between polycrystal and monocrystal; Eq. (8): fitted law for average concentration vs. grain/GB volume ratio, c = a·erfc(−b/(V_g/V_gb)), with a(t) = q·t^{1/2} (Fig. 9a, Table 3).

**Transport — constant diffusivity?** Yes, piecewise-constant. "For a two-dimensional case when the diffusion coefficient is constant, Fick's second law is written as follows" (p. 3). "The same equation is used for both grain and GB diffusion, but the difference is in the value of diffusion coefficient D: we use D = D_gb for diffusion in GB and D = D_g for diffusion in grains. Also, the same equation is used for atom transfer from grains to grain boundaries and vice versa: when atoms diffuse from GB to grain, then D = D_g; when from grain to GB, D = D_gb" (p. 3). Values: Table 2, D_gb/D_g = 10³–10⁴ (p. 6). Describing Fisher: "The diffusion coefficient remains constant along the GB (is isotropic) and is independent of concentration" (p. 1). No oxide, no phase/composition dependence, no porosity. The one departure from classical treatments: "In this work a change of concentration within GB during the diffusion process is allowed" (p. 4).

**Alloy/GB composition as input? Evolving oxide composition?** No. Single diffusing species, inert host; no oxide forms; concentrations in arbitrary units.

**Irradiation / RIS.** Not mentioned.

**Prediction and data.** Concentration contours for five grain sizes and three D cases (Fig. 3); regime identification: "The concentration contours in Case 1 correspond to the Harrison A regime [7] ... Case 2 corresponds to the Harrison B regime" (p. 6). Key finding: "It follows that it is not the D_gb/D_g ratio that determines distribution of concentration, but the absolute values of the diffusion coefficients D_gb and D_g" (p. 9). Surface boundary condition matters: "When adsorption/desorption is included, those curves differ even when they are calculated in a very deep layer (i = 140)" (p. 9); with adsorption/desorption "atoms diffuse from grains to GB, because the concentration in grains becomes higher than in GB. That never occurs when adsorption/desorption is not included" (p. 9). Average concentration follows erfc in V_g/V_gb, "accuracy > 99%" (p. 11); a(t) = q√t (p. 12). No comparison to experimental data within the paper; parameters α = β = 0.5 "are quite realistic for a wide class of materials, and are similar to values we previously used for fitting experimental curves [35]" (p. 6).

**Authors' stated limitations.** "All the calculated results presented below are outputted at a certain arbitrary time" (p. 4). On Eq. (8): "where a and b are fitting parameters whose physical meaning needs to be found" (p. 11). "The dependence on time of coefficient b is not clear ... No general function for fitting can be proposed, and the lines in Figure 9b are just point connections" (p. 12). The analytical form is justified only heuristically: "Such a formula can be assumed to be the analytical solution of the presented model equations, Equations (2) and (4), which, because of the difficult boundary conditions, cannot be solved by simple integration methods" (p. 11).

**Relation to the manuscript's model.** Numerically the closest kin (explicit finite differences, GB as a distinct region with its own D, non-constant-source surface BC), but it is category (a)-numerical: D's are constants, nothing reacts or changes phase, and the only "law" is an empirical erfc fit. Its demonstration that the inlet (surface) boundary condition changes profiles even deep in the solid is directly relevant to how the manuscript specifies oxygen supply at the GB mouth.

## 5. harrison1984ratios

**Citation (as printed).** P. L. Harrison, "The Ratios of Layer Thicknesses in a Multiphase Oxide Scale," *Oxidation of Metals* **22**(1/2), 35–43 (1984). CEGB Central Electricity Research Laboratories, Leatherhead. Received December 16, 1983.

**Model type and governing equations.** Analytical analysis of diffusion-controlled growth of two- and three-layer oxide scales by outward cation diffusion, each layer with its own rate constant. Two-layer: Eq. (1): volume conversion factor V₂₁ at the oxide1/oxide2 boundary; Eq. (2): fluxes J = K₁/X₁, (1−α)J = K₂/X₂; Eq. (3)–(4): dX₁/dt, dX₂/dt; Eq. (5): d(X₁/X₂)/dt; stability analysis of three conditions; Eq. (6)–(8): stable ratio R = X₁/X₂ (positive root of a quadratic). Three-layer (spinel / magnetite / hematite on 9Cr steel in steam): Eqs. (9)–(11): fluxes and growth rates with factor r (ratio of outward metal flux through oxide 1 to in-situ conversion at the metal interface) and negligible resistance of oxide 2; Eq. (12)–(13): stable X₂/X₃; Eq. (14): K₃ = ±D₃(C₃s − C₃₂) with defect concentration C₃ ∝ a p_O2^{−3/4} (triply charged Fe interstitials in Fe₂O₃); parameter k = D₃a/K₁; Table I: % Fe₂O₃ in the outer oxide vs. p_O2 and k at 500 °C.

**Transport — constant diffusivity?** Yes, per layer: "The flux J_i through an oxide layer of thickness X_i is given by J_i = K_i/X_i where K_i is a constant deduced from the diffusion and the boundary values of the defect concentrations" (p. 37). "It is assumed that equilibrium conditions exist at all the interfaces and that diffusional processes are rate controlling" (p. 36). **Transport does depend on which oxide phase is present** — each phase has its own K, and the hematite K₃ depends on p_O2 through defect chemistry (Eq. 14) — but the sequence of phases is prescribed, not predicted. "It is further assumed that the fluxes of metal through oxides 1 and 3 are determined by the respective diffusion coefficients and the boundary values of the defect concentrations and that oxide 2 offers negligible restriction to the metal ion flux through it as compared to that offered by oxide 1" (p. 39). No porosity.

**Alloy/GB composition as input? Evolving oxide composition?** Alloy composition enters only implicitly through the identity of oxide 1 ("mixed chromium-iron spinel", p. 39) and the conversion factor r (= 1.3, p. 41). What evolves is the *layer-thickness ratio* (hence hematite fraction), not the internal composition of any layer: "It is proved that the relative thicknesses of the oxide layers must tend to a limiting constant value for all oxidizing conditions" (abstract, p. 35).

**Irradiation / RIS.** Not mentioned.

**Prediction and data.** Analytical proof that condition (ii) is the only long-term-stable regime and gives a stable ratio R (pp. 37–38); "Equation (8) is, of course, identical to the many versions that have appeared in the literature, but which have been derived by assuming that the ratio is constant" (p. 38). For 9Cr steel in steam at 500 °C: predicted 3% hematite at p_O2 = 10⁻⁸ bar with mean k = 7×10⁻¹⁶, rising to 30% for a steel oxidizing at the 95% lower-confidence rate (p. 42); "the proportion of hematite in the scale at any oxygen partial pressure could range over two orders of magnitude, i.e., from nondetectable amounts to 100%" (p. 41); "the partial pressure of oxygen in the steam has a negligible effect unless it is substantially lower than the equilibrium value for the dissociation of pure steam" (p. 42). Compared qualitatively with plant observations of variable hematite content (ref. 11). Overall kinetics are parabolic in each layer.

**Authors' stated limitations.** "It should be noted that this model would not be applicable to the usual three layer oxide formation, such as the formation of FeO/Fe₃O₄/Fe₂O₃ on iron in air" (p. 39). **Citation (as printed).** D. P. Whittle, B. D. Bastow and G. C. Eqs. (1)–(2): Wagner's Eqs. (11) and (19) — flux equations in the scale in terms of self-diffusivities D_A, D_B, valencies Z_A, Z_B, Z_X, activities, local equivalent fraction ξ(y) of B(X), dimensionless position y = x/x_s and rate constant k = x_s dx_s/dt. **Irradiation / RIS.** Not mentioned.

Applicability requires γ > 3 (F(γ) > 0.95) (p. 220), i.e., negligible alloy diffusion relative to scale growth. Full verification deferred: "Solutions of Eqs. (1) and (2) are now being generated to provide a true experimental verification of the analysis" (p. 223).

**Relation to the manuscript's model.** The clearest classical precedent for category (c) — alloy composition in, oxide composition out, with composition-dependent cation diffusivities — but for a continuous solid-solution scale growing outward by cation diffusion under parabolic control; it has no GB channel, no multi-phase mixture (Cr₂O₃/spinel/SiO₂), no inward oxygen transport and no interfacial reaction limitation, all of which the manuscript adds.

**Irradiation / RIS.** Not treated. While oxide measurements from a single data point in time cannot be used to establish meaningful growth kinetics, they provide useful insights regarding the growth mechanism" (p. 175). The only equation, Eq. (1), is the metal/film interfacial reaction m → M_M + (χ/2) V_Ö + χe' (p. 180), used to argue that interfacial coherency controls rate.

**Transport — constant diffusivity? Phase dependence?** The authors argue that transport is *not* rate-limiting; interfacial reaction is: "The potential for the oxide thickness to vary by an order of magnitude depending on the orientation of the underlying metal grain, evident in Fig. 4, suggests that under the test conditions in the present study the limiting step is likely to be associated with reactions at the metal/film interface ... the variation in oxide thickness observed across grains cannot be accounted for by reactions at the film/solution interface, nor by diffusion/migration rates of ion vacancies through the oxide" (p. 179). "Since oxide growth is more dependent on the interfacial reaction rates, as previously concluded, the increased point defect densities and higher diffusion rates associated with grain boundaries do not offer a significant advantage" (p. 181). Oxide phases: "a nanocrystalline Cr-rich fcc spinel of type FeCr₂O₄ albeit with some variation in measured stoichiometry, and traces of Ni also reported [5–10]. While the outer layer is composed of larger magnetite Fe₃O₄ crystallites" (p. 175). A phase-dependent rationale (Robertson's) is considered but judged insufficient: "if the decrease in oxide growth with increased temperature could be accounted for by transition in stable oxide structures, it is not clear how this can be reconciled with the observations in the present study of growth dependence on orientation" (p. 179). Proposed mechanism: epitaxial coherency between austenite and spinel degrades with temperature because of thermal-expansion mismatch (16.2 vs 9.9 µm m⁻¹ °C⁻¹), reducing interfacial transfer (p. 180).

**Alloy/GB composition as input? Evolving oxide composition?** No model; alloy composition given (Table 1). CSL boundaries excluded from measurement as "unfavourable for intergranular oxidation" (p. 176). No GB composition measurement; no oxide-composition evolution beyond the inner Cr-rich/outer Fe-rich description (Fig. 1b EELS maps).

**Irradiation / RIS.** Not studied; the introduction cites Fukuya et al. on "surface oxides formed on irradiated stainless steels in simulated PWR primary water" [7] only as a source for inner-layer characterisation (p. 175).

**Prediction and data.** Measured maximum intergranular penetration after 1000 h (Fig. 2, p. 177): up to 1464 and 1378 nm at 290 °C vs roughly 160–870 nm at 320 and 360 °C — "the maximum value for the 290 °C test was at least twice the maximum of the other temperatures" (p. 177); surface inner-oxide maxima up to 899 nm (Fig. 3). "the depth of intergranular oxidation decreases with increased temperature within the range of 290 °C–360 °C" (p. 178), "contrary to the established oxidation models which either base their diffusion-migration relations on Fick's law or have Arrhenius type expressions to describe the interfacial reaction rates, both of which would imply an increased oxidation rate with temperature" (p. 178); consistent with Warzee and Maekawa rate peaks at 250–300 °C. Orientation: ⟨001⟩ ∥ surface normal favoured at 320 °C, disfavoured at 290 °C, no preference at 360 °C (pp. 177–178). Cold work: "the introduction of cold work by cold rolling to 20% reduction in thickness did not significantly increase the surface or intergranular Cr-rich inner oxidation penetration compared to the annealed samples" (p. 181). "Overall the oxidation did not penetrate down the grain boundaries or deformation bands to the same degree as observed in some nickel based alloys" (p. 181).

**Authors' stated limitations.** "These techniques are too time consuming to gather sufficient data to be statistically representative; however, the intention was nevertheless to measure the deepest oxide penetration in each sample" (p. 176). "The intergranular oxidation penetration values for the annealed samples were in some cases artificially high due to the contribution of surface oxidation rather than preferential grain boundary oxidation" (p. 177). "Existing oxide growth models require adaption in order to accurately predict this inverse in growth kinetics with temperature behaviour of 316L stainless steels exposed to typical primary water conditions" (p. 182).

**Relation to the manuscript's model.** Supplies the PWR-relevant observable the manuscript predicts — intergranular Cr-rich spinel penetration of a few hundred nm to ~1.5 µm in 1000 h at 290–360 °C — and argues that an interfacial (metal/oxide) reaction, not GB or oxide diffusion, is rate-limiting, which supports the manuscript's inclusion of composition-dependent interfacial reaction rates; it also warns that any Fickian + Arrhenius formulation will predict the wrong sign of the temperature dependence between 290 and 360 °C unless an additional mechanism is included.

## Cross-cutting conclusions for the manuscript

1. **Constant-D analytical penetration laws (a)** are provided by Fisher 1951 (exponential-in-depth, t^{1/4} penetration), Klinger & Rabkin 1999 (same law with effective leakage D → g²D_d or gD_d, plus a time-plateau regime), Gao et al. 1999 (exponential traction decay with constant δ_gb D_gb), Harrison 1984 (parabolic per layer with constant K_i) and Killeen et al. 1976 (analytical C_s(t), asymptotically parabolic). None couples the diffusivity to an evolving oxide composition.

2. **Transport depending on the oxide formed (b)** appears in three forms: per-layer constants with a prescribed phase sequence (Harrison 1984); Wagner-type composition- and a_X-dependent cation diffusivities in a single-phase solid-solution scale (Whittle, Bastow & Wood 1975); and a mechanistic argument that the rate-controlling k_p is set by which phase (Fe₃O₄ / Fe,Cr spinel / Cr₂O₃) forms, with pH-dependent defect concentrations and nm-scale pores for water ingress (Robertson 1991). Taylor & Husband 2023 add experimental evidence that intergranular SiO₂ changes local transport. No paper makes an inward-oxygen D_eff along a GB a continuous function of a mixed Cr₂O₃/Fe₃O₄/FeCr₂O₄/SiO₂ composition with SiO₂-dissolution porosity, as the manuscript does.

3. **Alloy composition in / evolving oxide composition out (c)** is achieved only partially: Whittle et al. 1967, Killeen et al. 1976 and Taylor & Husband 2023 take alloy composition as input but fix the oxide (single phase); Robertson 1991 predicts inner/outer oxide composition but statically (congruent partition, no depletion); Whittle, Bastow & Wood 1975 is the only one that yields an oxide composition from alloy composition through transport properties, and only for a single-phase scale under parabolic cation control.

4. **Irradiation / RIS** is absent from all eleven papers (Killeen et al. use neutron activation purely as a tracer; Robertson mentions radiation fields only in the context of activity transport; Matthews et al. cite an irradiated-steel oxide study for phase identification only). The manuscript's use of RIS-modified GB concentrations as the compositional input to GB oxidation has no precedent in this set.

5. **Observable and validation.** Matthews et al. 2017 give the directly comparable PWR intergranular penetration depths (hundreds of nm to ~1.5 µm at 1000 h, 290–360 °C) and a non-Arrhenius temperature trend attributed to interfacial-reaction control; Robertson 1991 gives the uniform-corrosion k_p context and the spinel-vs-chromia rate hierarchy; Taylor & Husband 2023 give the SiO₂-along-GB morphology (at high temperature in air).
