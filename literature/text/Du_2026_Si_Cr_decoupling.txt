ABSTRACT
Grain boundary (GB) segregation plays a critical role in irradiation-assisted stress corrosion cracking (IASCC) by 
influencing both intergranular oxidation and GB cohesive strength. To decouple the effects of Si enrichment and 
Cr depletion on crack enhancement, four model alloys with systematically varied Si and Cr concentrations were 
designed, and slow strain rate testing (SSRT) was performed on these alloys. The results demonstrate that the 
cracking susceptibility increases significantly with increasing Si content. In contrast, the low-Cr alloy exhibited 
only a marginal increase in cracking susceptibility compared to the alloy containing 0.85 wt% Si. These findings 
indicate that Si segregation at GBs is a more important factor promoting IASCC.
1. Introduction
Irradiation-assisted stress corrosion cracking (IASCC) in materials, 
which occurs early in the life of PWR core components (1–3 dpa), has 
become a major degradation mechanism of light water reactors (LWRs) 
[1,2]. IASCC is predominantly intergranular, making it somewhat more 
difficult to detect via non-destructive testing (NDT) methods and often 
leads to unexpected consequences. To date, failures have been reported 
in over 20 core components-manufactured from stainless steels and 
nickel-base alloys, including baffles, former bolts, fuel rods, control 
blades, instrumentation tubes, and springs-in both pressurized water 
reactors (PWRs) and boiling water reactors (BWRs) [3,4]. These in-
cidents have significantly impaired economic efficiency and posed a 
substantial safety risks to nuclear reactor power plants.
Neutron irradiation is responsible for the premature or enhanced 
cracking of reactor core components, as it concurrently induces micro-
structural and microchemical changes in materials, along with water 
radiolysis. Microstructure alterations, such as the formation of black 
dots, dislocation loops, voids, and precipitates, lead to hardening and 
increased strength, which have been correlated with IASCC suscepti-
bility [5–7]. Microchemical changes typically involve elemental segre-
gation, occurring both at grain boundaries (GBs) and within grains (e.g., 
in clusters and precipitates). In austenitic stainless steels, segregation of 
both minor and major elements has been observed at GBs, including 
enrichment of Ni, Mn, Si, and P, and depletion of Fe, Cr, and Mo [8–11]. 
Extensive research on the microstructural and microchemical evolution 
of irradiated materials, as well as their effects on stress corrosion 
cracking (SCC) behavior, has yielded significant progress in under-
standing IASCC mechanisms. Grain boundary oxidation is widely 
recognized as a precursor to IASCC crack initiation [12,13], as it 
weakens GBs and reduces fracture resistance [14–17]. Applied stress 
accelerates the oxidation process and promotes localized deformation in 
grains with favorable orientations, further enhancing oxidation at the 
intersection of dislocation channels (DCs) and GBs [14–17]. When DCs 
terminate at GBs, they generate high local stress concentrations, suffi-
cient to fracture the oxidized GBs, thereby initiating cracks [3,18–20].
Given the critical roles of GB oxidation and fracture in IASCC, 
considerable attention has been directed toward understanding 
elemental segregation at GBs, particularly Si enrichment and Cr deple-
tion. Studies on sensitized stainless steels have demonstrated that GB Cr 
depletion can lead to severe SCC in oxygenated environment [21,22] as 
insufficient Cr prevents the formation of a protective Cr-rich oxide film. 
In contrast, the GB carbides formed during sensitization can inhibit SCC 
cracking in hydrogenated high-temperature water [23]. Given that the 
Cr depletion feature at GB induced by irradiation differs from that of 
thermal sensitization (which involves carbide precipitation), its effect

distinct. Post-irradiation annealing which desegregates Cr and Si from 
GBs has been correlated with reduced IASCC susceptibility [24–26]. SCC 
tests on high-Si model alloys demonstrated that both crack initiation 
[27,28] and crack propagation rates [29–32] increase with Si concen-

have been shown to exert limited influence on the electrochemical 
behavior of materials in high-temperature water [33–35], irradiated 
materials exhibit deeper GB oxidation compared to their unirradiated 
counterparts [13,36], a phenomenon critical to IASCC.
However, it remains unclear whether the preferential GB oxidation is 
driven primarily by Si or Cr, as their segregation occurs simultaneously. 
Previous studies employed model alloys with either low Cr/high Si or 
variable Si/normal Cr, failing to effectively decouple the two effects. 
Therefore, systematic experiments on two sets of model alloys are 
necessary: one with a Cr content comparable to that of 304 stainless steel 
and varying Si levels, and another with low Cr and normal Si. Such 
studies would fill the knowledge gap and contribute to a more 
comprehensive understanding of IASCC mechanisms.
2. Experimental
2.1. Materials and specimen preparation
To isolate the effects of Cr depletion and Si enrichment on IASCC, 
four model alloys with systematically varied Si and Cr content were 
designed, based on the GB chemical composition of commercial nuclear- 
grade 304 stainless steel after neutron irradiation. The Ni concentration 
was fixed at 25 wt% in all alloys to simulate the Ni enrichment typically 
observed under high dpa damage conditions. For alloys with varying Si 
concentration (1, 2.5, 4.0 wt%), the Cr concentration was maintained at 
19 wt%. The fourth alloy, with reduced Cr content (9 wt%), was 
designed to have a Si level of 0.33 % comparable to that of standard 
nuclear-grade 304 stainless steel. The Mn concentration was consis-
tently maintained at 2.2 wt% across all alloys.
Columnar ingots were prepared using a vacuum induction levitation 
melting furnace. These ingots were then forged at 1150℃ into cuboids 
with a thickness of approximately 50 mm, followed by quenching in ice 
water. To verify the target compositions, chemical analyses were con-
ducted using a wavelength-dispersive X-ray fluorescence spectrometer, 
with results summarized in Table 1. Grain size characteristics were 
evaluated via electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD), and data were 
processed using AZtecCrystal software. The resulting inverse pole figure 
(IPF) maps and grain size distributions are presented in Fig. 1. All alloys 
exhibited comparable average grain sizes, ranging from 44 to 71 μm.
Each alloy block was sectioned midway through its thickness. One 
half underwent an additional one-step forging at 200 ◦C, achieving a 
20 % reduction in thickness. This deformation treatment was applied to 
enhance mechanical strength and increase susceptibility to SCC, thereby 
facilitating crack initiation within a practical experimental timeframe. 
Tensile specimens were fabricated from the forged plates using electrical 
discharge machining (EDM), with the specimen geometry illustrated in 
Fig. 2. These tensile specimens featured a gage length of 16 mm and a 
rectangular gage cross-section of 2.2× 1.6 mm2. To ensure consistency, 
all specimens used for slow strain rate testing (SSRT) were extracted 
from equivalent positions within the deformed plates across different 
alloys. Both the front and back surfaces of each tensile specimen were 
mechanically polished up to 4000-grit SiC paper to achieve a uniform 
surface finish and eliminate surface-related artifacts. The side surfaces of 
the gauge sections were also carefully polished to remove any scratch- 
like defects that could act as stress concentrators.

2.2. Grain boundary composition analysis
To investigate whether Si or Cr naturally segregates to GBs, trans-
mission electron microscopy (TEM) specimens were prepared from each 
alloy. Small sections were extracted from the bulk material and me-
chanically polished to a thickness of approximately 100 ~200 μm. Discs 
with a diameter of 3 mm were then punched from the thinned foils and 
subjected to double-jet electropolishing using a Struers TenuPol-5 sys-
tem. The electrolyte consisted of 5 vol% perchloric acid in methanol, 
maintained at a temperature below - 20 ◦C by cooling the solution with 
liquid nitrogen. Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) 
coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (STEM-EDS) map-
ping was performed using a Talos F200X G2 microscope operated at 
200 kV, with a probe size of approximately 1 nm.
2.3. Slow strain rate tensile test
SSRT tests were conducted in a 316 L stainless steel autoclave con-
nected to a freshly refreshed water loop capable of providing simulated 
PWR water conditions (320℃, 2.7 ppm H2). After achieving environ-
mental equilibrium, four specimens (one from each alloy) were strained 
simultaneously at a constant strain rate of 5× 10 8 s 1. Each specimen 
was actuated by an independent servo motor, ensuring that the stress 
and strain conditions of individual samples were not affected by others. 
The load on each specimen and the displacement were continuously 
recorded via the test control program. Two fiducial marks were inscri-
bed outside the gauge section of each specimen to enable accurate 
measurement of the actual plastic strain post-test. To account for system 
compliance and ensure consistent crack initiation across all specimens, a 
nominal total strain of more than 7 % was applied before stopping the 
tests. This strain level was determined through preliminary tests, which 
confirmed it reliably induced crack initiation in all specimens under the 
specified environmental conditions.
2.4. Post-strain characterization
Stress–strain curves were generated from the recorded load and 
displacement data, from which the yield stress was derived. The actual 
plastic strain for each specimen was calculated based on the change in 
distance between the two fluidical marks before and after testing, with 
measurements obtained by capturing SEM images of the marks. Post-test 
characterization included plane-view and cross-sectional imaging of the 
deformed specimens using a GeminiSEM 460. Crack initiation behavior 
was quantified by counting the number of cracks and measuring their 
lengths and depths. From these measurements, crack number density, 
length density, and depth distribution were determined. Additionally, 
the morphology and composition of the oxide films were examined using 
SEM and EDS.
To analyze GB oxidation at crack tips, site-specific TEM specimens 
were prepared using a dual-beam focused ion beam (FIB)-SEM system 
(FEI). For deeply embedded cracks inaccessible via conventional surface 
FIB milling, a plane lift-out technique was employed on pre-exposed 
cross-sectional surfaces. The FIB lift-outs were sequentially thinned 
using 30 kV voltage with currents of 5 nA and 1 nA, followed by final 
thinning at 5 kV (60 pA), and a cleaning step at 2 kV. The resulting TEM 
lamellae were characterized via STEM and EDS using an FEI Talos 
F200X G2 microscope operating at 200 kV and equipped with a Super-X 
EDS detector. Quantitative EDS analysis was performed using Velox 
software.

3. Results
3.1. Elemental composition of the grain boundary
Fig. 3 presents compositional profiles of the main elements across a 
random high-angle GB in the as-prepared model alloys. For alloys H1, 
H2, H3, and H4, no segregation of Si or Ni was detected at the GBs, with 
their concentrations remaining consistent with those in adjacent grain 
interiors, indicating the absence of natural segregation in these alloys. 
Similarly, no Cr depletion or precipitate formation was observed at the 
GBs in the first three alloys. In contrast, nanoscale Cr23C6 carbides were 
identified along GBs in alloy H4, with corresponding Cr depletion zones 
evident in the regions between these precipitates. However, EDS line 
scans across precipitate-free GB segments in alloy H4 confirmed the 
absence of Cr depletion, as clearly illustrated in Fig. 3. Although nano-
scale Cr23C6 carbides were observed along some GBs in alloy H4, their 
size was limited, and their distribution was sparse and discontinuous.
3.2. Stress-strain curves
The stress-strain curves for all tested samples are shown in Fig. 4. An 
initial stress of approximately 150 MPa is observed at the start of each 
curve, arising from the autoclave pressure acting on the tensile speci-
mens. Key mechanical properties, including yield strength, stress at the 
target strain (7 %), measured actual plastic strain, and corrosion dura-
tion, are summarized in Table 2. The actual plastic strains measured 
after SSRT were 6.92 %, 7.06 %, 7.14 %, and 7.16 % for the four alloys, 
respectively, all within 5 % of the target strain value. Yield strength 
decreased progressively with increasing alloy number (from H1 to H4), 
with a maximum difference of only 35 MPa between alloys H1 and H4. 
The final engineering stresses at the target strain of 7 % were 356 MPa, 
367 MPa, 346 MPa, and 315 MPa, respectively, with a maximum dif-
ference of 42 MPa between alloys H2 and H4. The relatively small dif-
ferences in strengths among the alloys provide a well-controlled 
mechanical basis for evaluating and comparing their SCC susceptibility.
3.3. Oxidation and crack initiation behavior
3.3.1. Plan-view
Fig. 5 presents an overview of SEM micrographs of the gauge surfaces 
after SSRT, composed of stitched low-magnification images. The 
absence of visible necking confirms uniform elongation of all specimens. 
Surface-initiated cracks are marked with white arrows; although some 
finer cracks are not clearly resolved at this magnification, the overall 
distribution and quantity of cracks remain distinguishable. Alloy H3 
exhibited the highest number of cracks, followed by alloy H2, while 
alloys H1 and H4 showed comparatively fewer cracks. To quantitatively 
assess SCC susceptibility, the total number of cracks in the entire gauge 
section was counted, and the length and width of each crack were 
measured. From these data, crack number density and crack length 
density (total crack length per unit area) were calculated. Additionally, 
the maximum crack width and length for each alloy were determined.

All quantitative metrics are summarized in Table 3. Except for average 
crack length, all parameters-including maximum crack width, maximum 
crack length, crack number density, and crack length density-increase in 
the order: H1< H4< H2< H3. This trend indicates a corresponding 
increase in SCC susceptibility across the four alloys. The initiation site of 
stress corrosion cracking (SCC) is strongly influenced by factors such as 
the angle between the tensile axis and the grain trace, the Luster-Morris 
m′ parameter, and grain boundary misorientation [37]. Consequently, in 
a polycrystalline sample with a sufficient number of grains, the number 
of initiated intergranular cracks is thus determined by the material’s 
inherent SCC resistance.
Fig. 6 shows higher-magnification SEM images of the gauge surfaces, 
revealing detailed crack initiation sites and oxide morphology. Clear 
differences in cracking behavior and oxidation characteristics are 
observed among the alloys. The surface of alloy H1 remains relatively 
smooth and intact, with minimal oxide spallation. In contrast, alloys H2 
and H3 exhibit rough, irregular surfaces with extensive peeling and 
flaking of oxide films, with alloy H3 showing more severe and wide-
spread exfoliation compared to the localized spallation observed in H2. 
Alloy H4 displays a moderately affected surface, featuring isolated re-
gions of high-density oxide particles and minor exfoliation. Widespread 
oxide exfoliation suggests poor adhesion of the oxide film or high sus-
ceptibility to fracture under tensile stress, likely due to high defect 
density or limited oxide ductility.
To further investigate this behavior, SEM-EDS analysis was con-
ducted on alloy H3, which exhibited the most severe exfoliation (Fig. 7). 
The image includes both adherent and spalled oxide regions: the area 
within the orange outline represents intact oxide, which is depleted in Cr 
and Si; in contrast, the exfoliated region is enriched in Cr and Si, indi-
cating a distinct compositional difference between the two oxide states. 
The TEM-EDS analysis on the high-Si alloy performed by Han et al. [38]
have demonstrated that the inner oxide film is rich in Si and Cr, which is 
a good agreement with our results. Sparse oxide particles are visible in 
the exfoliated zone, suggesting renewed corrosion occurred after spall-
ation. From this comparison, it can be deduced that the spalled oxide 
experienced depletion of Si and Cr before detachment, highlighting their 
critical role in oxide film fracture and detachment. This cyclic process of 
oxidation-exfoliation-reoxidation likely repeats throughout the corro-
sion and straining process.
In summary, the dissolution of Si-rich oxides in the surface oxide film 
generates a high density of defects, which consequently weakens the 
film’s fracture resistance under sustained tensile stress. Given that the 
oxidation mechanisms and resulting oxide structures are similar be-
tween the specimen surface and the GB, the GB oxides are expected to 
undergo an analogous process during crack initiation.
3.3.2. Cross-sectional view
Fig. 8 presents cross-sectional views of the oxide films and inter-
granular oxidation/cracks in the four alloys after SSRT. Due to the low 
crack density and large spacing between cracks, representative images 
of individual intergranular attacks are provided for each alloy. The 
cross-sectional morphology of oxide films is consistent with plan-view 
observations. As shown in Fig. 8a, alloy H1 exhibits sparse oxide parti-
cles, while alloy H4 shows a high density of oxide particles on the 
outermost surface. Cross-sectional analysis reveals that exfoliation oc-
curs not directly at the metal-oxide interface, but within the inner oxide 
layer itself, resulting in oxide thinning. Alloys H1 and H4 exhibit only 
fine, vertically oriented cracks within the oxide. In contrast, alloys H2 
and H3 developed horizontally propagating cracks that led to localized 
spallation. Additionally, numerous voids are observed within the upper 
oxide film on alloy H4, likely arising from continuous strain and 
dissolution.

To evaluate the influence of Si and Cr on oxidation behavior, oxide 
thickness was statistically measured on polished side surfaces. Shen 
et al. [39] demonstrated that the composition and microstructure of 
surface oxides are similar to the SCC intergranular oxides; thus, the 
oxidation behavior of the surface can reflect intergranular oxidation. 
The measured thickness values and corresponding averages are sum-
marized in Fig. 9. The data follow a lognormal distribution and exhibit 
considerable scatter in alloys H2 and H3, likely due to oxide spallation, 
yet a clear trend emerges: average oxide thickness increases significantly 
with Si content. The thickness of the inner oxide layer generally reflects 
oxidation resistance and correlates well with SCC susceptibility in 
high-Si alloys; however, this trend does not hold for the low-Cr alloy 
(H4). Despite having a thicker average oxide layer than alloy H2, alloy 
H4 exhibits lower SCC susceptibility, indicating that uniform corrosion 
resistance alone does not determine cracking behavior. This discrepancy 
underscores the complexity of SCC mechanisms and suggests that dif-
ferences in oxide evolution, such as stability under prolonged stress and 
susceptibility to fracture, play a critical role in determining overall SCC 
performance.
Fig. 8b shows representative intergranular oxidation/cracks for each 
alloy. Some GBs oxidation depths of up to several microns with a sharp 
wedge shape, which is consistent with the characterization of neutron- 
irradiated stainless steels that experience more severe GB oxidation 
than the unirradiated counterparts, with GB oxidation depth increasing 
with dose [36]. Part of the intergranular oxide cracked at a position 
close to the surface. Based on the characteristics of intergranular 
oxidation and cracks, it can be inferred that the cracks originate from 
preferentially oxidized GBs. In other words, GB oxidation is a precursor 
to intergranular cracking. This result is also consistent well with our 
previous study on neutron-irradiated 304 and 316 stainless steels [12, 
13,40]. Our model alloys replicate the severe GB oxidation behavior of 
neutron-irradiated 304 and 316 stainless steels. Thus, the depth of 
intergranular oxidation/crack can reflect the IASCC susceptibility of 
alloys and was used to evaluate the cracking susceptibility of the model 
alloys.

3.4. STEM characterization of crack tips
Intergranular oxidation and crack tips in alloys H1 to H4 were 
examined using STEM, with results presented in Figs. 11–14. Each figure 
includes an overview of the FIB-prepared sampled region, followed by 
HAADF, BF, and EDS mapping images of the tip region (Figs. 11 -14a). A 
high density of dislocations, introduced by warm working or slow strain 
during testing, is evident surrounding the oxidation front in all alloys. 
Quantitative EDS line scans across the GB oxide and ahead of the tip are 
provided in Figs. 11–14(b, c).
For the high-Si alloys (H1, H2, H3), the oxidized GB tip consists of Cr- 
and Si-enriched oxide. The Si enrichment levels measured at the tip are 
1.6 wt%, 6.4 wt%, and 17.5 wt% for alloys H1, H2, and H3, respec-
tively, corresponding to 2.0, 2.5, and 4.4 times the matrix concentration. 
This indicates that Si enrichment at the tip escalates with increasing bulk 
Si content. Nickel enrichment is also observed ahead of the oxidized tip 
in these alloys. Additionally, GB migration, attributed to significant 
elemental diffusion [41,42], is notably present in H3. The length of the 
Ni-enriched zones is 58 nm, 239 nm, and 232 nm for alloys H1, H2, and 
H3, respectively. Although the Ni-enriched region is longer in alloy H2 
than in H3, the higher enrichment ratio and observed DIMG in H3 
suggest more extensive elemental outward diffusion. It should be noted 
that the anomalously high Si signal at one location in the EDS map of the 
H2 specimen is an artifact. A tungsten protective layer was deposited to 
preserve the GB oxide during FIB preparation of the TEM specimen. 
Therefore, the artifact is likely due to a peak overlap, specifically from 
the W-Kα line (1.775 keV), which has an energy very close to that of the 
Si-Kα line (1.740 keV)
The low-Cr alloy H4 exhibits distinct GB oxidation behavior 
compared to the high-Si alloys. No Si-rich oxide is formed along the GB. 
Nanoscale Cr-rich oxides are present in alloy H4, likely originating from 
pre-existing carbides. The Ni-enriched zone ahead of the oxide is narrow 
but exceeds 1.1 µm in length, with no accompanying GB migration. The 
apparent Si-rich region at the upper left corner of the Si map is also a 
mis-indexing of tungsten.
The carbides in this study are small and sparsely distributed. 
Although GB carbides have been reported to mitigate oxidation and slow 
SCC in hydrogenated water [43,44], their beneficial effect in this alloy is 
likely less pronounced than in alloys with intentionally introduced 
high-density carbides via thermal processing.
4. Discussion
Post-test analysis of surface oxide characteristics and crack 
morphology, combined with statistical analysis of crack density and 
depth, revealed distinct crack initiation behaviors among the four model 
alloys. As shown in Fig. 15, both crack length density and average 
intergranular crack depth increased across the alloy series. Crack length 
density is commonly associated with resistance to crack initiation, while 
crack depth reflects the tendency for crack propagation. Thus, the SCC 
susceptibility of the alloys, in descending order, is determined to be:

H3> H2> H4> H1. For the high-Si alloys, SCC susceptibility 
increased with increasing Si concentration in the matrix. The low-Cr 
alloy exhibited comparatively low susceptibility, only marginally 
higher than that of alloy H1 (0.85 wt% Si). These results imply that in 
hydrogenated water the effect of Cr depletion at GBs on IASCC is sec-
ondary to the influence of Si enrichment, particularly when Si concen-
trations exceed 2.63 wt%.
The higher SCC susceptibility of the high-Si alloys compared to the 
low-Cr alloy can be attributed to differences in oxidation kinetics and 
oxide film evolution. The thickness of the surface oxide film was 
observed to increase with Si content, indicating an accelerated corrosion 
rate. This acceleration is likely driven by the oxidation of Si and sub-
sequent degradation of the resulting Si-containing oxide film in high- 
temperature water. Silicon has a high affinity for oxygen and forms 
SiO2 [45,46]. Higher Si concentrations lead to increased SiO2 formation 
within the oxide layer. Although SiO2 is protective in high-temperature 
air, it becomes unstable in aqueous environments and tends to dissolve 
preferentially [30,38,47–50], introducing defects into the oxide film. 
Higher SiO2 content thus results in a more porous and less protective 
oxide structure, which facilitates continued corrosion and leads to 
thicker films. This porous oxide provides pathways for oxygen diffusion 
along GBs, promoting intergranular oxidation. Furthermore, the defec-
tive oxide is more prone to fracture under applied stress, which can 
trigger crack initiation. Consistent with this mechanism, oxide film 
exfoliation and Si depletion in the remaining oxide layers were observed 
in the high-Si alloys. STEM characterization of intergranular oxides and 
crack tips further confirmed that both GB diffusion rates and overall 
corrosion rates increase with Si content. In summary, the rapid degra-
dation of the oxide film in high-Si alloys, driven by SiO₂ dissolution, 
accelerates corrosion and reduces the stress required for oxide fracture, 
thereby explaining the positive correlation between Si content and SCC 
susceptibility.
In contrast, the low-Cr alloy (H4) exhibited low SCC susceptibility, 
likely due to the slower degradation of its oxide film. Compositional 
analysis suggests that its inner oxide layer is similar to those of the high- 
Si alloys but with lower Cr and Si fractions [38]. Chromium is known to 
reduce corrosion rates by forming a protective Cr-rich oxide that hinders 
ion diffusion [51]. The composition and thickness of the inner oxide 
layer depend strongly on the matrix Cr content, with higher Cr levels 
leading to higher Cr fractions in the oxide and thinner films. The 
measured oxide thickness on alloy H4 (~0.93 μm) is consistent with its 
low Cr content and aligns with findings from Terachi et al. [51]. Despite 
developing the second-thickest oxide film after H3, alloy H4’s inner 
oxide layer, which had a low SiO2 content, retained its integrity and 
resisted transformation into a porous structure. Thus, despite its thick-
ness, the oxide on alloy H4 retained resistance to ion diffusion and 
mechanical fracture, unlike the brittle oxide films on high-Si alloys.
However, based on the above analysis, one might expect alloy H4 to 
have the lowest SCC susceptibility, given its lower Si content compared 
to H1 (0.85 wt% Si). This discrepancy is likely explained by the much 
higher Cr content in H1 (19.84 wt%) and, consequently, the formation 
of higher Cr containing inner oxide, which mitigates the detrimental 
effects of SiO2 dissolution.
Based on these findings, we conclude that Si enrichment at GBs, 
rather than Cr depletion, is a mor important factor contributing to high 
IASCC susceptibility in hydrogenated high-temperature water. There-
fore, controlling Si concentration at low levels is essential for developing 
advanced IASCC-resistant alloys. It should be noted, however, that these 
conclusions are environment-specific. Since this study was conducted in 
hydrogenated water, further validation in oxygenated conditions is 
necessary, as different oxidation and dissolution behaviors may alter the 
relative contributions of Si and Cr. Many researchers have reported that

5. Conclusion
To clarify the respective roles of GB Si enrichment and Cr depletion 
in IASCC, four model alloys with systematically varied Si and Cr con-
centrations were subjected to slow strain rate testing in simulated hy-
drogenated high-temperature water, with testing stopped once 7 % 
plastic strain was achieved. Distinct differences in crack initiation 
behavior were observed, and these differences were correlated with 
insights from comprehensive microstructural characterization. The 
main findings are as follows: 
1) SCC susceptibility in hydrogenated water increases significantly with 
increasing Si content, while showing low dependency on Cr content. 
This indicates that Si segregation at GBs, rather than Cr depletion, is 
a more important factor controlling crack initiation in such envi-
ronments. Cr depletion alone is insufficient to induce substantial 
IASCC.
2) The elevated SCC susceptibility of high-Si alloys is attributed to the 
dissolution of SiO2 in the inner oxide layer, which leads to the for-
mation of a porous and defective oxide film. This degraded oxide film 
structure accelerates intergranular oxidation and compromises the 
mechanical integrity of the film, thereby facilitating crack initiation 
under applied stress.
3) Based on these results, it is recommended that Si content be main-
tained at a much lower level when developing advanced IASCC- 
resistant structural materials for nuclear reactor core components.
