ABSTRACT  
Irradiation-assisted stress corrosion cracks (IASCCs) from a proton-irradiated 316 L stainless steel (2.5 dpa) after 
constant load test in simulated PWR primary water were analyzed. The irradiation damages are consistent with 
those from neutron irradiations at similar dose. The IASCC cracks show microstructural and microchemical 
features similar to those reported from neutron-irradiated samples, confirming that proton is effective in 
emulating neutron for IASCC study. Si segregated at grain boundary gets oxidized preferentially beyond the crack 
tip and then dissolves into solution, resulting in enhanced porosity within the intergranular oxide. The Si effect is 
an important factor behind IASCC in reducing environment.   
1. Introduction 
Irradiation assisted stress corrosion cracking (IASCC) is a critical 
mode of environmental degradation of in-core components in nuclear 
power plants that greatly impacts their safety and economy [1,2]. A 
prominent example of IASCC is the cracking of baffle/former bolts in 
pressurized water reactors (PWRs) which has been systematically 
investigated in many countries [3–6]. Clarifying the underlying mech-
anism is imperative for mitigating IASCC. Despite extensive research 
efforts, an understanding of IASCC still remains vague [1,7]. The main 
reasons are: 1) multiple factors (e.g. radiolysis induced environmental 
changes, radiation induced element segregation (RIS) at grain bound-
aries, and localized deformation mode) are coupled in the processes of 
IASCC and it is difficult to separate them out individually; 2) the limited 
availability of neutron-irradiated materials for IASCC study along with 
the added complications of handling the radioactive sample in the 
laboratory. 
Up to now, facilities for testing macro-scale neutron-radiated sam-
ples exist only in a small number of labs and the research efforts have 
been focused mainly on measuring the crack growth rate or crack 
initiation threshold stress for IASCC [8–12]. Nevertheless, to gain a 
deeper insight into the IASCC mechanism, it is of great importance to 
examine the microstructure within the crack using modern analytical 
instruments. Thomas et al. [13,14] examined the microstructural and 
microchemical characteristics of IASCC cracks in stainless steel (SS) 
in-core components extracted from a boiling water reactor (BWR) and 
observed the following:  
• a layer of epitaxial Cr-rich spinel oxide film along the crack wall  
• Fe-rich coarse-grained spinel toward the crack center  
• a segment of narrow Cr-rich spinel along the grain boundary beyond 
the crack tip, and  
• a highly Ni-enriched zone near the grain boundary ahead of the 
intergranular oxide. 
A study on cracks from a PWR baffle/former bolt by the same group 
indicates similar structural and chemical features except that the spinel 
oxide is mostly polycrystalline and porous with some shear bands 
intersecting the crack wall [15]. Fukuya et al. [16] examined IASCC 
crack tips in highly neutron-irradiated 316 SS after constant load tests in 
both hydrogenated and oxygenated high temperature water environ-
ments and found that there was no significant oxidation near the crack

tips. More recently, Laurent et al. [17] investigated the microstructure of 
IASCC crack tips from a PWR baffle former bolt with high-performance 
transmission electron microscopes (TEMs) and found pronounced Ni 
enrichment ahead of the crack tip in excess of the segregation due to RIS. 
Moreover, they reported that Si was oxidized beyond the crack tip while 
it was locally depleted along the grain boundary ahead of the oxide tip 
and in the oxide within the crack center [17]. Although the investigation 
into the microstructure of the IASCC crack from neutron-irradiated 
samples helps to improve the understanding of IASCC, the available 
data is still too limited to clarify the mechanism behind this complicated 
issue. 
Ion irradiation has been proposed to emulate neutron irradiation as it 
can significantly reduce the time and cost of irradiation and subsequent 
characterization. More importantly, many microstructure and property 
features of neutron-irradiated samples can be successfully reproduced 
with ion irradiation when the irradiation parameters are carefully 
controlled [18–22]. Nevertheless, a major drawback of ion irradiation is 
that the irradiation damage is only limited to a thin surface layer of 
sample, posing challenges for assessing mechanical properties and 
IASCC susceptibility of the irradiated material. For example, heavy ions 
at 5 MeV only penetrate up to 2 µm in steels. Fortunately, protons can 
generate much thicker irradiation damage zone (~20 µm at 2 MeV). 
Given that SCC initiation occurs within one grain domain from the 
sample surface, the acceleration effect of irradiation on this process can 
be effectively captured on proton-irradiated samples. Hence, numerous 
studies on IASCC have been performed with proton-irradiated samples 
[23–27]. However, the microstructure and microchemistry character-
ization of the IASCC cracks from proton-irradiated samples are sur-
prisingly absent. Such analysis is of great importance because the 
microstructural and microchemical features of IASCC cracks from 
proton-irradiated samples should be compared with those in 
neutron-irradiated samples to validate proton irradiation as a tool to 
emulate neutron irradiation in IASCC study. Further, such 
high-resolution analysis is critical for improving the understanding of 
IASCC mechanism. 
This work examines IASCC cracks from a proton-irradiated 316 L SS 
after constant load test in simulated PWR primary water with the aid of 
advanced analytical instruments. The acquired microstructural and 
microchemical features near the crack tips were compared with those 
from neutron-irradiated samples and the consistency between them was 
evaluated. Thereafter, the insights into IASCC mechanism was 
discussed.

2.2. SCC initiation test 
After proton irradiation, constant load experiment was performed in 
a simulated PWR primary water at 320 ºC with 1000 ppm B as H3BO3, 2 
ppm Li as LiOH, and 35 cc H2/kg H2O. A refreshed 4-L stainless steel 
autoclave connected to a recirculation water loop was used in the irra-
diated material testing lab (IMTL) at the University of Michigan. The 
autoclave was refreshed approximately 3 times per hour. The sample 
was loaded to 140 MPa using a servo installed below the autoclave. This 
load is well below the irradiated yield strength (estimated to be ~700 
MPa based on the measured change in hardness) to avoid any local 
plastic deformation in the irradiated region. The test was stopped after 
the sample had been constantly loaded for 4716 h. 
2.3. Microstructure characterization 
TEM specimens for post-irradiation analysis were extracted from the 
bars by mechanical grinding, punching with a disc punch, and electro-
polishing with a TenuPol-5 twinjet polisher. Before jet-polishing, the 
flow rate was adjusted to ensure the solution from both sides were 
balanced. The specimens were pre-polished to a thickness of ~20 µm on 
the back side, and then polished from both sides until perforated. The 
sample thinning was conducted to place the electron transparent region 
of the sample at the relatively uniform damage region. Meanwhile, the 
void size was checked against a cross-section sample to ensure that the 
peak damage region was avoided. Post-irradiation characterization was 
performed using a JEOL 3011 TEM operated at 300 kV. Voids were

3. Results 
Fig. 1 shows the inverse pole figure and the phase map overlaid with 
grain boundary network from the EBSD measurement on one un- 
irradiated sample surface orthogonal to the irradiated one. The result 
from the irradiated surface shows similar uniform and equiaxed grain 
structure. The grain size is around 80 µm with the twin boundary 
ignored. Some ferrite stringers were detected from the phase map 
(Fig. 1b) which accounts for ~0.5% in area. Element segregation at 
original grain boundary was analyzed. The EDS line profile across a 
grain boundary shows that Cr, Mo and P are enriched while Fe and Ni are 
depleted at the grain boundary (Fig. 1c). Mo is enriched up to 8 at% 
which is well above the bulk level. There is no Si segregation at grain 
boundary. From the SRIM damage profile (Fig. 1d), the proton damaged

initiation and early-stage propagation.

number density is (5.6± 0.6) × 1022/m3, contributing to a dislocation 
length density (the sum of all loop circumferences per volume) of 
2.9× 1014/m2. The size distribution of voids and Frank loops are plotted 
in Fig. 2c and d.

4. Discussion 
The proton irradiation induced microstructure changes in this ma-
terial will be firstly compared with those from neutron-irradiated ma-
terials. Then the microstructural and microchemical features of both 
cracks will be analyzed in reference with IASCC cracks from neutron- 
irradiated stainless steels. Afterwards, the IASCC mechanism will be 
discussed based on the results. 
4.1. Irradiation damage 
For neutron-irradiated samples (damage ranges from 6 to 25 dpa and 
irradiation temperature ranges from 310 to 330◦C), the size and density 
were reported to be 6.4–11.6 nm and 1.2–14.9× 1022/m3 for Frank 
loops respectively, and 2–6 nm and 2–30.9× 1021/m3 for voids [17]. 
The stainless steel in this work was irradiated to 2.5 dpa at 360◦C. The 
Frank loop is 16.7± 8.2 nm in size with a number density of (5.6± 0.6) 
× 1022/m3 and the void is 6.0± 1.4 nm in size with a number density of 
(5.9± 3.6)× 1021/m3. Thus, the major microstructure features induced 
by proton irradiation in this sample are quantitatively consistent with 
those measured from neutron-irradiated stainless steels in PWR 
conditions. The RIS at grain boundary (Fig. 7f) is also very comparable 
with that measured from neutron-irradiated samples [17,32,33]. It 
should be noted that Mo depletion, which is normally detected at the 
grain boundary in neutron-irradiated samples [17,32,33], is not found 
in this sample (Fig. 7f). That is probably because Mo was enriched at the 
grain boundaries prior to proton irradiation (Fig. 1c). Although P seg-
regates in the un-irradiated grain boundary as well, it is enriched even 
more after irradiation. Overall, proton irradiation conducted in this 
work yields comparable microstructure damage with neutron 
irradiation. 
4.2. Crack microstructure analysis

boundary B (22.4º vs 16.3º). So grain boundary A is a random high angle 
boundary while grain boundary B has a misorientation angle that is very 
close to the limit for low angle grain boundary. The difference in 
misorientation angle may result in notable difference in cracking pro-
pensity and microstructure between these two cracks. From Fig. 12f, 
grain boundary B shows a lower degree of RIS than grain boundary A 
(Fig. 7f). It has been reported that low angle grain boundary is more 
resistant to RIS than random high angle grain boundary and the resis-
tance decreases with increasing misorientation angle [34,35]. In

the flank along the crack depth is smaller for crack A. Thus, it is believed 
that crack A was growing faster than crack B when the test was stopped. 
Although there is notable difference in cracking propensity between 
these two cracks, they still share some common microstructure features. 
4.2.1. Crack flank 
Both cracks contain a single layer of epitaxial spinel oxide along the 
crack walls which extends all the way to the oxide tip (Figs. 4, 5, 9 and 
10). The spinel oxide contains large fractions of Fe and Cr and a small 
amount of Ni (Figs. 7b and c, 12a-c). The study on the IASCC crack of 
baffle former bolt reveals that magnetite forms in the crack center which 
is also a type of spinel and contains large amounts of Fe and Cr [17]. The 
earlier work by Thomas et al. also suggests Cr-Fe spinel forms next to 
metal substrate in IASCC crack of PWR baffle bolt [15]. Concentrated Ni 
near the oxide/metal interface is evident in crack B (Figs. 11 and 12) and 
has also been observed by Legras et al. [17]. Such Ni enrichment is 
insignificant in crack A (Fig. 6) probably because this crack was still 
actively growing and the exposure time was not long enough to develop 
such feature near the crack tip. Interestingly, Thomas et al. also found 
that epitaxial Cr-Fe spinel forms along the crack wall up to crack tip in 
boiling water reactor (BWR) service components and Ni enrichment 
exists near the oxide/metal interface [13,14]. Such similarity suggests 
that the local environment condition within the crack may not differ too 
much between these two types of water chemistry. 
Thomas et al. [15] reported that the crack flanks were intersected by 
shear bands from the matrix and proposed a discontinuous cracking 
mode. However, there is no shear band or dislocation channel in either 
the cracks presented here (Figs. 5 and 9) or in the work of Legras et al. 
[17]. Thus, the generation of such localized deformation zone should be 
related to the stress state and the deformability of material, but is not 
essential to the occurrence of IASCC. 
4.2.2. Crack tip 
Grain boundary migration was detected in grain boundary A but not 
in boundary B, which is attributed to the difference in grain boundary 
structure. Grain boundary migration beyond the oxide tip in IASCC 
crack of PWR baffle bolt was not evident in the previous works [15]. 
Actually it is challenging to confirm grain boundary migration when the 
migration zone is very narrow. In the work of Legras et al. [17], the Ni 
enrichment zone beyond the oxide shows a tapered shape which sug-
gests that grain boundary migration probably has occurred. Grain 
boundary migration has also been observed in some but not all IASCC 
cracks of 304 SS from BWR component [13]. For un-irradiated materials 
tested in PWR primary water, grain boundary migration was barely 
observed in the SCC cracks of stainless steel [36] but can be easily 
detected in alloy 600 [37] and alloy 690 [30,31]. Also, the susceptibility 
to migration is closely related to the grain boundary structure [29]. 
Therefore, whether the grain boundary migrates depends on the 
boundary structure and the alloy composition. Grain boundary migra-
tion may be promoted by irradiation due to enhanced diffusion of solute 
atoms, but it is not a prerequisite for the occurrence of SCC. For grain 
boundary A, the migration zone is highly enriched in Ni and depleted in 
Fe, Cr and Mn (Fig. 6). Si and P concentrate in either the original 
boundary or the migrated grain boundary with contents lower than 
those in the pristine boundary (Fig. 7e). As for grain boundary B, a 
segment of boundary just beyond the oxide has higher Ni content and 
lower contents of Fe, Cr, Si and P (Fig. 12e) compared with the pristine 
boundary. Therefore, regardless of the occurrence of grain boundary 
migration, the region just ahead of the oxide tip contains more Ni and 
less other elements than the as-irradiated grain boundary, which is 
consistent with the previous analytical results of IASCC cracks from 
either PWR or BWR components [13–15,17]. That is probably due to the 
combined effects of Ni expulsion from oxidation [38–40] and prefer-
ential outward diffusion of Fe, Cr, Si and P which are prone to either 
oxidation or dissolution. 
Whether the grain boundary migrates affects the composition of 
oxide tip to some extent. For grain boundary A, although Si is enriched 
at the oxide tip (Fig. 7d and c), the maximum Si concentration in the 
oxide is lower than that in the pristine boundary. As to grain boundary B,

4.3. Insights into IASCC mechanism 
The microstructures of IASCC crack of stainless steel revealed in this 
work are summarized in Fig. 13. Spinel oxide layer epitaxial with the 
matrix covers both the sample surface and crack flank and extends 
beyond the crack tip. A layer of Ni-enriched transition zone forms be-
tween oxide and matrix. Radiation-induced voids may be retained in the 
oxide layer. Si diffuses out preferentially along grain boundary and 
concentrates in the oxide tip temporarily. Then the oxidized Si gradually 
dissolves into solution and leads to a porous interface within the oxide 
beyond crack tip. The boundary zone right beyond the oxide tip contains 
higher concentration of Ni and lower contents of other elements 
compared to the pristine grain boundary. 
Although there are some variations in microstructure between these 
two cracks due to the difference in grain boundary structure, the main 
features of the sampled cracks are similar to those from neutron- 
irradiated samples, further confirming the validity of using proton 
irradiation for IASCC study. Irrespective of the length of crack, inter-
granular oxidation always extends beyond the crack tip, suggesting that 
grain boundary oxidation is an inevitable precursor of IASCC, as in the 
case of SCC of Fe-Cr-Ni alloys [30,36,37]. The strength of grain 
boundary decreases as intergranular oxide forms and the oxidized grain 
boundary fractures when its strength drops below the applied stress. 
However, Fukuya et al. reported that there is no significant oxidation 
near the crack tip of highly irradiated (38 dpa) stainless steel [41]. 
Nishioka et al. also found that intergranular cracking could occur on 
highly irradiated (35 dpa) stainless steel in inert environment [42]. So it 
seems that intergranular cracking without oxidation only applies to the 
highly irradiated stainless steel. While for structural materials used in 
the reactor core of nuclear plants, it normally takes many years to 
accumulate a high dose during which intergranular oxidation and IASCC 
can occur. 
Intergranular oxidation is closely linked to the element segregation 
at grain boundary. Si has the highest affinity for oxygen among the el-
ements segregated at the grain boundary. Thus, Si is consumed fast near 
the oxidation front and chemical gradient develops along the grain 
boundary. The chemical gradient drives Si preferentially diffuses to the 
oxide front and gets oxidized. That is why Si is enriched in the 
intergranular oxide tip and depleted in the grain boundary immediately 
beyond the oxide tip (Figs. 7 and 12). As pointed out in the previous 
works [12,43,44], Si containing oxide is not stable in high temperature 
water and is subject to dissolution, which explains the depletion of Si in 
the oxide behind the oxide tip (Figs. 7b and 12a). The depletion of Si in 
oxide formed in high temperature water has also been found on Si 
containing model alloys [45] and additively manufactured stainless 
steel with Si-rich oxide inclusions [46]. Consistently, the preferential 
oxidation of Si at the grain boundary and subsequent dissolution of 
oxidized Si have been revealed in oxidized grain boundaries from 
proton-irradiated stainless steel [47] and in IASCC cracks from baffle 
former bolts as well [17]. P shows similar trend as Si except that it is not 
accumulated in the oxide tip (Fig. 12d), indicating that P dissolves 
directly into solution during intergranular oxidation. As P does not 
participate in the oxidation, it would not degrade the property of oxide 
and affect the cracking susceptibility. In addition, it has been reported 
that doping with P did not promote the intergranular cracking of model 
stainless steel in simulated PWR primary water [48]. Thus, the disso-
lution of oxidized Si probably leads to porosity observed in the center of 
intergranular oxide (Figs. 5a, 9b and 10a), which can accelerate the 
diffusion of oxidant and significantly reduce the strength of grain 
boundary, thus increasing the propensity to grain boundary fracture and 
the susceptibility to SCC. Consistently, the crack growth rate tests 
indicate that elevated Si (either added in bulk or adjusted by 
post-irradiation annealing) can significantly promote the crack growth 
rate of stainless steel in hydrogenated water [12,48,49]. It has also been 
reported that the length of intergranular oxide increases with increasing 
irradiation dose for both proton and neutron irradiated stainless steels in 
simulated PWR water [50–52], and Deng et al. linked the accelerated 
oxidation rate with the irradiation-induced Cr depletion at grain 
boundary [50,52]. Such conclusion needs to be further confirmed as the 
influence of the grain boundary structure was not considered. In this 
work, the Cr in intergranular oxide tip is not depleted due to the outward 
diffusion of Cr. Cr is even enriched at the oxide tip in crack A (Fig. 7d). 
Our recent finding reveals that the oxide along irradiated grain bound-
ary even contains higher Cr content than its counterpart in un-irradiated 
region in spite of irradiation-induced Cr depletion [53]. In addition, 
previous works suggest that the change of SCC susceptibility of stainless 
steel in simulated PWR water does not correlate with Cr depletion at 
grain boundary [54,55]. Thus, it is believed that the segregation of Si is a 
more important element of IASCC mechanism for stainless steel in 
simulated PWR primary water.

5. Conclusions 
This work studied the microstructural and microchemical features of 
IASCC cracks from a proton-irradiated 316 L SS (2.5 dpa) after constant 
load crack initiation test in simulated PWR primary water. The conclu-
sions are summarized as follows: 
1. The irradiation damage induced by proton irradiation (void, dislo-
cation loop and RIS) are qualitatively and quantitatively consistent 
with those from neutron-irradiated samples at similar doses.  
2. The IASCC cracks studied in this work show microstructural and 
microchemical features similar to those reported from neutron- 
irradiated samples: the crack flank is covered with an epitaxial 
layer of Cr-Fe spinel oxide which extends to the crack tip; the 
intergranular oxide tip is enriched in Si and the boundary just 
beyond the oxide is further enriched in Ni and leaner in other ele-
ments compared to the as-irradiated grain boundary.  
3. Cracking was shown to initiate in the oxide of a grain boundary after 
intergranular oxidation, after which the crack progressed down the 
grain boundary. Grain boundary oxidation is a necessary precursor of 
IASCC for stainless steel irradiated to low doses while grain boundary 
migration is not essential to the occurrence of IASCC.  
4. Si segregated at grain boundary tends to diffuse out and get oxidized 
preferentially during intergranular oxidation. The oxidize Si tends to 
dissolve into solution, leading to a porous interface along the center 
of intergranular oxide. The Si effect is likely a key element of IASCC 
mechanism for stainless steel in PWR primary water.
