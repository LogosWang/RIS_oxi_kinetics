   Abstract 
 Irradiation-assisted stress corrosion cracking (IASCC) refers 
to the process(es) by which irradiation enhances the inherent 
susceptibility of alloys to stress corrosion cracking (SCC). 
This article begins with an introduction to the IASCC problem 
experienced in light water reactors, followed by a summary of 
existing plant and laboratory data that reveal its dependence 
on irradiation, environment and alloy parameters. The spe-
ciﬁ c effects of irradiation on IASCC are classiﬁ  ed into two 
categories: the effects on water chemistry and the effects on 
microstructure. Water chemistry effects include radiolysis 
and its effects on corrosion potential, and effects of corrosion 
potential on IASCC. Microstructure effects include radiation- 
induced segregation (RIS), irradiated microstructure, swell-
ing and creep, and hydrogen and helium generation. Leading 
mechanisms proposed to explain the role of radiation in the 
SCC process are: radiolysis and crack tip strain rate, grain 
boundary chromium depletion, irradiation hardening, local-
ized deformation and RIS of minor elements. As nuclear 
power plants operate for longer, an increased incidence of 
SCC can be expected unless active mitigation steps are taken. 
Drawing on the accumulated understanding over the past four 
decades of the key processes believed to control IASCC, a set 
of attributes of an ideal, IASCC-resistant alloy are identiﬁ ed 
as a ﬁ rst step.

  1. Introduction 
 Nuclear power is rapidly becoming established as a key source 
of clean, low-cost energy throughout the world. Today, some 
441 nuclear power plants are in operation worldwide, supply-
ing about 17 %  of the world ’ s electricity needs. More than 60 
nuclear reactors are under construction, and another  ∼ 400 are 
planned or proposed. The success of this technology critically 
depends on the safe, reliable and economical operation of 
these plants. Stress corrosion cracking (SCC) of core internal 
components must be mitigated or managed to ensure the long-
term success of nuclear energy. SCC has occurred in all water 
cooled reactors, including boiling water reactors (BWR) and 
pressurized water reactors (PWR), with a greater incidence 
in unirradiated, out-of-core components, especially between 
1970 and 1990. As these materials, component designs and 
water chemistries have improved, and operating time and 
ﬂ uence has increased, an increasing percentage of cracking 
problems has occurred in irradiated components (IASCC). 
 The core of a nuclear reactor is an extreme environment 
consisting of high temperature water, imposed stresses and 
strains, intense radiation ﬁ  elds, and a relatively corrosive 
medium (Figure  1  ). Most irradiated core components consist 
of austenitic stainless steels and nickel-base alloys exposed 
in environments that span oxygenated to hydrogenated water 
at  ∼ 270 – 340 ° C. Initially, the affected components were pri-
marily small components (bolts, springs, etc.) or components 
designed for replacement (fuel rods, control blades, or instru-
mentation tubes). However, in the last  ∼ 20   years, IASCC has 
been observed in structural components such as PWR bafﬂ  e 
former bolts and BWR core shrouds and top guides. 
 Extensive literature exists for SCC under unirradiated 
conditions, and the basic factors and dependencies are well 
deﬁ ned and reasonably well modeled for austenitic stainless 
steel and nickel alloys (e.g., Alloys 600 and its weld metals) 
(Andresen & Was, 2008; Bruemmer et al., 1992; Cowan & 
Gordon, 1977; Ford & Andresen, 1994; Jones, 2001; Staehle, 
Forty, & VanRooyen, 1967; Staehle, Hochmann, McCright, 
& Slatern, 1977). A complete consensus on the underlying 
mechanism of cracking has not emerged, although the well-
behaved continuum in crack growth rate response vs. material/
composition (including from stainless steels to nickel alloys), 
water chemistry, temperature and radiation suggests that a 
common crack growth mechanism is operative (Andresen, 
1996, 2001; Andresen & Ford, 1988; Andresen & Was, 2008; 
Ford & Andresen, 1988, 1994). 
 Our understanding has evolved from the view that SCC 
occurs under very speciﬁ c and unique conditions, to the view 
that a continuum in response exists (Andresen, 1996, 2001; 
Andresen & Ford, 1988; Andresen & Was, 2008; Ford & 
Andresen, 1988, 1994). With steady improvement in labora-
tory and plant detection of SCC, it is clear that SCC occurs 
under a wide range of conditions, but also at a wide range of 
growth rates. Figures  2   and  3   show examples of the effect 
of environment (corrosion potential and water purity), mate-
rial condition (sensitized vs. cold worked) and stress (stress 
intensity factor) on SCC growth rates; the solid curves are 
the predicted response (Andresen & Ford, 1988; Andresen 
& Was, 2008; Ford & Andresen, 1988, 1994). SCC occurs 
even at low corrosion potential (Figure  2 ), and thus the

irradiation dose (neutron ﬂ uence) have been well established, 
it remains possible that additional factors will emerge at high 
ﬂ uence (e.g.,  > 30   dpa, or displacements per atom). 
 Intergranular (IG) SCC is promoted in austenitic stainless 
steels above a  “ threshold ”  ﬂ uence (Figures  5  and  6 ). This 
occurs in oxygenated (e.g., BWR) water above 2 to 5 × 10 20    
n/cm 2  (E > 1   MeV), which corresponds to about 0.3 – 0.7   dpa, 
and the ﬂ uence depends on the stress, water chemistry (esp. 
sulfate and chloride), operating time and other factors. 
Attempts to reproduce the same level of IG cracking in inert 
environments have been unsuccessful, conﬁ  rming that it is 
an environmental cracking phenomenon, not simply a change 
in the microstructure, mechanical properties and the overall 
response of the irradiated material in an inert environment. 
 Cracking in hydrogenated water [i.e., BWR hydrogen 
water chemistry (HWC) or PWR water] is typically observed

 It is important to distinguish the results of different kinds 
of SCC testing. Crack growth testing typically uses fracture 
mechanics specimens, commonly a compact tension (CT) 
specimen. It has the signiﬁ cant advantage of continuous, on-
line monitoring of crack length vs. time, usually based on 
an electric current, potential drop technique. It can provide 
resolution of about 1    µ m, and can accurately characterize the 
inherent resistance to crack advance (i.e., the stage beyond 
the ill-deﬁ ned  initiation  stage), as well as the dependencies 
on corrosion potential, stress intensity factor, etc. Smooth 
specimen tensile tests, whether using constant load, constant 
displacement, or slow strain rate (SSRT) techniques, are sim-
pler to perform, but represent some combination of initiation 
and growth. SSRT tests impose failure, and can over-state or 
under-state the SCC susceptibility. Constant load or displace-
ment tests generally require periodic interruption for exami-
nation, and  “ initiation ”  can represent microscopic cracks or 
complete failure.

  2. Plant data on IASCC 
 Even early observations exhibited characteristics and depen-
dencies consistent with unirradiated materials, and appro-
priately led to the description of irradiation  assisted  stress 
corrosion cracking. Subsequent observation made this clearer 
e.g., by showing a similar effect of the water purity (as mea-
sured solution conductivity) on unirradiated and irradiated 
stainless steel (Figure  4 ). The initial reports of IASCC occurred 
in the early 1960s (Andresen & Ford, 1995; Andresen, Ford, 
Murphy, & Perks, 1990a; Armijo, Low, & Wolff, 1965; Brown, 
Storhok, & Gates, 1967; Bruemmer et al., 1999; Cheng, 1964, 
1970, 1975; Cowan & Gordon, 1977; Duncan et al., 1965; Ford 
& Andresen, 1994; Hanninen & Aho-Mantila, 1988; Jacobs & 
Wozadlo, 1985; Multer, 1975; Pashos et al., 1964; Pasupathi 
& Klingensmith, 1981; Schaffer, 1962; Scott, 1994; Storrer 
& Locke, 1970; Was & Andresen, 1992a; 2007) and involved 
intergranular cracking of stainless steel fuel cladding initiat-
ing from the water side. Post-irradiation mechanical tests, by 
comparison, were performed in inert environments at various 
strain rates and temperatures and exhibited mostly ductile, 
transgranular cracking. Grain boundary carbide precipitation 
was rarely observed, indicating that thermal sensitization was 
not an adequate explanation, although it was later found that 
radiation-induced segregation (RIS) did occur, and resulted in 
chromium depletion at grain boundaries.

 Time-to-failure and stress were found to be correlated, 
with the earliest failures occurring in thin-walled rods with 
small fuel-to-cladding gaps, where fuel swelling strains were 
largest. Areas of high heat ﬂ  ux  –  where the highest ﬂ uence 
developed and the greatest fuel-cladding interaction (high-
est stresses and strains) existed  –  were the regions of high-
est cracking incidence. The stainless steel cladding in PWR 
service exhibited a lower cracking incidence of intergranular 
failure, and some believed that cracking occurred because 
of off-chemistry conditions or stress rupture. But in the last 
two decades, it has become clear that SCC is also  irradiation 
assisted  in PWR water. 
 The incidence of cracking in stainless steel and nickel 
alloys core components steadily increased. In 1976, neu-
tron source holders exhibited IASCC, and in 1978 cracking 
occurred in control rod absorber tubes. Low stress compo-
nents also cracked, such as instrument dry tubes and control 
blade handles and sheaths (Figure  5 ), generally at higher 
ﬂ uences and in creviced locations (Andresen, Vasatis, & Ford, 
1990b; Brown & Gordon, 1988; Gordon & Brown, 1990; Was 
& Andresen, 1992a, 2007). Incidents of IASCC, for example 
in BWR core shrouds (Andresen & Ford, 1995; Andresen 
et al., 1990; Ford & Andresen, 1994; Horn, Gordon, Ford, 
& Cowan, 1997) and PWR bafﬂ  e bolts (Heuze, Goltrant, & 
Cauvin, 1998; Scott, 1994; Scott, Meunier, Deydier, Silvestre, 
& Trenty, 2000) continued to emerge.

most BWRs have deployed an electrocatalytic technology 
called NobleChem ™  (Andresen, 1995; Andresen, Diaz, & 
Hettiarachchi, 2004; Hettiarachchi, Wozadlo, Diaz, Andresen, 
& Cowan, 1995) to create a low corrosion potential. While 
the actual corrosion potentials remain several hundred milli-
volts lower in PWRs because of the higher H 2  fugacity and 
the presence of the boron/lithium chemistry, what is impor-
tant in SCC is that there is no differential aeration cell present 
in either case to produce a shifted crack chemistry. 
 PWR cracking was often initially attributed to low ductil-
ity stress rupture, a mechanism that is also relevant to BWRs. 
Small amounts of intergranular cracking of irradiated stain-
less steels was reported in a few laboratory in SSRT tests 
in  ≈ 300 ° C inert environments (Manahan, Kohli, Santucci, 
& Sipush, 1989), although in many related experiments 
(Jacobs, Hale, & Siegler, 1986; Onchi, Dohi, Soneda, Navas, 
& Castano, 2003) no intergranular failure was found. Small 
amounts of intergranular cracking in inert tensile tests is not 
surprising and, since the early 1990s, the plant and laboratory 
IASCC data show that cracking is environmentally assisted 
and follows a well-behaved continuum in response over 
ranges in ﬂ uence, corrosion potential, temperature, stress, etc. 
(Andresen & Ford, 1995; Andresen et al., 1990; Bruemmer et 
al., 1999; Ford & Andresen, 1994). 
 Compared to BWRs, PWRs operate at a higher tempera-
ture, at  ≈ 10X higher peak neutron ﬂ ux and ﬂ uence in core 
structural components, at  ≈ 100X higher hydrogen fugacity, 
and with borated-lithiated water chemistry. The higher ﬂ  ux 
produces higher gamma heating and the possibility of local-
ized boiling and thermal concentration cells in crevices, 
which could lead to aggressive local chemistries. To address 
this concern, some bafﬂ e plate and former bolt designs have 
been modiﬁ ed to allow the bulk water to communicate more 
readily in the bolt shank area. 
 In both PWRs and BWRs, high ﬂ  uence brings the pos-
sibility of high silicon segregation to grain boundaries, and 
laboratory data on irradiated alloys and unirradiated alloys 
with elevated silicon show that the SCC response can be 
unaffected by high vs. low corrosion potential (Andresen & 
Morra, 2005b; Andresen, Morra, & Catlin, 2004). 
 SCC of Alloy 600 shroud head bolts was ﬁ rst observed in 
1986, and in 1984 stainless steel safe ends and in-core instru-
mentation tubes ﬁ rst exhibited SCC, with creviced compo-
nents cracking somewhat sooner (Brown & Gordon, 1988; 
Cowan & Gordon, 1977; Gordon & Brown, 1990), as sum-
marized in Figure  4 . The highest radiation exposure occurred 
for the intermediate range and source range monitor (IRM/
SRM) dry tubes, which contain ﬂ ux monitors housed in thin-
walled, annealed stainless steel tubes. Cracking initiated in 
the crevice between the spring housing tube and the guide 
plug at ﬂ uences between 0.5 and 1.0 × 10 22    n/cm 2  (E > 1   MeV). 
Wedging stresses from the thick oxide observed in the crevice 
were implicated, as other (applied and residual) stresses were 
negligible. 
 Average coolant conductivity is the primary distinguishing 
factor among plants, and it correlates strongly with cracking 
incidence (Figure  4 A – C). Each point in Figure  4  represents 
inspection results for one BWR plant, and data are normalized 
using reactor operating time (i.e.,  “ percentage of components 
with intergranular cracks divided by the on-line exposure 
time ” ). The scatter in Figure  4 A was attributed to variations 
in ﬂ uence and speciﬁ c ion chemistry, as well as limitations 
in the resolution of underwater visual inspection. Plants with 
high conductivity early in operation fall on a different distri-
bution, as this effect is not adequately reﬂ ected in the average 
(Figure  4 C) because cracks initiate and grow fast at high con-
ductivity, creating a stress intensity factor that can continue 
to drive the crack as the water purity improves. IASCC and 
water purity were also correlated in SCC of shroud head bolts 
(Figure  4 C) and creviced safe ends (Figure  4 B). The strong 
inﬂ uence of conductivity on cracking of stainless steel has 
also been shown in laboratory tests and plant recirculation 
piping, where predictive modeling (Andresen & Ford, 1988; 
Andresen et al., 1990; Ford & Andresen, 1988, 1994; Ford, 
Taylor, Andresen, & Ballinger, 1987; Ford et al., 1990) has 
been compared to ﬁ eld data on the operational time required 
to achieve a detectable crack (typically 10 %  of the wall thick-
ness). Preliminary prediction (Figure  4 C) of the shroud head 
bolt cracking response (Ford & Andresen, 1994) also provides 
good agreement with the observation. 
 Table  1  also shows that high strength, nickel-base alloys 
components (Andresen et al., 1990; Hanninen & Aho-
Mantila, 1988) have also experienced IASCC, with many 
incidents in lower radiation ﬂ ux regions (e.g., where the end-
of-life ﬂ uence is below  ≈ 5 × 10 19    n/cm 2 ), such as cracking of 
Inconel X-750 jet pump beams in BWRs. Alloy X-750 PWR 
fuel hold-down springs, which have an end-of-life ﬂ  uence 
of 1 – 10 × 10 21    n/cm 2 , also cracked extensively. Cracking may 
have been aggravated by vibrational stresses. Compared with 
stainless steels, the effects of irradiation on IASCC in high 
strength, precipitation-hardened nickel-base alloy compo-
nents have not been characterized as well.

ﬁ fteen years are BWR core shrouds (Andresen & Ford,

arises primarily from weld residual stresses and weld shrink-
age strains (Angeliu, Andresen, Sutliff, & Horn, 1999; Angeliu 
et al., 2000; Othon & Morra, 2008). Cracking is observed in 
both low ﬂ uence and moderate ﬂ uence areas, and the extent of 
the enhancement in SCC susceptibility by irradiation is limited 
because, while radiation hardening and RIS occur, radiation 
creep also relaxes the weld residual stress. SCC predictions for 
a BWR core shroud that account for the damaging effect of RIS 
and RH and the beneﬁ cial effect of radiation creep relaxation

interactions of these phenomena in the evolution of cracking.

e.g., from high coolant impurity levels or severe surface grind-
ing, predictions indicate that susceptibility will decrease with 
ﬂ uence in the shroud welds because of radiation-induced creep 
relaxation. Note that many shroud welds are in very low ﬂ ux 
areas where relaxation and other irradiation effects are small. 
 Extensive failures of PWR bafﬂ e bolts (Heuze et al., 1998; 
Scott et al., 2000), with large plant-to-plant and heat-to-heat 
differences, have been observed in the last ﬁ fteen years. Most 
bafﬂ e bolts are fabricated from type 316 stainless steel cold 
worked to  ≈ 15 %  to increase their yield strength. The complex 
bafﬂ e former structure exists in a PWR because their fuel does 
not have a surrounding  “ channel ” , so the bafﬂ e former struc-
ture must conform closely to the geometry of the fuel to provide 
well-distributed water ﬂ ow. The bafﬂ e former plates are usu-
ally made from annealed material, typically type 304 stainless 
steel. Because of their proximity to the fuel, very high ﬂ uences 
can develop  –  up to  ∼ 80 dpa by the end of the original design 
life, and proportionately longer for 60 – 80   year extended life. 
The high gamma ﬂ ux produces signiﬁ cant heating in the com-
ponents, estimated at up to  + 40 ° C, especially in designs where 
the PWR coolant does not have good access to the bolt shank. 
Although heat-to-heat variability is not understood, plants that 
load-follow (and therefore undergo power level changes and 
thermal cycles) are much more prone to bafﬂ e bolt cracking. 
Another aggravant is the thermal gradient and possible boiling 
(resulting in altered water chemistry) in the shank area of the 
bolt if the coolant access is restricted. However, a key element 
that must be factored into any understanding of bafﬂ  e bolt 
cracking is the very sizeable stress relaxation that occurs in 
the ﬁ rst few dpa, followed by preferential radiation swelling of 
the annealed bafﬂ e plates compared to the cold worked bafﬂ e 
bolts, which will cause reloading. The dynamic equilibrium 
between swelling and radiation creep, which determines the 
 “ reloading ”  stress in the bolt, is likely a complex function of 
many parameters, including local neutron ﬂ  ux, temperature, 
bafﬂ e plate geome try and composition. 
 Replaceable components often receive less attention than 
structural components. However, cracking that can impede the 
safe operation of the reactor is very important. Cracking of 
control rods and control rod blade handles can result in disso-
lution of B 4 C (making it unavailable to absorb neutrons when 
needed) and potentially affect the insertion of the control rods. 
An NRC Event Notiﬁ  cation Report on December 2, 2010 
(U.S.NRC, 2010) describes signiﬁ  cant cracking of control 
rods that was severe enough to raise concern for insertion. 
 Given the diversity of components that have cracked, 
and the low stresses of many, the incidence of IASCC can 
only be expected to grow. Strategies to mitigate IASCC 
(e.g., NobleChem ™  (Andresen, 1995; Andresen et al., 2004; 
Hettiarachchi et al., 1995) and manage IASCC (e.g., by 
showing some IASCC can be tolerated, installing mechanical 
restraints to mitigate the impact of IASCC in BWR shrouds, 
or selectively inspecting and replacing bafﬂ e bolts) have been 
successful. Designs have been pushed to lower stress with, for 
example greater annular tolerance so that B 4 C will not cause 
swelling of the stainless steel tubing in which it is contained. 
However, there is less success in understanding and identify-
ing resistant materials, and it is likely that materials well out-
side of the historical 18Cr-8Ni austenitic stainless steels will 
have to be considered to improve IASCC resistance. 
 While there have been few indications of cracking in pres-
sure vessel steels, and none in areas where radiation effects are 
involved, there are conceptual concerns for the effect of radia-
tion on yield strength, and the effect of yield strength on SCC 
of low alloy steels. Structural components are attached to the 
pressure vessel via attachment pads that are welded directly 
onto the pressure vessel before the structure is heat treated 
(tempered). Attachment pads are generally Alloy 182 weld 
metal, which is susceptible to SCC, and as cracks propagate 
through the pad to the interface with the pressure vessel, they 
can be at high stress intensity factor. While the ﬂ uence is suf-
ﬁ cient to affect the pressure vessel properties (especially yield 
strength and toughness), it is not sufﬁ cient to cause any conse-
quential creep relaxation of weld residual stresses. As the pres-
sure vessel ﬂ uence increases and its yield strength increases, 
its susceptibility to SCC (and its crack growth rate) increases. 
The discovery that ppb levels of chloride can increase the crack 
growth rate of low alloy steel by  > 100X (Figure  10  ) (Kumagai 
et al., 2009; Ritter & Seifert, 2004) adds to the concern.

similarly affect IASCC in BWR water (Figure  4 ). This cor-

 4 B) (Gordon & Brown, 1990), and is closely paralleled in 
out-of-core components (Andresen & Ford, 1988; Andresen 
et al., 1990; Brown & Gordon, 1988; Ford & Andresen, 
1988, 1994; Ford et al., 1987, 1990) At higher levels, the

potential conditions form in the PWR primary (where boron 
and lithium are present), the crack chemistry is dramatically 
altered, and high growth rates can result (Andresen & Was, 
2008; Andresen et al., 2007). 
   2.2. Corrosion potential  
Field and laboratory data demonstrate that corrosion potential 
is a very important parameter, with its effect being consistent 
from zero to low to high ﬂ uence, except in some high ﬂ uence 
materials and/or under high stress intensity factor conditions 
where high growth rates are always observed. Materials prone 
to high radiation-induced changes in grain boundary silicon 
level may exhibit a very limited effect of corrosion poten-
tial (Andresen & Morra, 2005a; Andresen et al., 2004). While 
corrosion potential usually affects crack initiation and growth, 
there is no evidence of  threshold  potential and, indeed, it is 
not fully accepted that irradiated materials exhibit IASCC in 
deaerated water. 
   2.3. Crevices   
Cracking is enhanced by crevices, primarily because of their 
ability to create a more aggressive crevice chemistry from the 
gradient in corrosion potential (in BWRs) or in temperature 
(most relevant to PWRs), which in turn can accelerate crack 
initiation. Stress and strain concentration can also occur in 
crevices. 
   2.4. T emperature   
Temperature increases both IASCC initiation and growth 
rate. 
   2.5. Fluence   
IASCC of annealed stainless steel was once thought to 
occur only at ﬂ  uences above  ≈ 0.3 × 10 21    n/cm 2 . But signiﬁ -
cant intergranular cracking in BWR core shrouds (which do 
not have thermal sensitization) occurs over a broad range 
of ﬂ uences, showing that a true ﬂ  uence threshold does not 
exist. (Andresen & Ford, 1995; Andresen et al., 1990) The 
observations of SCC in unirradiated, unsensitized stainless 
steel (with or without cold work) also undermine the con-
cept of a  threshold  ﬂ uence below which no SCC occurs. 
Thresholds in corrosion potential, water impurities, tem-
perature etc. (Andresen, 2001; Andresen, Angeliu, & Young, 
2001; Ford & Andresen, 1994; Ford et al., 1990) have also 
been disproven. 
   2.6. Integration of ﬂ uence effects   
Irradiation has a complex effect on SCC susceptibility. While 
RIS and radiation hardening increase susceptibility, radiation 
creep relaxation of constant displacement loads (e.g., bolts 
and welds) tends to reduce susceptibility. For these reasons, 
SCC in BWR shrouds and PWR bafﬂ e bolts does not always 
correlate strongly with ﬂ uence. SCC can only be interpreted 
and predicted by accounting for the conjoint effects of mul-
tiple factors. 
   2.7 . Dynamic stress and strain   
High stress and dynamic strains were responsible for the 
earliest incidents of IASCC, but cracking has since been 
observed at quite low stresses at high ﬂ uences during longer 
operating exposure. Laboratory and ﬁ  eld data indicate that 
IASCC growth can occur at stress intensity factors well below 
10   MPa √ m, and initiation can occur at  < 20 %  of the irradiated 
yield stress. 
   2.8. Cold work   
Bulk cold work, surface cold work (especially abusive sur-
face grinding), and weld residual strain in the heat affected 
zone tend to exacerbate all forms of SCC, although it can also 
delay the onset of some radiation effects (especially swelling) 
by creating more sinks for interstitials and vacancies. 
   2.9. Grain boundary chromium depletion   
Pre-existing grain boundary carbides and/or chromium deple-
tion are not essential to IASCC, although furnace sensitized 
stainless steels are clearly highly susceptible to cracking in-
core. Pre-existing chromium depletion, e.g., from thermal 
sensitization, will be magniﬁ  ed by irradiation, and increase 
IASCC susceptibility (primarily in pH-shifted environments, 
as can develop when potential or thermal gradients exist). 
In the absence of chromium depletion, the presence of grain 
boundary carbides or other particles is beneﬁ  cial. The role 
of nitrogen, sulphur, phosphorous, and other grain boundary 
segregants is less clear.

stress and strain, corrosion potential, solution conductivity,

  3.2. Crack growth 
 Crack growth data on unirradiated structural materials in 
BWR and PWR environments have been acquired since the 
1970s, and have been extensively discussed by Andresen 
and many other researchers (Andresen, 1993, 2002, 2005a; 
Andresen, Chou, Morra, Nelson, & Rebak, 2009; Andresen, 
Emigh, & Morra, 2005; Andresen & Ford, 1994; Andresen 
& Morra, 2005a, 2007a, 2007b, 2008a, 2008b; Andresen 
et al., 2004a; Young, Andresen, & Angeliu, 2001). The ﬁ  rst 
crack growth rate measurements on irradiated stainless 
steels in the laboratory (Andresen et al., 1996) and  in situ   
in an operating BWR (Andresen & Ford, 1995; Andresen 
et al., 1990) were performed by GE in the mid-1980s, and 
subsequently many other IASCC growth rate studies have 
been performed. Figure  14   shows an example of crack length 
vs. time data obtained in the earliest studies (Andresen 
et al., 1996), which used careful intergranular transitioning 
techniques, along with small sulfate additions to promote 
well-behaved crack growth. In these initial studies, it was 
proposed, then demonstrated, that irradiation affects four 
primary parameters: grain boundary chemistry from RIS; 
yield strength from radiation hardening; elevated corro-
sion potential from radiolysis of water; and, reduction in 
stress from radiation creep relaxation. Other parameters, 
such as swelling (especially differential swelling that can 
lead to reloading) and perhaps phase formation and precipi-
tate dissolution may occur and play a role in SCC at higher 
ﬂ uences. 
 Subsequent testing did not improve on the quality of data 
from these early tests, and problems related to poor IG tran-
sitioning, large errors in dcpd that resulted in much higher 
stress intensity factors than intended, specimen unloading 
during critical portions of the tests, failure to manage the test 
to obtain well-behaved data, etc. continue to affect the qual-
ity of some data. Summaries of the data (e.g., Figure  15   in 
BWR water) show extensive scatter, which makes it difﬁ cult 
to identify dependencies or even propose disposition lines for 
regulation with conﬁ dence.

  3.2.1. CGRs in unirradiated and irradiated stainless

Was & Andresen, 2007) compare the CGRs of irradiated 
stainless steels with unirradiated stainless steels. Increased 
CGRs in cold and warm work hardened 304L and 316 SS 
were observed in both BWR and PWR water, with the growth 
rate at a yield strength of  > 800   MPa being about an order 
of magnitude higher than in solution annealed materials. 
Sensitization is not a necessary condition for IGSCC growth, 
but it accelerates cracking at high corrosion potential. 
 Andresen (2002) summarized data that showed radiation 
hardening and cold work have similar effects on enhancing 
yield strength and also SCC growth rate. Figure  16   compares 
the CGRs in cold worked, sensitized and irradiated stain-
less steels (Was & Andresen, 2007). Unirradiated and irra-
diated alloys with similar yield strength show similar CGRs 
at low corrosion potential. The similarity in CGR response 
of unirradiated and irradiated materials can also be seen in 
their similarity in K dependence. As reported by Andresen 
(2002), a K 2.3  dependence was observed for unirradiated cold 
worked stainless steel tested over a wide K range (Figure  17  ), 
and irradiated stainless steel showed a similar K dependence 
(Figure  18  ). 
 In PWR water, Fukuya, Nakano, Fujii, and Torimaru 
(2004c) reported that the apparent CGR acquired from 
SSRT tests in deaerated water increased with increasing 
dissolved hydrogen, although the enhancement may be 
associated with the near-surface (initiation) phase of crack-
ing. However, the effect of hydrogen on CGR was less 
signiﬁ cant in high dose CW316 (50   dpa). PWR-irradiated 
pipe specimens were used for this study. The crack growth 
behavior in PWR water needs to be examined with addi-
tional CGR testing.  
  3.2.2. Key factors affecting IASCC growth rate     The 
scatter in the CGR data, whether measured in post-irradiation 
laboratory tests or in-reactor, is signiﬁ  cant. Discerning 
the effect of key parameters is generally not possible by 
statistical evaluation of large collections of (scattered) data 
 –  a parameter that affects CGR by a factor of 10 or 20 is 
impossible to identify with high statistical conﬁ  dence in a 
set of data exhibiting a 1000X scatter (Andresen, Gott, & 
Nelson, 1999). Similar to SCC of unirradiated materials, the 
dependencies are best identiﬁ  ed in speciﬁ c, well-controlled 
studies, especially studies where changes are made on-the-ﬂ y 
and with no other test perturbations.

effect of solute addition on CGR. Seven alloys were tested at 
K between 11 and 28   MPa  √  m. Compared with the high purity

comparable in NWC because of the difference in K. Miwa

improved the resistance to IASCC in PWR water compared to 
additions of molybdenum or hafnium. Not enough data exist 
to compare the CGRs for other element additions.  
  3.2.2.5. Effect of chromium depletion     Grain boundary 
Cr depletion has been recognized as deleterious to corrosion 
and stress corrosion resistance for decades. Like cold 
work, sensitization affects the SCC susceptibility before 
neutron ﬂ uence has accumulated, and there is an evolution 
in response during irradiation. Unlike cold work, where 
the characteristic microstructure is converted to a purely 
irradiated microstructure after  ∼ 5   dpa, irradiation enhances 
the extent of chromium depletion but does not eliminate the 
prior, wider chromium proﬁ le from thermal sensitization. In 
most cases, thermal sensitization results in a 30 – 70   nm width 
chromium depletion proﬁ  le near grain boundaries, while 
radiation segregation at  < 350 ° C produces a 3 – 5   nm wide 
chromium depletion proﬁ le, which therefore adds to, rather 
than substitutes, for the pre-existing chromium depletion 
proﬁ le. 
 Andresen (1994) and Bruemmer, Arey, and Charlot 
(1994) separately evaluated the effect of very narrow chro-
mium depletion proﬁ  les using multi-step heat treatments 
that nucleated grain boundary carbides, then eliminated the 
(wide) chromium depletion proﬁ  le (by heat treating near 
the Cr-Cr 23 C 6  equilibrium temperature of  ∼ 950 ° C for sev-
eral hours), then water quenching and heat treating at 400 –
 450 ° C. This produced a very narrow chromium depletion 
proﬁ le, although also a deep proﬁ  le, because at  ∼ 400 ° C the 
chromium, in equilibrium with the growing carbide, is only 
 ∼ 5 % . However, in high temperature water, SCC responded 
to the minimum in chromium in the grain boundary (chro-
mium diffuses rapidly along the grain boundary, and so the 
entire grain boundary is close to the chromium content at 
the interface of the growing carbide). This is true whether 
the minimum chromium is 5 % , 10 % , or 15 %   –  that is, there 
is no threshold chromium level at which SCC response sud-
denly changes. In contrast, sensitization tests including elec-
trochemical potentiokinetic repassivation (EPR, whether 
 single loop   or  double loop  ), respond to a threshold chro-
mium concentration in the vicinity of 14 – 15 % , and often 
are inﬂ uenced strongly by the width of the chromium proﬁ le 
at this threshold chromium level, not by the depth of the 
chromium proﬁ le. Thus, care must be used when interpret-
ing such measures of sensitization. 
 The impact of chromium depletion is larger in more aggres-
sive water chemistries, such as BWR water at high corrosion 
potential (especially when chloride or sulfate is also present), 
or even deaerated low pH water. There are some effects of 
chromium depletion in deaerated neutral water (Yonezawa, 
Iwamura, Fujimoto, & Ajiki, 1999), but it is not as pronounced 
as in aggressive water chemistry.

  3.2.2.9. Effect of cold work     Crack growth has been 
observed in annealed materials, but it increases with 
cold work, typically by a factor of 10 – 20X (Figure  28  ), 
both in aerated and deaerated water. The response tends 
to saturate with increase yield strength, so the difference 
between, for example, 800 and 1000   MPa, is not as 
consequential as between 200 and 400   MPa. The increase 
in crack growth rate makes it much easier to perform crack 

===== PDF PAGE 17 =====
G.S. Was et al.: IASCC  23
growth rate experiments, so observations of crack growth 
are much more common at elevated yield strength. While 
few materials are used in the cold worked condition, it 
is common for cold work to exist in the surface layers of 
many components. More importantly, weld residual  strains  
exist (along with weld residual stresses) in the heat affected 
zone of weld, and the equivalent room temperature tensile 
strain is often in the range of 20 %  and sometimes as high as 
30 % . Unsurprisingly, most SCC in stainless steels occurs in 
the heat affected zone near the weld fusion line, where the 
residual strain is highest (Andresen, 2010b; Angeliu et al., 
1999, 2000). 
 Another source of increased yield strength is irradiation, 
which begins to enhance the yield strength of austenitic materi-
als at  ∼ 0.2   dpa, and it saturates at  ∼ 5   dpa. The mechanism of 
hardening is quite different than precipitation hardening or the 
dislocation structures that form during cold work. The very small 
vacancy and interstitial loops that form during irradiation impede 
dislocation motion, but once the ﬁ  rst few dislocations move 
through the structure, subsequent dislocations move through the 
channel readily. The reason these different mechanisms of hard-
ening all produce a similar effect on crack growth rate is attrib-
uted to the size of the plastic zone (smaller in high yield strength 
materials) and the resultant steeper strain proﬁ le at the crack tip.

  4. Effects of irradiation

or pre-irradiated materials tested without an active radiation 
ﬂ ux). Radiation dose rate in Rads/h is often used in radiolysis,

1994; Bruemmer et al., 1994).

  4.1. Water chemistry 
  4.1.1. Radiolysis and its effect on corrosion 
potential     Corrosion potential, not oxidant and reductant 
concentrations  per se , control SCC susceptibility (Andresen 
& Young, 1995; Andresen et al., 1990; Ford & Andresen, 
1994). The lower hydrogen concentration in BWRs (typically 
10 – 40   ppb H 2 ) distinguishes BWRs from PWRs (typically 
3   ppm H 2 ) because it permits the radiolytic formation of 
oxidants. Above  ≈ 500   ppb (5.6 cc/kg) H 2 , radiolytic formation 
of oxidants is effectively suppressed and the corrosion 
potential remains close to its thermodynamic minimum 
(which is a function of temperature, H 2  fugacity, and pH). 
BWRs cannot achieve this H 2  level in the core because H 2  
partitions to the steam phase, which begins to form about 
a quarter of the way up the fuel rods. Thus, this section is 
primarily relevant to BWRs, although there is some thought to 
reducing H 2  in PWRs, which would be dangerous if radiolysis 
were to occur. 
 When considering the effect of radiolysis and oxidizing 
species, many sequential and non-linear dependencies must be 
considered, e.g., radiation ﬂ ux produces oxidizing and reduc-
ing species, the corrosion potential is controlled by multiple 
reactions involving these species, the crack chemistry is non-
linearly dependent on the corrosion potential and the aqueous 
ions present, and the SCC growth rate is a non-linear func-
tion of the crack chemistry (Andresen & Young, 1995; Ford 
& Andresen, 1994). The relationship between radiation ﬂ  ux 
and SCC cannot easily be determined empirically, but rather 
requires a fundamental understanding of each sub-process. 
 Ionizing radiation decomposes water into a variety of pri-
mary species (Burns & Moore, 1976; Cohen, 1969; Lin, 1986), 
including radicals (e.g., e aq  - , H, OH, HO 2 ) and molecules (e.g., 
H 2 O 2 , H 2 , O 2 ), which can be oxidizing (e.g., O 2 , H 2 O 2 , HO 2 ) or 
reducing (e.g., e aq  - , H, H 2 ). The predominant species that are 
stable after a few seconds are H 2 O 2  and H 2 , with O 2  forming 
primarily from the decomposition of H 2 O 2 . Because H 2  parti-
tions to the steam phase and H 2 O 2  is not volatile, the  ≈ 87 %  
of the water that is recirculated in a BWR ( ≈ 11 – 14 %  of the 
core ﬂ ow becomes steam) is oxidant rich. H 2  is introduced in 
the feed water, which mixes with the recirculated water near 
the top of the annulus (the region of down-ﬂ  ow between the 
core shroud and pressure vessel). So the annulus, recircula-
tion piping, lower plenum (below the core) and lower part of 
the core are H 2 -rich. 
 The concentrations of radiolytic species are roughly pro-
portional to the square root of the radiation ﬂ ux in pure water. 
The radiation energy-intensity spectrum inﬂ  uences the con-
centration of each radiolytic species, which is described in 
terms of a yield, or G value, which is the number of mol-
ecules produced per 100   eV absorbed by water. In LWRs, the 
G values for most species are within a factor of  ≈ 3 for fast 
neutron vs. gamma radiation; however, fast neutrons have a 
much bigger impact than gamma radiation primarily because 
the energy deposition rate, or mean linear energy transfer 
(LET), is greater [40   eV/nm for fast neutrons vs. 0.01   eV/nm 
for gamma radiation (Burns & Moore, 1976)]. Also, the neu-
tron ﬂ ux in LWRs (e.g.,  ≈ 1.03 × 10 9    Rad/h core average and 
 ≈ 1.68 × 10 9    Rad/h peak in a BWR4 of 51   W/cc power density) 
is also higher than the gamma ﬂ  ux ( ≈ 0.34 × 10 9    Rad/h). The 
contribution of thermal neutrons and beta particles to radioly-
sis in LWRs is small. 
 Gamma radiation in a certain ﬂ  ux range can be beneﬁ  -
cial. The intermediate gamma level in the downcomer of the 
annulus of a BWR core promotes recombination of hydrogen 
and various oxidants (Lin, 1986;  Proc. Water Chemistry of 
Nuclear Reactor Systems, No.5 , October 23-27, 1989). BWRs 
that have a wider annulus and lower gamma ﬂ  ux near the 
pressure vessel respond less to a given H 2  addition; this is a 
key element in the effectiveness of HWC. 
 The integrated effects of various oxidants and reductants 
on environmental cracking is best described via changes in 
corrosion potential, which controls the thermodynamics and 
inﬂ uences the kinetics of most reactions. Electrochemical 
potentials are logarithmically dependent on local oxidant, 
reductant and ionic concentrations via the Nernst relation-
ship,  ϕ  =  ϕ  o  + RT/nF ln (products/reactants), where  ϕ  is electro-
chemical potential, R is the gas constant, T is temperature in 
Kelvin, n is the number of moles and F is Faraday ’ s constant. 
Radiation may increase the concentration of various species 
by many orders of magnitude but have comparatively small 
effects on the corrosion potential in hot water. Further, cor-
rosion potentials are mixed potentials involving a balance 
of anodic and cathodic reactions on the metal surface, and 
radiolysis produces a stoichiometric balance of oxidizing and 
reducing species. At low oxidant concentrations, the rapid 
drop in corrosion potential to  ≈ -0.5   V she  (Figure  30  A) results 
from mass transport limited kinetics of oxidants to the metal 
surface.

et al. (1986) evaluated pre-irradiated stainless steels using

  4.2. Metallurgy and microstructure 
  4.2.1. RIS     RIS describes the redistribution of major alloying 
elements and impurity elements at point defect sinks (Allen, 
Busby, Was, & Kenik, 1998; Hindmarsh, 1972; Jenssen et 
al., 1998; Lam, Kumar, & Wiedersich, 1982; Okamoto & 
Rehn, 1979; Okamoto & Wiedersich, 1974; Perks, Marwick, 
& English, 1987; Simonen (personal communication); Was, 
2007; Was et al., 1999a; Wiedersich, Okamoto, & Lam, 1979). 
As IASCC is intergranular, the sinks of greatest interest are 
the grain boundaries. RIS is driven by the ﬂ  ux of radiation-
produced defects to sinks, and is therefore fundamentally 
different from thermal segregation or elemental depletion 
because of grain boundary precipitation. Vacancies and 
interstitials are the basic defects produced by irradiation 
and can reach concentrations that are orders of magnitude 
higher than those at thermal equilibrium, resulting in large 
increases in diffusion rates. If the relative participation of 
alloying elements in the defect ﬂ uxes is not the same as their 
relative concentration in the alloy, then a net transport of the 
constituents to or from the grain boundary will occur (Figure 
 31 ). This unequal participation of solutes in the vacancy and/
or interstitial ﬂ uxes to sinks results in either enrichment or 
depletion of an alloying element at the grain boundary. The 
species that diffuse more slowly by the vacancy diffusion 
mechanism are enriched and the faster diffusers become 
depleted. 
 Enrichment and depletion can also occur by association 
of the solute with the interstitial ﬂ ux. The undersized species 
will enrich and the oversized species will deplete (Okamoto 
& Wiedersich, 1974). The magnitude of the build-up/deple-
tion is dependent upon several factors, such as whether a 
constituent migrates more rapidly by one defect mechanism 
or another, the binding energy between solutes and defects, 
the dose, dose rate and the temperature. RIS proﬁ les are also 
characterized by their narrowness, often conﬁ  ned to within

 RIS increases with neutron dose in LWRs and saturates 
after several ( ∼ 5) dpa in the 300 ° C temperature range. Figure 
 34   shows grain boundary chromium depletion for austenitic 
stainless steels as a function of dose (Asano, Fukuya, Nakata, 
& Kodama, 1992; Carter et al., 1994; Jacobs, 1995; Jacobs, 
Clausing, Miller, & Shepherd, 1990; Jacobs et al., 1993; 
Kenik, 1992; Nakahigashi et al., 1992; Walmsley, Spellward, 
Fisher, & Jenssen, 1997; Was et al., 2002). As the slowest dif-
fusing element, nickel, becomes enriched at the grain bound-
ary. As iron depletes in 304 and 316 stainless steels, the nickel 
enrichment makes up for both chromium and iron depletion 
and can reach very high levels, up to  ∼ 30   wt % . 
 Minor alloying elements and impurities also segregate and 
have been implicated in the IASCC process. Manganese and 
molybdenum deplete at the grain boundary under irradiation 
(Bruemmer et al., 1999). Minor alloying or impurity elements 
such as silicon and phosphorous also segregate under irra-
diation. Silicon strongly enriches at the grain boundary to as 
much as 10   times the bulk (0.7 – 2.0   at % ) composition in the 
alloy (Bruemmer, 2002), and can be important in IASCC. 
Phosphorus is present at much lower concentrations and is 
only modestly enriched at the grain boundary as a result of 
irradiation (Bruemmer et al., 1999; Jenssen et al., 1998; Jiao 
& Was, 2011a). Phosphorus tends to segregate to the grain 
boundary following thermal treatment, which reduces the 
amount of additional segregation to the grain boundary dur-
ing irradiation making the contribution caused by irradiation 
difﬁ cult to detect (Bruemmer et al., 1999). Undersized solutes 
such as carbon, boron and nitrogen should also segregate, but 
there is little evidence of RIS, owing, in part, to the difﬁ culty 
of measurement. Another potential segregant is helium, pro-
duced by the transmutation of  10 B. The mobility of helium is 
low at LWR core temperatures, but the opportunity for accu-
mulation at the grain boundary is increased by segregation of 
boron to the boundary. Overall, the behavior of these minor 
elements under irradiation is not well understood.

 Segregation is a strong function of irradiation temperature, 
dose and dose rate (Figure  33  ). Segregation peaks at inter-
mediate temperatures since a lack of mobility suppresses the 
process at low temperatures, and back-diffusion of segregants 
minimize segregation at high temperature (where defect con-
centrations approach their thermal equilibrium values). For 
a given dose, a lower dose rate results in a greater amount 
of segregation. At high dose rates, the high defect population 
results in increased recombination that reduces the number of 
defects that are able to diffuse to the grain boundary. Figure 
 33  shows the interplay between temperature and dose rate for 
an austenitic stainless steel. RIS occurs in the intermediate 
temperature range, and this range rises along the temperature 
scale with increasing dose rate to compensate for the higher 
recombination rate. 
 In iron-chromium-nickel alloys, the vacancy exchange 
(inverse Kirkendall) mechanism successfully explains the 
observed major element segregation (Allen et al., 1998; Was 
et al., 1999b). Studies have shown that nickel segregates to 
grain boundaries while chromium and iron deplete. The direc-
tions of segregation are consistent with an atomic volume 
effect in which the subsized solute migrates preferentially 
with the interstitial ﬂ ux, and the oversized solute participates 
preferentially in the vacancy ﬂ  ux. The results are also con-

 Oversize solutes can affect the microchemistry or micro-
structure of the alloy, thereby altering the IASCC suscepti-
bility. They are believed to affect RIS by acting as vacancy 
traps, thereby increasing the effective recombination of vacan-
cies and interstitials and thus reducing RIS. Kato, Takahashi, 
and Izumiya (1992) conducted electron irradiations of several 
stainless steels at temperatures of 400 – 500 ° C up to 10   dpa. 
Results showed that some solutes (zirconium and hafnium) 
consistently produced a large suppression of radiation-induced 
chromium depletion while others resulted in less suppression 
or suppression at only certain temperatures. Fournier, Sencer, 
Was, Simonen, and Bruemmer (2003) conducted irradiation of 
316 containing hafnium or platinum using three MeV protons 
(400 ° C) and 5   MeV nickel ions (500 ° C). Nickel irradiations 
showed little effect of the oversize impurity in reducing grain 
boundary chromium depletion (chromium depletion increased 
in the case of hafnium), but proton irradiation showed a sig-
niﬁ cant suppression of RIS of chromium at low dose (2.5   dpa) 
with the effect diminishing at higher (5.0   dpa) dose. In zir-
conium-doped 304SS there were no consistent results of sup-
pression of grain boundary chromium after 3.2   MeV proton 
irradiation to 1.0   dpa at 400 ° C (Gan et al., 2004). Recent work 
involving neutron irradiations suggested that zirconium had no 
effect on RIS, even at 0.38   dpa (Allen, Was, Bruemmer, Gian, 
& Ukai, 2006). Hackett and Was conducted the most com-
prehensive study of oversized solutes and concluded that size 
was not the most important issue, but regardless, the beneﬁ  t 
was temporary, as higher doses resulted in the resumption of 
chromium depletion that was delayed at lower dose (Hackett, 
Busby, Miller, & Was, 2009a; Hackett, Najafabadi, & Was, 
2009). Titanium and niobium similarly produced little change 
in the grain boundary chromium concentration after irradiation 
with 3.2   MeV protons to 5.5   dpa at 360 ° C. Neutron irradiation 
at very low dose (0.5   dpa) shows a small effect of titanium 
and niobium on grain boundary chromium (Dumbill & Hanks, 
1993). In all, the data on the effect of oversize solutes on RIS 
of chromium is inconsistent.  
  4.2.2. Irradiated microstructure     The microstructure of 
austenitic stainless steels under irradiation changes rapidly 
at LWR service temperatures. Point defect clusters (called 
 “ black dot damage ”  when electron optics are unable to 
resolve the details) begin to form at very low dose, dislocation 
loops and network dislocation densities evolve with dose 
over several dpa, and the possibility exists for the formation 
and growth of helium-ﬁ  lled bubbles, voids and precipitates 
in core components in locations exposed to higher dose 
and temperature (Edwards, Simonen, & Bruemmer, 2003a; 
Edwards et al., 2002, 2003b; Garner, 1993; Maziasz, 1993; 
Maziasz & Mchargue, 1987; Zinkle, Maziasz, & Stoller, 
1993). Below 300 ° C, the microstructure is dominated by small 
clusters and dislocation loops. Near 300 ° C, the microstructure 
contains larger faulted loops plus network dislocations from 
loop unfaulting and cavities at higher doses. 
 The primary defect structures in LWRs are vacancy and 
interstitial clusters and Frank dislocation loops. The clusters 

===== PDF PAGE 24 =====
30  G.S. Was et al.: IASCC
are formed during the collapse of the damage cascade asso-
ciated with primary and secondary atom collisions after 
interaction with a high energy particle. The larger, faulted 
dislocation loops nucleate and grow as a result of the high 
mobility of interstitials. The loop population grows in size 
and number density until absorption of vacancies and inter-
stitials equalize, at which point the population has saturated. 
Figure  35   shows the evolution of loop density and loop size 
as a function of irradiation dose during LWR irradiation at 
280 ° C. Note that saturation of loop number density occurs 
very quickly, by  ∼ 1   dpa, while loop size continues to evolve 
up to  ∼ 5   dpa. The speciﬁ c number density and size are depen-
dent on irradiation conditions and alloying elements, but the 
loop size rarely exceeds 20   nm and densities are of the order 
of 1 × 10 23    m  -3 . 
 The hardening process and IASCC susceptibility are 
inﬂ uenced by small defects. The traditional view that small 
defect clusters are predominantly faulted interstitial loops and 
vacancy clusters (Stoller, 1996) may be inaccurate. Analysis 
of recent post-irradiation annealing experiments by Busby, 
Kenik, & Was (2005) suggests that there are at least two types 
of defects with different annealing characteristics; vacancy 
and interstitial faulted loops, each with different annealing 
kinetics. The step change in hardness as a function of anneal-
ing time suggests that the density of vacancy loops is perhaps 
much higher than previously believed, and higher than the 
density of interstitial loops (Busby, Sowa, Was, & Simonen, 
2005). 
 Above 300 ° C, voids and bubbles may begin to form, aided 
by the increased mobility of point defects at the higher tem-
perature. The dislocation structure will evolve into a network 
structure as larger Frank loops unfault. The reduction in 
the sink strength of the dislocation loops aids in the growth 
of voids and bubbles. While their size and number density 
increase with temperature, the dislocation microstructure 
continues to be the dominant microstructure component 
over the temperature range expected for LWR components 
( < 350 ° C). 
 Irradiation can also accelerate or retard the growth of sec-
ond phases, modify existing phases or produce new phases. 
In stainless steels, the principle second phase is chromium 
carbides, which are relatively stable under irradiation. A key 
factor in phase formation in austenitic stainless steels under 
LWR operating conditions is RIS, which can induce the 
formation of phases by exceeding the local solubility limit. 
Precipitation of  γ  ′  was observed in cold worked 316 stain-
less steel bafﬂ e bolts irradiated to 7   dpa at 299 ° C (Edwards, 
Simonen, & Bruemmer, 2007). Gamma prime is a coherent 
precipitate that can signiﬁ cantly strengthen the matrix and has 
the potential to alter the deformation behavior in the unirradi-
ated and irradiated conditions. Was et al. (Busby, Kenik, & 
Was, in press) irradiated a high purity stainless steel contain-
ing 1   wt %  silicon with 3.2   MeV protons to 5.5   dpa at 360 ° C, 
and observed the formation of  γ  ′  (Ni 3 Si) in the matrix. In addi-
tion to  γ  ′ , G phase (nickel/silicon rich) is frequently observed 
in irradiated stainless steel alloys (Edwards & Bruemmer, 
2008). In high strength nickel-base alloys, second phases can 
undergo several types of transformations:  γ  ′  can dissolve,  γ    
can dissolve and re-precipitate, and Laves phase can become 
amorphous. 
 Oversize solutes can also affect the irradiated microstruc-
ture by mechanisms similar to RIS. Proton and nickel ion 
irradiations show that the addition of hafnium to a 316SS 
base alloy increased loop density, decreased loop size and 
eliminated voids (Fournier et al., 2003). Platinum addition 
to 316SS resulted in no change in loop density and a small 
increase in loop size, but increased void size and density. 
The good agreement between proton and nickel ion irradia-
tion results indicates that the major effect of the oversized 
solute is not caused by the cascade (where there are large 
differences between proton and nickel ion irradiation), but 
rather is caused by the post-cascade defect partitioning to the 
microstructure evolution. Electron irradiation experiments by 
Watanabe, Muroga, and Yoshida (1996) and proton irradiation 
experiments by Was et al. (Busby et al., in press) showed that 
stainless steel with titanium additions had slightly lower dis-
location loop densities and larger sizes compared to the base 
alloy. Niobium addition only increased loop size. In contrast 
to the base alloy, neither the titanium- nor the niobium-doped 
alloys formed voids under the conditions tested. Zirconium 
addition to 304   SS resulted in reduced hardness, decreased 
loop density and no change in loop size in proton irradiation 
to 1.0   dpa at 400 ° C and compared to the base alloy (Hackett 
et al., 2009b). Zirconium-containing samples also had a lower 
void density with no change in void size compared with the 
base alloy.  
  4.2.3. Swelling and creep     Irradiation can also result 
in the creation of stress and enhanced plastic ﬂ  ow through 
processes such as swelling, stress relaxation and creep. While 
swelling is more prevalent at high doses ( > 30   dpa) and high 
temperatures ( > 350 ° C), there are locations in LWRs, such as 
the bafﬂ e bolt and bafﬂ e former plates, that experience high 
temperatures (from gamma heating) and high doses. Further, 
the lower dose rate compared with fast reactors enhances the 
nucleation of voids and the production of helium through 
transmutation reactions that can stabilize void embryos. 
Thus, swelling can occur in core internal components in 
LWRs. The result of swelling is an increase in volume of 
the component, which translates into an applied stress. For 
example, swelling of bafﬂ  e former plates bolted together 
with bafﬂ e former bolts will produce a tensile stress along 
the axis of the bolt. Circumferential cracking in bafﬂ e former 
bolts has been observed at the intersection of the head and 
shank, and the sources of stress may well be swelling of the 
bafﬂ e plates. 
 At LWR temperatures radiation creep results from dif-
fusion of the radiation-produced vacancies and interstitial 
atoms to dislocations, enhancing the climb-to-glide process 
that controls time-dependent deformation. Radiation creep 
can be both beneﬁ  cial and detrimental. Beneﬁ  ts accrue 
because of relaxation of constant displacement stresses, 
e.g., weld residual stress and in loaded bolts and springs. 
However, under these conditions  –  and more so under con-
stant load conditions  –  radiation creep also induces elevated 
creep rates, including grain boundary sliding, that may help

nitude of stress may be reduced by creep-induced stress 
relaxation, constraint in the polycrystal aggregate may result 
in high local stresses that could initiation a stress corrosion 
crack directly or cause large amounts of local grain boundary 
deformation that can rupture the oxide ﬁ  lm locally, creating 
conditions for SCC initiation.

 Radiation creep relaxation also affects PWR bafﬂ e bolts, 
which are subject to large variations in ﬂ uence and tempera-

niﬁ cant radiation creep relaxation occurs) or later in life when 
reloading occurs from differential swelling in the (annealed) 
bafﬂ e plates relative to the (cold worked) bafﬂ e bolts. 
 While difﬁ cult to prove, the elevated and sustained defor-
mation rates associated with radiation creep can also accentu-
ate susceptibility to SCC. Estimates of crack tip deformation 
rates (Andresen et al., 1990) indicate that radiation creep is 
not a large contributor in actively growing cracks, but rather 
it is expected to promote crack initiation and to sustain crack 
growth (or promote crack re-initiation, if an existing crack does 
arrest). It is obviously very important to factor radiation creep 
relaxation into initial component design and subsequent SCC 
analysis. Its impact is large, and occurs in the same ﬂ  uence 
range as RIS and radiation hardening.  
  4.2.4. Hydrogen and helium generation     Transmutation 
of various elements causes an increase in the helium content 
of irradiated stainless steel and nickel alloys. At low ﬂ uence, 
transmutation of boron produces helium ( 10 B + n →  6 Li +  4 He); 
the  10 B is consumed (burned out) within a few dpa. While B 
segregates strongly to grain boundaries, the (n, α ) reaction is 
energetic, and helium is distributed throughout a region of 
 ∼ 10    µ m around the grain boundary. A more proliﬁ c source of 
helium occurs at higher ﬂ uence associated with  58 Ni(n, γ ) 59 Ni 
and  59 Ni (n,p and n, α ) reactions. Because  59 Ni must be 
produced ﬁ rst, the production of helium from this reaction 
is limited until 5 – 10   dpa. There is no evidence that helium 
directly affects SCC behavior, but it can produce an increase 
in hardness and yield strength, and may affect fracture 
toughness. It is known to have a large impact on weld repairs 

===== PDF PAGE 26 =====
32  G.S. Was et al.: IASCC
because the thermal transient permits migration of helium 
and the formation of bubbles along the grain boundaries. This 
can be managed to a certain extent by using low heat input 
processes, such as laser welding. Helium also stabilized void 
embryos, resulting in a shortening of the nucleation stage 
of voids. This also reduces the dose to reach steady state 
(breakaway) swelling. Swelling can impact IASCC indirectly 
by causing isotropic strains, resulting in stresses in welded 
or bolted components, especially when swelling occurs at 
different rates in the fastened structures. 
 Hydrogen is also produced by transmutation and proton 
injection during radiolysis of water, but these are small con-
tributions in iron- and nickel-base alloys compared to the 
ﬂ ux of hydrogen that occurs from exposure to the coolant. 
Unlike helium, hydrogen is quite mobile, and readily per-
meates through the material, entering and leaving voids and 
other microstructural features. Figure  37   shows that the per-
meation rate in stainless steel is proportional to the square 
root of the coolant H 2  fugacity, and that H 2  inside a tube can 
dissociate and permeate back out of the tube into the coolant 
if the coolant H 2  level is reduced. Elevated hydrogen levels 
in metals are not evidence of extreme H fugacities in the 
metal, only that there are more storage or trapping sites as it 
permeates through the metal.

constant load provide well-behaved and accurate predic-
tion continuum across these different loading conditions 
(Figure  43  ). A more complex version has been proposed by 
Gao et al. (Gao & Hwang, 1981; Gao, Zhang, & Hwang, 
1983) for growing cracks in strain hardening materials and 
developed by (Shoji, 2003; Shoji, Lu, & Murakami, 2010) 
and Young et al. (2001). These formulations recognize 
that strain redistribution occurs during crack advance, and 
also addresses yield strength, strain hardening and other 
factors. While such formulations provide some basis for 
quantifying the important parameters that control crack tip 
strain rate, until veriﬁ  ed there is only a limited basis for 
conﬁ dence in their accuracy. This is especially true of for-
mulations based on continuum mechanics, when the crack 
tip deformation processes are in fact localized in space and 
time, and strongly affected by inhomogeneities in grain 
boundaries, relative grain orientation and slip systems. 
Estimations of the effect of thermal and irradiation creep 
(Andresen et al., 1990) suggest that their role during active 
crack advance is small, although their role in crack initia-
tion or in reactivating cracks that have stopped growing 
may be large.

  5.2. Grain boundary chromium depletion 
 Because of the similarity to thermally-induced sensitization 
for creating a chromium depletion zone adjacent to the grain 
boundary, chromium depletion arising from RIS has been 
implicated as a likely cause of IASCC, especially in oxi-
dizing environments. This is understandable because of the 
documented effect of thermal sensitization in extensive data 
from laboratory and plant operational experience (Andresen 
& Ford, 1995; Andresen et al., 1990; Brown & Gordon, 
1988; Bruemmer et al., 1999; Gordon & Brown, 1990; 
Nelson & Andresen, 1992; Scott, 1994; Was & Andresen, 
1992b, 2007; Was et al., 1999b). One distinct difference 
between chromium depletion because of RIS and that 
caused by chromium carbide precipitation is the width of 
the depleted zone. While RIS produces depleted zones that 
are typically 5 – 10   nm at half maximum, chromium deple-
tion because of chromium carbide precipitation produces 
depleted zones 100x that width, resulting in a much higher 
volume of affected material adjacent to the boundary. 
 Figure  44  A shows a correlation between grain boundary 
chromium level and IGSCC susceptibility in unirradiated 
stainless steels where the grain boundary depletion is caused 
by thermal sensitization (Bruemmer & Was, 1994). Much 
data have been accumulated to support the role of chromium 
depletion as an agent in IGSCC of austenitic alloys in oxi-
dizing conditions. Numerous studies show that as the grain 
boundary chromium level decreases, intergranular SCC 
increases. As shown in Figure  34 , grain boundary chromium 
depletion during irradiation can be severe. 
 Figure  44 B shows a similar correlation between grain 
boundary chromium level and IASCC susceptibility, as mea-
sured by the  %  IG cracking on the fracture surface during 
SSRT experiments. There is also a signiﬁ  cant amount of 
scatter in the data of Figure  44 B that makes a direct correla-
tion difﬁ cult to support. In particular, at low grain boundary 
chromium levels, the  %  IG ranges from 0 to 100. However, 
as the grain boundary chromium content increases, the data 
becomes less  “ noisy ” , suggesting that a higher grain bound-
ary chromium concentration is a strong indicator of resistance 
to IASCC. Also, there is a trend in the maximum level of  %  
IG with grain boundary chromium content. Further, the nature 
of the water chemistry appears to play a large role in whether 
chromium depletion correlates with cracking. Fukuya, 
Nakano, Fujii, and Torimaru (2004b) measured chromium 
depletion in 304L, 316 and 316L after irradiation to 0.8   dpa at 
400 ° C, and then assessed SCC susceptibility via SSRT tests 
in pure water containing either dissolved hydrogen (2.8   ppm) 
at 320 ° C or dissolved oxygen (3   ppb or 32   ppm) at 288 ° C. 
Chromium depletion was greatest for 304L, followed by 
316L and then 316. Cracking was greatest for 304L 32   ppm 
DO water, for 316L in 3   ppm DO water, and in 2.8   ppm DH, 
none of the samples exhibited cracking. These data indicate 
that chromium depletion is not likely a major factor in IASCC 
in reducing environments. 
 In fact, even in oxidizing environments, the data do not 
clearly support chromium depletion as the major cause of 
IASCC. Busby, Was, and Kenik (2002) showed that the dif-
ferent rates at which RIS and dislocation loops anneal during 
a post-irradiation heat treatment creates a window in time-
temperature space in which annealing of the dislocation loop 
structure can occur to a great extent with little change in the 
grain boundary composition proﬁ le (Figure  45  ). Several stud-
ies have been conducted on the post-irradiation annealing of 
irradiated stainless steels and the assessment of the IASCC 
susceptibility as a function of annealing time and temperature

1992; Katsura et al., 1998), and all show that the grain bound-

well beyond the point where the IASCC susceptibility has been 
substantially reduced. This occurrence is again an indication 
that while RIS may be an important factor in IASCC, it does 
not alone explain IASCC. Hence, grain boundary chromium

  5.3. Irradiation hardening 
 The dislocation loop microstructure formed during irradiation 
hardens the alloy, which correlates with increased SCC sus-
ceptibility. Under applied stress, the dislocation network inter-
acts elastically with dislocation loops, producing an increase 
in the yield strength of the alloy, which can be detected in 
tensile tests or indentation hardness measurements. This is 
accompanied by a decrease in elongation, which is affected 
more than reduction in area because necking occurs relatively 
early in most tensile tests of irradiated stainless steel. Fracture 
toughness also decreases with ﬂ uence. 
 The increase in yield strength with a dose in 300 series 
stainless steels irradiated around 300 ° C is shown in Figure 
 46  . The yield strength can reach values up to ﬁ ve times the 
unirradiated value by about 5   dpa, and its increase follows a 
square root dependence on dose. Both the source hardening 
model (Singh, Foreman, & Trinkaus, 1997) and the dispersed 
barrier hardening model (Seeger, 1958) provide reasonable 
correlations between hardening and the dislocation loop 
microstructure. In the dispersed barrier hardening model, 
the increase in hardness is proportional to (N loop xd loop ) 1/2 , 
where N loop  is the loop number density and d loop  is the loop 
diameter.

  5.4. Localized deformation 
 Deformation changes dramatically with radiation. Homo-
geneous deformation at low dose is replaced by heteroge-
neous deformation at higher doses as the defect microstructure 
begins to impede the motion of dislocations. Plasticity 
becomes localized to narrow channels that have been cleared 
of defects by preceding dislocations, providing a preferred 
path for subsequent dislocation motion. The channels are very 
narrow ( < 100   nm) and closely spaced ( < 1    µ m) and typically 
run the full length of a grain, terminating at the grain boundar-
ies (Bruemmer et al., 1993). Dislocation channeling can cause 
localized necking and a sharp reduction in uniform elongation 
(Bloom, 1975).

 Microstructure can also inﬂ uence the deformation mode. 
Farrell, Byun, and Hashimoto (2002) conducted neutron irra-
diation of 316 stainless steel at temperatures between 65 and 
100 ° C to doses of  < 1   dpa, then characterized the deformation 
behavior. With increasing dose, the propensity for disloca-
tion channeling increased. The volume of material occupied 
by channels increases rapidly with dose and saturates at 
 < 0.5   dpa. These data show that the defect microstructure cre-

of narrow dislocation channels. 
 The importance of slip localization in IASCC may be in the 
way in which the dislocations interact with the grain bound-
ary. In planar slip, well deﬁ  ned and separated slip bands or 
dislocation channels (for irradiated materials) transmit dislo-
cations during plastic deformation. These slip bands or chan-
nels terminate at grain boundaries where dislocations are fed 
into the grain boundary region vs. forming a tangled disloca-
tion network within the grain. In the more traditional view 
of dislocation infusion into grain boundaries, the pile-up of 
dislocations in the intersecting channel creates progressively 
higher stresses at the grain boundary at the head of the pileup. 
If the stress exceeds a critical value, separation of the grain 
boundary could occur according to a Stroh (wedge) crack-
ing type of mechanism (Stroh, 1957). This cracking process 
could occur regardless of the environment and may in fact 
be the mechanism that occurs in some of the IG cracking 
observed in very highly irradiated steels in inert environment 
(Bloom, 1975). At lower ﬂ  uences, the stress at the grain 
boundary may promote rupture of the oxide ﬁ  lm, leading to 
exposure of the metal to the solution and subsequent corro-
sion and IASCC. 
 Alternatively, deformation could occur in the bound-
ary plane, which would rupture the oxide ﬁ  lm and promote 
IASCC. Alexandreanu and Was (2003) showed that dislo-
cation absorption by grain boundaries in nickel-base alloys 
leads to deformation in the grain boundary, usually by bound-
ary sliding, and promotes IGSCC. They observed that ran-
dom grain boundaries exhibited more sliding, and boundaries 
where sliding occurred were four times more susceptible to 
IGSCC in primary water at 360 ° C than boundaries that did 
not slide. This same process could explain how dislocation 
injection into grain boundaries by planar slip or dislocation 
channeling can result in IASCC. 
 The concept that slip planarity controls the deformation 
mode and IASCC is consistent with observations of IASCC 
in 304   SS and 316   SS. 316   SS has, in some cases (Jenssen 
et al., 1998) been shown to be more resistant to IASCC 
than is 304   SS, which is consistent with its higher SFE. 
Observations by Bailat et al. (2000) on neutron irradiated 
samples and by Busby, Sowa, and Was (2004) on proton 
irradiated samples show clear dislocation channel patterns 
on the surface of 304   SS samples that cracked in SSRT tests, 
but an absence of those patterns on the 316   SS samples that 
did not crack. In addition, SSRT tests on high nickel alloys 
(with SFE of  ∼ 40   mJ/m 2 ) showed no IG cracking and also no 
dislocation channeling. Further support for the inﬂ  uence of 
slip planarity on IASCC comes from the annealing studies 
cited earlier. On samples that underwent either a short, low 
temperature anneal, or were tested in the as-irradiated con-
dition, IG cracking was accompanied by a high density of 
dislocation slip bands on the surface of  ∼ 65/mm 2 . Annealing 
at 500 ° C for 45   min. resulted in the elimination of IG crack-
ing and a reduction in the surface slip band density to  ∼ 25/
mm 2 . 
 In the annealing experiments described earlier, hardening 
by irradiation was much more effective in initiating IGSCC 
than was cold work, where the dislocation structure will likely 
be more cellular than planar. Thomas and Bruemmer (1999) 
showed a correlation between slip band intersection with the 
walls of a growing crack and the accompanying steps on the 
oxide on the walls. Figure  49  A shows a TEM micrograph of 
SCC in a cold worked 316 stainless steel bafﬂ e bolt. The slip 
bands intersecting the walls of the narrow crack at 45 °  to the 
crack growth direction. The slip band- crack wall intersec-
tions are also coincident with steps in the oxide as shown 
schematically in Figure  49 B. This suggests that the ﬂ ow of 
dislocations along the slip steps and into the grain boundary 
may have been responsible for discontinuous growth of the 
crack along the grain boundary.

unit area. SFE, hardness, RIS and localized deformation were 
characterized and the strengths of their correlations with crack-

were both around 0.5, indicating that both contributed to crack-

in this study. The correlation strength of localized deformation,

  6. IASCC-resistant alloys 
 The fundamental result of irradiation that leads to   all   of the 
changes in microstructure (RIS, loops, voids, precipitates), 
that then lead to   all   of the changes in materials properties 
(hardening, embrittlement, IG corrosion, IG cracking, creep, 
growth, swelling, etc.) is the production of vacancies and 
interstitials in excess of their thermodynamic equilibrium 
values. The strategy to eliminate or minimize the effect of 
irradiation is then quite simple: reduce the vacancy and inter-
stitial concentrations in the solid. 
 Vacancy and interstitial concentrations are determined by 
the reactions responsible for their creation and annihilation. 
Vacancies and interstitials are created by the impingement 
of neutrons on the lattice and cannot be avoided in a reac-
tor environment. They are destroyed by either mutual anni-
hilation or by diffusing to sinks where they are lost from the 
matrix. Therefore, the strategy is to develop materials that 
greatly enhance mutual annihilation and loss to sinks. 
 Annihilation occurs when vacancies and interstitials 
encounter each other. The rate of loss by mutual annihilation 
is governed by the concentrations of the defects with higher 
concentrations leading to greater annihilation rates. However, 
the concentrations at which annihilation becomes effective is 
already far above the equilibrium values, so this is not a sen-
sible strategy. Annihilation can be  “ assisted ”  or  “ augmented ”  
by providing sites at which defects become trapped until their 
complement arrives and annihilation can occur. Such sites are 
coherent precipitates and oversized solutes. 
 Increasing the trap density is the obvious path for keep-
ing defect concentrations low. The trap density must be high 
enough such that the distance between traps is small com-
pared to that between defects, which will prevent defects 
from building up to high levels. Strategies to accomplish this 
include providing interfaces or providing other sinks such as 
dislocations. This is the idea behind ODS alloys, in which 
the very high density of closely spaced, nm-sized ppts pro-
vides enormous surface area per unit volume to facilitate 
defect annihilation, preventing the build-up of defects to high 
levels. Cold work serves the same purpose but is not stable 
at high doses. Grain boundaries can perform the same func-
tion but would require grain sizes on the nm-scale, resulting 
in such high levels of internal energy that the microstructure 
would likely be unstable against recrystallization at high 
temperature. 
 IASCC resistance can thus be achieved by eliminating 
the vacancy and interstitial super-saturation as a result of 
irradiation. In considering what an IASCC-resistant alloy 
may look like, the following characteristics are noted. While 
not complete, the following is intended to provide a starting 
point in thinking about the attributes of an alloy that is resis-
tant to IASCC. 
  6.1. High nickel content 
 Experience with high nickel alloys, such as alloy 800 indicate 
that higher nickel content is effective in reducing IASCC sus-
ceptibility. The mechanism by which nickel imparts increased 
resistance to IASCC is not well understood, but it could be 
linked, in part, to an increase in stacking fault energy. High 
stacking fault energy produces wavy slip, which could be 
important in avoiding high levels of localized deforma-
tion, which, as shown in section 5.4, contributes to IASCC 
susceptibility.  
  6.2. High chromium + nickel content 
 While not all IASCC results can be explained by chromium 
depletion at grain boundaries because of RIS, there is a cor-
relation in oxidizing environments. Therefore, a higher chro-
mium content will make it more difﬁ cult for IASCC to occur 
as the grain boundary chromium level will not fall as much 
because of RIS and, presumably, remain above some criti-
cal level. However, higher chromium contents in nickel base 
alloy 690 vs. 600 has been shown to provide signiﬁ  cantly 
higher resistance to IGSCC in PWR environments as well, in 
which the potential is very low. Thus, high chromium + nickel 
has the potential for further increases in IASCC resistance 
above either addition alone.  
  6.3. Low silicon 
 As discussed earlier, there is growing evidence that high sili-
con content is detrimental to both crack initiation and crack 
growth in irradiated alloys in water environments. The forma-
tion of  γ  ′  or G-phase may be a part of the detrimental effect of 
silicon as these precipitates could serve to increase hardness 
and enhance localized deformation, both of which are detri-
mental to IASCC resistance. Thus, keeping the silicon content 
low in prototype alloys should reduce IASCC susceptibility.  
  6.4. High CSL fraction (grain boundary engineering) 
 Data on the effect of grain boundary engineering is mixed. 
As opposed to just increasing the number or fraction of spe-
cial boundaries, it is probably more important to break up the 
high angle grain boundary (HAB) network. However, both 
phenomena generally occur during grain boundary engineer-
ing. There exists a sizeable set of data indicating that altera-
tion of the grain boundary structure in a way that replaces 
HABs with CSLs and breaks up the connectivity of the HABs 
is quite beneﬁ cial for IG corrosion, and less so for IGSCC 
(Gertsman & Bruemmer, 2001; Lehockey, Palumbo, & Lin, 
1998; Palumbo, King, & Aust, 1991; Pan, Adams, Olson, & 
Panayotou, 1996). The application of a stress also brings in

other factors, such as the orientation of the boundary with 
respect to the tensile stress and, thus, the resolved shear stress 
(and normal stress) on that boundary. Nevertheless, while the 
improvement may be small, it is unlikely to be detrimental 
and, if additive with other measures, it could contribute to 
improved IASCC resistance.  
  6.5. Low inclusion density 
 Studies by both Cookson and Was (1998) and Chung, Ruther, 
Sanecki, and Kassner (1992) showed that oxide inclusions 
were excellent sites for nucleation of IASCC cracks. IASCC 
is promoted by the fracture of brittle oxides that intersect 
grain boundaries, leading to crack extension along the grain 
boundary. Hard carbide particles such as TiC or TiN can 
also contribute in a similar way. Thus, it is clear that avoid-
ing hard, brittle inclusions will be a key feature of an IASCC 
resistant alloy.  
  6.6. Grain boundary carbides 
 Grain boundary carbides have been shown to be an effec-
tive means of reducing cracking in nickel-base alloys by 
what is generally agreed upon as a  “ mechanical ”  mechanism 
(Alexandreanu, Capell, & Was, 2001; Norring, Engstrom, 
& Norberg, 1988). Grain boundary carbides have also 
been shown to homogenize slip (Bruemmer & Henager Jr., 
1986). So, both by improving the mechanical response of 
grain boundaries, as well as by homogenizing deformation, 
grain boundary carbides should positively impact IASCC 
resistance.  
  6.7 . Other microstructure features 
 There are other changes to the microstructure that could be 
made to affect IASCC, but neither the direction nor mag-
nitude of the effect is well enough understood. Grain size 
is one such factor, cold work is another and the addition of 
titanium is a third. Deﬁ  nitive data on grain size effects are 
hampered by the difﬁ  culty in controlling just that one fea-
ture. Cold work appears to increase crack growth rate, but 
its effect on crack initiation is not well understood and good 
data is lacking. Finally, while titanium addition is helpful 
in preventing weld sensitization, experiments in the CIR 
program (Scott, 2010) showed that the crack growth rate 
of cold-worked 316 containing titanium was signiﬁ  cantly 
higher than that without titanium. Therefore, there is no 
clear advantage of adding titanium to cold worked stainless 
steel.  
  6.8. Ferritic or Ferritic-Martensitic steels 
 Ferrritic-Martensitic (F-M) steels are very attractive for use 
in fast reactor and fusion reactor systems for several reasons, 
including excellent dimensional stability (low swelling), high 
strength, high thermal conductivity, and low neutron activa-
tion making repairs less costly. Relative to LWR conditions, 
F-M steels exhibit high resistance to stress corrosion cracking, 
but suffer from higher general corrosion rates and radiation 
embrittlement manifest in an increase in the ductile-to-brittle 
temperature. For high dose applications in LWRs, the key 
challenges for austenitic steels are IASCC and swelling, both 
of which are advantages of F-M steels. Hence, the challenge 
of F-M steels for LWR applications is whether the corrosion 
rates are low enough to be acceptable for water chemistry 
control, and whether radiation embrittlement is an issue for 
the operating temperatures of LWR cores.   
  7 . Summary 
 Cracking of in-core reactor components exposed to both irra-
diation and high temperature water indicate that there is a sig-
niﬁ cant role of irradiation in intergranular SCC. The effects of 
irradiation on SCC occur through changes in the water chem-
istry and in the alloy microstructure. It is the  “ persistent ”  
radiation effects on the microstructure that are responsible for 
the  “ threshold-like ”  behavior of IASCC. The principal effect 
of irradiation on water chemistry is through radiolysis, which 
forms a variety of oxidizing and reducing molecules and radi-
cals. Their net effect is captured by the change in corrosion 
potential, which can be considerable when the dissolved H 2  
level is below  ∼ 500   ppb. High corrosion potentials strongly 
correlate with severity of cracking in both laboratory tests of 
pre-irradiated specimens and in core components in operating 
power reactors. 
 Irradiation causes a significant change in local com-
position near grain boundaries and other defect sinks. 
The enrichment of nickel and silicon, and the depletion 
of chromium can affect the susceptibility to IASCC, 
especially under oxidizing conditions. Irradiation also 
alters the microstructure, and under LWR conditions, 
faulted dislocation loops represent the primary irradia-
tion-induced microstructure defect. The loops impede 
the motion of dislocations, resulting in an increase in the 
yield strength by factor of up to five. Radiation harden-
ing correlates with IASCC propensity, and also induces 
highly localized deformation in the form of dislocation 
channels, which could contribute to IASCC. Irradiation 
also induces creep that can relax macroscopic stresses, 
and can also enhance local dynamic deformation. Other 
factors, such as swelling and formation of new phases, 
may accentuate IASCC at high fluence. With the many 
effects of irradiation, which overlap spatially and tem-
porally, more work is needed to identify their roles in the 
mechanism(s) of IASCC and develop a comprehensive 
prediction methodology. 
 In considering the attributes of an IASCC-resistant alloy, 
the following are likely beneﬁ cial: high nickel and chromium 
contents, low silicon content, absence of brittle oxide and 
nitride inclusions, high CSL fraction, low connectivity of high 
angle grain boundaries and grain boundary coverage by chro-
mium carbides. Finally, ferritic or ferritic-martensitic alloys 
should be considered because of their inherently greater resis-
tance to radiation effects and IGSCC compared to iron- and 
nickel-base austenitic alloys.
