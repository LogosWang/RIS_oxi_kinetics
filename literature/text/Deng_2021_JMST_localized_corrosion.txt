1. Introduction
The austenitic stainless steel (SS) is widely used in the pri-
mary circuit of pressurized water reactors (PWRs) for fabrication
of core structural components. The steel, though typically corro-
sion resistant, was found by both laboratory experiments and plant
experiences to be susceptible to irradiation-assisted stress corro-
sion cracking (IASCC) in primary PWR water [1–4]. Due to the
radioactivity of neutrons, the effect of irradiation on materials is
usually studied by using simulation irradition such as protons and
Fe3+/ Fe2+. To date, a number of studies have been carried out to clar-
ify the mechanism of IASCC of the austenitic SS [5–15]. Lear et al.
compared the IASCC behavior of 13 alloys in two environments
and suggested that the irradiation microstructure and irradiation
hardening are very necessary for IASCC [14]. Miura et al. focused
on the role of grain orientation on cracking formation in irradiated
SS, which indicated pure mechanical strength can lead to crack-
ing [15]. The deformation structures in the irradiated steel were

before the base metal cracking by promoting localized grain bound-

the high temperature water and subsequent corrosion and IASCC
[7,16–19]. These investigations indicated that the change in defor-

grain boundary were key factors promoting the IASCC.
Similar to stress corrosion cracking (SCC), IASCC is also a syn-
ergic effect of an aggressive environment, a susceptible material
and the applied stress/strain. Since corrosion is one of the key fac-
tors affecting the SCC [20,21], a better understanding of the role
of corrosion in IASCC is needed in addition to the role of the local-
ized deformation. This requires at ﬁrst to understand whether and
how the irradiation affects the corrosion. In fact, the promotional
effect of irradiation on corrosion has been conﬁrmed [22–24]. It
was found that irradiation promoted both the overall and inter-
granular corrosion of austenitic SS in primary PWR water, which

austenitic alloys in high temperature water by the enhanced diffu-

usually initiates locally at grain boundary regions with which the
slip steps interact [5–7,17–19], the localized corrosion at the grain
boundary region may also be affected by the localized deformation.
This suggests that to fully clarify the mechanism of the promo-
tion effect of localized deformation on IASCC, the synergic effect
between the corrosion and localized deformation needs to be clar-

regions as the slip step and grain boundary. To date, the corro-
sion at slip step and grain boundary as well as their correlation
with IASCC has not received much attention. While one study con-
cluded that no penetrative oxidation was observed along the slip
step induced by pre-straining in air, the synergic effect between
synchronized deformation and corrosion was not considered [31].
In the actual process of SCC, corrosion occurs simultaneously with
localized deformation in the vicinity of the crack tip [32,33], sug-
gesting that the promoted corrosion at the slip step may occur in
such a case.
This study focused on the synergic effect of localized defor-
mation and corrosion in irradiated 304 nuclear grade (NG) SS in
simulated primary water of PWR. The objective is to correlate
the localized deformation to the corrosion at the slip step and
grain boundary, as well as the localized deformation and corro-
sion to IASCC. The specimens irradiated to various doses by proton
were strained by constant extension rate tensile (CERT) at a ﬁxed
strain rate of 3 × 107/s in simulated primary PWR water, in efforts
to investigate the localized corrosion and IASCC behavior during
straining. Following the CERT test, characterization of the local-
ized deformation, corrosion and IASCC behaviors was conducted by
using atomic force microscopy (AFM), electron backscatter diffrac-
tion (EBSD), transmission electron microscope (TEM) and scanning
electron microscope (SEM).
2. Experimental
2.1. Material and specimens
The material used was solution annealed (SA) 304NG SS with
a chemical composition listed in Table 1. Average grain size of
the material was in the range of about 2050 /H9262m. Small-sized ten-
sile specimens with the geometry and dimension shown in Fig. 1
were fabricated from the steel and used for the IASCC tests. Each
specimen with two threaded ends had a 2 mm × 2 mm squared
gauge section with a gauge length of 21 mm. The specimen was
wet ground using SiC papers up to 4000 grit, followed by 1-/H9262m
diamond paste polishing to obtain a mirror surface. Then the speci-
men was exposed to 40-nm colloidal silica slurry polishing for over
2 h to produce a surface free of residual strain prior to proton irra-
diation. At last, all specimens were irradiated by protons of 2 MeV
at 360 ◦C using the 3 MV pelletron accelerator at the Michigan Ion
Beam Laboratory, to a dose of 0.5, 1.5 and 5 displacement per atom
(dpa), respectively. It is worth noting that the dose here was calcu-
lated with full cascade option. The proton beam was raster-scanned
over a 10 mm length in the gauge section of the specimens, creat-
ing a nearly uniform damage proﬁle within the ∼1 to ∼10 /H9262m of
the whole 20 /H9262m-thick damaged layer [22]. The dose rate as cal-
culated by using the Stopping and Range of Ions in Materials 2008
(SRIM 2008) computer code was about 5.96 × 106 dpa per second
(dpa/s). Detailed description of the irradiation process is available
elsewhere [6,34].
Following the irradiation, the irradiated specimens were pol-
ished again by 40-nm colloidal silica slurry for about 1 h to get fresh
surfaces, which removed the surface with a thickness of 12 /H9262m and
could ensure that the specimen surfaces following diff ;erent doses
of irradiation were consistent.
2.2. High temperature water test
A low ﬂow rate (∼100 mL/min) refreshed loop equipped with
a 3-L autoclave made of 316 SS was used for the IASCC tests
in simulated primary PWR water at 320 ◦C and 13 MPa. The
ﬂow and pressure of water were maintained using a commercial
high-pressure pump in connection with a backpressure valve. Tem-
perature in the autoclave was achieved by one pre-heater at the
autoclave inlet and two resistance heating bands at the autoclave
surface, which was controlled by the k-type thermocouple with
an accuracy of ±1 ◦C. The primary water was prepared by mixing
deionized water with 1200 mg/L of B as H3BO3 and 2.3 mg/L of Li
as LiOH·H2O. Before ramping the autoclave to target temperature,
concentration of dissolved oxygen (DO) in the inlet water was con-
trolled at less than 5 /H9262g/L by bubbling H2 gas into the water tank,
followed by storing H2 in the tank with a hydrogen overpressure
of 0.08 MPa, resulting in a dissolved hydrogen (DH) concentra-
tion of 2.6 mg/L. DO, DH and conductivity of the inlet water were
continuously monitored during the tests.
The 0.5-, 1.5- and 5-dpa irradiated specimens as well as the SA
specimen with no irradiation were employed for the IASCC tests.
These specimens were strained by CERT at a ﬁxed strain rate of 3 ×
107/s in the primary water using a 50 kN servo motor in the con-
stant displacement rate mode. The servo motor was provided by
Interactive Instruments Inc. The test was interrupted at the strain
of 1% and 3% for all the specimens in an effort to study the effect
of applied strain on IASCC initiation behavior. Prior to CERT, the
specimens were exposed in the environment at a small load of
approximate 50 N for a period of three days to stabilize the environ-
mental condition. Throughout the test, the load, displacement and
environmental parameters were collected and recorded in every
thirty seconds using a personal computer equipped with a LabView
software. At each interrupted strain, the specimens were removed
from the autoclave for a characterization of the deformation and
IASCC behavior on the irradiated region of the specimens. Following
the interruption, the specimens were installed into the autoclave
again and the test was continued to next targeted strain with the
same condition as that employed before the interruption.
To correlate the localized corrosion with the IASCC, following
the strain of 3%, the oxide scale formed at grain boundary and slip
step in the SA and irradiated specimens was characterized to clarify
the localized corrosion behavior. Detailed description of the charac-

2.4. Analyses of the corrosion behavior
Following the strain of 3%, cross-sections of the oxide scale
formed on the specimens were characterized using a JEM-2100
TEM. The TEM was equipped with a Gatan 4k charge coupled device
camera for capturing high-resolution TEM images, and an energy
dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) device for the composition
analysis using both point- and line-scans. The high-resolution TEM
images collected were analyzed using the Fast Fourier Transform
(FFT) technique for crystallographic details of the oxide scale. The
TEM samples were prepared using a dual-beam focus ion beam
(FIB)-SEM technique with a Quanta 200 3D system, so that precise
sampling of the cross-section of a selected slip step and/or grain
boundary can be attained, allowing the observation and analysis
of the corrosion at the slip step and grain boundary. The high-
angle grain boundaries (HAGBs) were of interest to select, which
ensured by using ion channeling contrast of the grains [36]. Before
the TEM analysis, the selected grain boundaries were identiﬁed
again by the electron diffraction pattern of TEM to ensure that
they were HAGBs. A total of thirteen grain boundaries and thir-
teen slip steps were analyzed, including three grain boundaries
for the SA specimens, four grain boundaries and six slip steps
for the 0.5-dpa irradiated specimens, and six grain boundaries
and seven slip steps for the 5-dpa irradiated specimens. In addi-
tion, depth of the oxide formed at the grain boundary in strained
and strain-free specimens was compared to correlate localized
deformation to the intergranular corrosion. The data of the oxide
depth for strain-free specimens was available in a previous study
[22].

3.1.2. Localized residual strain at grain boundary and slip step
The typical SEM morphology and color-coded ML distribution
mappings of the 0.5- and 5-dpa irradiated specimens following the
3% straining in the primary water are shown in Fig. 4. As illustrated,
the residual strain developed heterogeneously in the irradiated
steel due to the applied strain. In particular, the strain tended to
accumulate to slip steps and grain boundaries, suggesting a strain
concentration at these regions. The level of residual strain concen-
tration at the grain boundary was increased by the irradiation dose
(Fig. 4(a) and (b)). In addition, the 5-dpa irradiated specimen also
exhibited a higher strain concentration level than the 0.5-dpa irra-
diated specimen at the slip step. This implies a correlation between
the strain concentration and the height of the slip step, which is in
agreement with the AFM analysis.
Fig. 4(c) shows the statistical distribution of the local misori-
entations with a regression curve optimized using the log-normal
distribution. The ML distribution is seen to be well represented by
the log-normal distribution, while the ML for frequency peak in 5-
dpa irradiated specimen was higher than that in 0.5-dpa irradiated
specimen. This suggested that increasing irradiation dose increased
the mean value of ML and thus the strain concentration at the slip
step and grain boundary.

The morphology and microstructure of the oxide formed at the
grain boundary in SA, 0.5- and 5-dpa irradiated specimens follow-

of intergranular corrosion. While the morphology of the oxide at
the grain boundary was similar, the depth of the oxide showed
difference in the specimens. In SA specimen, the oxide depth
was approximately 60 nm, shown in Fig. 8(a). The oxide depth
in 0.5- and 5-dpa irradiated specimens, however, was increased

EDX mappings and line-scans shown in Fig. 8(d)-(i) revealed the
composition proﬁles of the oxide formed at the grain boundaries
in SA, 0.5- and 5-dpa irradiated specimens. While all the oxides

was decreased by a higher irradiation dose of the specimen. This
is attributed to the depletion of Cr at the primary grain boundary
by the radiation induced segregation (RIS), as has been discussed
in a previous study [22].

4. Discussion
4.1. Effect of irradiation on localized deformation of 304NG SS in
primary PWR water
The above description of AFM and EBSD analysis showed that
the height of slip steps, as well as the strain concentration at the
slip step and grain boundary in the 5-dpa irradiated specimen was
larger than those in the 0.5-dpa irradiated specimen. These results
indicated that increasing irradiation dose led to a higher level of
localized deformation at slip step and grain boundary in 304NG
SS.
The effect of irradiation on localized deformation has been rec-
ognized in multiple irradiated austenitic alloys [6–8]. The increase
in slip step height for irradiated 304NG SS with irradiation dose
is further conﬁrmed in this study as shown in Fig. 3. At a higher
irradiation dose, more dislocations induced by the irradiation are
accumulated to mitigate homogeneous deformation. As such more
deformation was localized into the dislocation channel, leading to
a higher slip step height [7,41,42]. On the other hand, since the
slip step transmits or terminates at the grain boundary as shown
in Fig. 10, it will eventually impact on the grain boundary and pro-
mote localized grain boundary deformation [7,10,12]. As a result,
the localized deformation in 5-dpa irradiated specimen is larger
than that in 0.5-dpa irradiated specimen because a higher slip step
is likely to have a stronger impact on the grain boundary, leading
to a higher level of strain concentration at the grain boundary as
illustrated in Fig. 4.

4.2. Effect of localized deformation on localized corrosion of
304NG SS
The
 TEM observation and
 analysis shown in Figs. 5 and 8
revealed that increasing irradiation dose promoted localized cor-
rosion along the
 slip step and grain boundary. Further, as discussed
above, increasing the irradiation dose also led to a higher localized
deformation at the slip step and grain boundary. This indicated a
correlation between the enhanced corrosion and localized defor-
mation.
The higher slip step corresponds to a highly deformed
microstructure, in which enhanced corrosion could occur easily
by the process of stress/strain assisted oxidation [30,43]. This is
in accordance with the enhanced localized corrosion along defor-
mation band in cold worked steel [25,26]. Further, according to the
high resolution TEM analysis shown in Fig. 5(g) and (h), the grain
size of the oxide formed at the slip step is much smaller than that
of the inner oxide. This also suggested a higher localized corrosion
rate along the slip step, since the ﬁner grain of the oxide could
certainly promote the oxygen diffusion by providing more diffu-
sion paths [44–46]. The formation of polycrystalline oxide could
relate to the continuing changing of step height during deforma-
tion and therefore induce breaking of the oxide and formation of
small oxides.
The above discussion suggests that the localized deformation
assisted diffusion of oxygen is the primary cause for enhanced local-
ized corrosion along the slip step. The deformation, on the other
hand, also promotes the intergranular corrosion by means of strain
concentration at the grain boundary. During the exposure in the
primary water, a homogeneous and ﬂat oxide was formed at ﬁrst
on
 the surfaces of the 0.5- and 5-dpa irradiated specimens. Then
enhanced intergranular corrosion occurred due to Cr-depletion
induced by RIS at the grain boundary, as has been acknowledged
in a previous study [22]. In the following process of straining, the
intergranular corrosion is related to the combined effect of RIS and
strain concentration at the grain boundary. Further, the strain con-
centration at the grain boundary in 5-dpa irradiated specimen is
heavier than that in 0.5-dpa irradiated specimen, as discussed pre-
viously in section 4.1. As concluded in other studies [26,30,43,47],
strain concentration could accelerate the oxygen diffusion along
grain boundaries, causing a further grain boundary oxidation.
4.3. Correlation of localized deformation and localized corrosion
with IASCC
The SEM observation and analysis shown in Figs. 10 and 11
revealed that increasing the irradiation dose resulted in a higher
IASCC susceptibility of 304NG SS. Further, the discussion in Sec-
tions 4.1 and 4.2 suggested that increasing the irradiation dose
also promoted the localized corrosion along the slip step and grain
boundary, in correspondence with a localized residual strain con-
centration in these regions. This indicated a correlation between
the higher IASCC susceptibility and the enhanced localized defor-
mation and corrosion along the slip step and grain boundary.
As has been acknowledged, the residual strain concentration at
grain boundary in austenitic alloys could promote the cracking sus-
ceptibility in high temperature water [27,28,48–51]. As mentioned
previously, straining could result in formation of a higher slip step
and heavier lattice distortion at the grain boundary in the specimen
subjected to a higher irradiation dose. This was correlated with the
deformation heterogeneity and well described by the number of
dislocations that were localized into the dislocation channel and
accumulated to the grain boundary [7,8]. The slip step and dis-
torted grain boundary indicated high dislocation density and strain
concentration (Fig. 4), which accelerated the cracking initiation.
The strain concentration, on the other hand, affected the local-
ized corrosion behavior at the slip step and grain boundary. As the
oxidation under stress/strain favors the formation of cracking initi-
ation [32,33,43,52], a higher corrosion rate at the slip step and grain
boundary in the steel with a higher irradiation dose caused a higher
cracking susceptibility, shown in Figs. 10 and 11. The oxide at the
grain boundary promoted the initiation of a typical intergranular
SCC, while the oxide at slip step promoted the intragranular crack-
ing initiation since most part of the slip step was within the grain.
As such, increasing the irradiation dose promoted SCC initiation
due to the enhanced localized corrosion at the grain boundary and
slip step. Further, as a higher irradiation dose led to generation of
more slip steps with a higher height (Figs. 2 and 3), increasing the

5. Conclusions
Irradiation assisted localized corrosion and stress corrosion
cracking in 304NG SS in simulated primary PWR water were
investigated. The following conclusions can be drawn from the
investigation:
(1) Increasing the irradiation dose led to a higher localized defor-
mation at grain boundary and slip step of 304NG SS due to the
irradiation induced defects.
(2) Irradiation and strain promote intergranular corrosion of
304NG SS in simulated PWR primary water. This is attributed to
the synergic effect of irradiation and strain, which resulted in a
strain concentration and a depletion of Cr at the grain boundary
by RIS.
(3) The depth
 of the oxidation at slip step of irradiated 304NG SS
in primary PWR water is increased by a higher height of the
slip step due to the accumulated localized strain by a higher
irradiation dose.
(4) Increasing irradiation dose within the studied dose range (0.55
dpa) promoted IASCC initiation as a result of the synergic effect
of the enhanced localized deformation and corrosion at the
grain boundary and slip step.
