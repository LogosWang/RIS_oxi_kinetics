# Model survey D: oxidation–SCC/IASCC coupling models and RIS models

Full-text read of 11 PDFs (text extracted page-by-page with PyMuPDF; the harness PDF renderer was unavailable, so all quotes come from the extracted text and were checked against page markers; slides of the INL deck were additionally rendered and inspected as images). Reference lists were skipped. Page numbers refer to the printed journal pagination where it exists; for the Yavas accepted manuscript and the INL slide deck they refer to the manuscript page / slide number.

Context: the manuscript's own model couples RIS at a GB in 304 SS to GB oxidation in PWR primary water through a 1D oxygen transport equation along the GB in which D_eff depends on the local oxide-phase assemblage (Cr2O3, Fe3O4, FeCr2O4, SiO2, with SiO2 dissolution leaving porosity), interfacial reaction rates depend on the RIS-modified GB concentrations of Cr, Fe, Si, and the observable is intergranular oxide penetration depth versus time and dose. The three questions asked of every paper are (a) how GB oxidation kinetics enter, (b) whether oxygen transport/oxidation rate depends on the oxide composition formed, (c) whether alloy/GB composition (especially RIS-modified) is an input and whether oxide composition evolves.

## 1. pu2017diffusioncoupled

**Citation (as printed):** Chao Pu, Yanfei Gao, Yanli Wang, T.-L. Sham, "Diffusion-coupled cohesive interface simulations of stress corrosion intergranular cracking in polycrystalline materials", Acta Materialia 136 (2017) 21–31.

**Model type and governing equations.** A 2D polycrystal with crystal-plasticity FE (CPFEM) grain interiors and user-defined cohesive elements on the GBs. Impurity flux along the GB is driven by concentration and pressure gradients, Eq. (1) J = −D(T)∇c + k_p∇p, with mass conservation Eq. (2) ∂c/∂t = D(T)(∇²c + k_p∇²p), solved in 1D along each GB by explicit finite differences, Eqs. (16)–(18), with a Kirchhoff-type flux balance at triple junctions, Eqs. (19)–(23). The cohesive law is the Xu–Needleman potential Eq. (4) with viscous regularisation Eqs. (5)–(6); impurity concentration degrades cohesive strength and characteristic separations linearly, Eqs. (7)–(9): σ_max(c) = σ_max(0)(1 − mc), δ_n(c) = δ_n(0)(1 − mc), δ_t(c) = δ_t(0)(1 − mc), so K_IC = (1 − mc)·sqrt(EΦ_n/(1 − ν²)). CPFEM is Peirce–Asaro–Needleman, Eqs. (10)–(15).

**How oxidation kinetics enter.** There is no oxidation reaction at all; the "impurity" (oxygen is mentioned as the motivating species) only diffuses with a constant diffusivity and degrades the cohesive law. Quote (p. 26): "Measurement for grain boundary diffusivities is very limited in current experimental techniques [40]. Oxygen diffusivity in nickel based superalloy is estimated based on the Focus Ion Beam observations for oxidation layer depth [4]. Furthermore, as mentioned in introduction, grain boundary diffusivity is usually 10^5–10^8 times higher than that inside grains [18]. Thus, diffusivity is estimated as D_GB = 0.1085 µm²/s and oxygen diffusivity in nickel-based superalloy is adopted for the rest of this work." Table 1 (p. 26) and Table 3 (p. 28) list D_GB = 0.1085 µm²/s, k_p = 10^4 atoms/(N nm), c0 = 10^4 atoms/µm³. The pressure factor is likewise taken from Karabela et al.: "We adopted the pressure factors and diffusivities based on the predicted range from the study of Karabela et al. [4]" (p. 26).

**Oxide / alloy composition.** No oxide phase exists in the model; the diffusing species is a generic impurity concentration. Alloy composition does not enter; CPFEM parameters are "for a nickel-based superalloy" (Table 2, p. 27). Oxide composition is not predicted.

**Irradiation / RIS.** Not mentioned (the only "neutron" occurrence is a neutron-diffraction reference in the bibliography).

**Predictions and data.** Verification of the FD diffusion solver against the ABAQUS diffusion module on a three-grain model (Fig. 6, p. 26). Impurity accumulation at triple junctions under stress (Fig. 7, p. 27). Three failure modes (none / internal crack / near-surface crack) vs degradation factor mc0 at a strain rate of 10^-3 s^-1 (Figs. 8–9, pp. 28–29). Failure mechanism maps vs normalised strain rate, strain amplitude and normalised diffusivity D_GB/(γ̇0 d²) (Figs. 10–11, pp. 29–30). Comparison is qualitative only: "This effect of strain rate on stress corrosion intergranular failure modes is consistent with observations from slow strain rate tensile tests (SSRT) on nickel based alloy and SS316 [42]" (p. 29).

**Authors' limitations.** "Due to the technique restrictions, experimental measurement of diffusivity and impurity concentration remains unclear" (p. 21); "very few studies investigated the pressure factor and diffusivities in diffusions due to the limitation of measurement techniques. It can be only estimated from indirect experimental fittings" (p. 26); "In our future studies, the proposed framework will be extended to a 3D polycrystalline model" (p. 30).

**Relation to the manuscript.** Pu et al. supply a mechanical consequence (cohesive degradation) of GB oxygen ingress but treat the ingress itself as a single constant D with no reaction, no oxide and no composition dependence; the manuscript's phase-dependent D_eff, interfacial reaction rates and RIS-controlled GB chemistry are precisely the chemistry this framework takes as an external input.

## 2. sedlak2019coupled

**Citation (as printed):** M. Sedlak, B. Alfredsson, P. Efsing, "A coupled diffusion and cohesive zone model for intergranular stress corrosion cracking in 316L stainless steel exposed to cold work in primary water conditions", Engineering Fracture Mechanics 217 (2019) 106543.

**Model type and governing equations.** Implicit, segregated multi-physics FE model (Abaqus *UEL) on a CT specimen: (i) 1D Fick's second law for aggressive ions (Cl−, SO4²−) along the crack/GB, Eq. (1) with electromigration and reaction terms dropped, giving Eq. (2) ∂c_ions/∂t = ∂/∂x[D(η_T) ∂c_ions/∂x]; (ii) the diffusivity is a function of the cohesive damage parameter η_T, Eq. (3), interpolating between D_s (species in the grain boundary) and D_l (species in liquid) with an exponent n0 ∈ [1, ∞) (n0 = 1, linear, used), "which created a moving boundary for the oxide" (p. 3); (iii) PPR cohesive potential Eq. (5), damage parameter Eq. (6), irreversibility Eq. (7); (iv) degradation: accumulated anodic slip-dissolution c_sd = ∫ h(c_ions, T_eff) dt, Eq. (8); fracture energies reduced linearly, Φ_n = Φ_n^ini − k_csd c_sd/l_el, Eq. (9), bounded by [Φ_full, Φ_ini]; (v) dissolution rate h(c_ions, T_eff), Eq. (10), an inverse-exponential-plus-linear function of ion concentration scaled by (T_eff/T_eff^ref)^n3.

**How oxidation kinetics enter.** As a fitted phenomenological law. Quote (p. 2): "The anodic slip-dissolution rate was defined as an inverse exponential with a linear function giving the crack growth rate (CGR). The dissolution rate depended on the concentration and traction normal to the crack surface." Quote (p. 6): "Eq. (10) includes five process parameters c_top, T_eff^ref, n1, n2 and n3 which must be adjusted to the experiments at hand." Quote (p. 8): "The parameters in the anodic dissolution Eq. (10) where fitted by the IGSCC experiment at σ_Y = 250 MPa by Terachi et al. [24]"; values c_top = 1.2·10^-15, n1 = 25·10^-10, n2 = 7·10^-7, n3 = 1.7, T_eff^ref = 200 MPa, k_csd = 18.4·10^-4. Diffusivities (p. 8): "The diffusivity for the ions D_l was set to chlorides diffusivity in water at temperature 288 °C, D_l = 3.8·10^-5 mm²/ms. D_s was set to D_s = 5.0·10^-13 mm²/ms, which was obtained from evaluating the process zone at zero CW". Note the diffusing species is the aggressive ion, not oxygen.

**Oxide / alloy composition.** Oxide composition does not enter; the oxide is a fixed thickness whose only role is the fracture-energy change: "In the case with the material 316L and PWR conditions a thin oxide (1 µm [32]) compared to specimen size will be formed" (p. 3) and "the 1 µm oxide thickness was negligible in other aspects than degrading the fracture energy and fracture behaviour" (p. 4). Alloy composition enters only through the bulk stress–strain curve / yield stress (316L, Alloy 600); no Cr, Fe or Si dependence. Oxide composition is not predicted.

**Irradiation / RIS.** Not included; the word "irradiation" appears only in the title of the authors' earlier reference [20].

**Predictions and data.** Crack length vs time for σ_Y = 250–550 MPa (Fig. 10, p. 8); CGR vs yield stress compared with Terachi et al. [24] (Fig. 11, p. 9): "all the diffusion and dissolution parameters … were fitted to the experiment at σ_Y = 250 MPa, alone. The results at the other yield stresses were attained by only altering the yield stress σ_Y" (p. 9); Alloy 600 vs Speidel and Magdowski (Fig. 15, p. 11); CGR vs K_I for 15 % CW 316L, fitted slope K^2.0 between the experimental K^1.8 and K^3.2 (Fig. 16, p. 12, discussed p. 10).

**Authors' limitations.** "The rapid increase is the change from having the specimen in vacuum to putting it in the PWR environment, the shape is not well-known and an exponential function was chosen for computational reasons" (p. 6); "the linear slope represented by the parameter n2 must be iterated to experiments with different chemical potential. In this case the n2 was set low because the focus is on the CGR due to cold work and not due to chemical properties" (p. 6); "The simulations may suggest slight underestimate for the higher K_I and a possible overestimate for the lower K_I" (p. 10).

**Relation to the manuscript.** Sedlak et al. is the closest mechanical analogue (1D GB diffusion with a damage-dependent diffusivity feeding a cohesive law), but their transported species is an ion, their "oxidation" is a five-parameter fitted dissolution rate, and the oxide is a fixed 1 µm layer; the manuscript's oxide-phase-dependent D_eff and composition-dependent reaction rates would replace exactly those fitted ingredients.

## 3. konstantinovic2017internal

**Citation (as printed):** M.J. Konstantinović, "Internal oxidation and probabilistic fracture model of irradiation assisted stress corrosion cracking in stainless steels", Journal of Nuclear Materials 495 (2017) 220–224.

**Model type and governing equations.** Probabilistic fracture mechanics of the oxidised part of the specimen only. K_I = gσ√a, Eq. (1); dK_I/dt = (gσ/2√a) da/dt, Eq. (2); with the empirical crack-velocity law da/dt = C K_I^n, time-to-failure t ∝ σ^-n, Eq. (3), and t1/t2 = (σ2/σ1)^n, Eq. (4). Weibull strength distribution P(σ) = 1 − exp[−(σ/σ_i)^m], Eq. (5); time-to-failure distribution P(t) = 1 − exp[−(t/t_i)^τ] with τ = m/(n − 2), Eq. (6); volume (oxide-thickness) effect P = 1 − exp[−(V/V_i)(σ/σ_i)^m], Eq. (7). Buffon's-needle grain-size argument P = 2a/(πd) (p. 224).

**How oxidation kinetics enter.** They do not; sufficient oxidation is a starting assumption and the only kinetic law is the subcritical crack velocity inside the oxide. Quote (p. 221): "The starting assumption is that substantial oxide layer is formed on the surface of the specimens, and that there is sufficient internal oxidation along the grain boundaries in front of the crack tip. … If the crack propagation is entirely governed by the oxide (breaking of only oxide bonds), the specimen failure (IASCC) can be regarded as being caused by the quasi-brittle oxide, and the failure analysis can be done by ignoring the metallic part of the specimen." A critical oxide thickness "of the order of 20 nm" is estimated from K_I^sub ≈ 0.1 MPa√m at 690 MPa (p. 221). Oxide thickness enters only as the Weibull volume V/V_i (p. 223). Oxidation kinetics are invoked qualitatively for crack arrest: "If the oxidation kinetics on the grain boundary have a time dependence that saturates, or is so slow in the absence of further fracture because of the rapidly decreasing rate of oxide penetration, then crack arrest occurs because the oxidized but non-fractured grain boundary cannot achieve the fracture toughness value" (p. 224).

**Oxide / alloy composition.** The oxide is identified only as "the chromium/spinel ceramic oxide formed on ss316 material [14]" whose cracking-susceptibility parameter n and inert strength are unknown and are fitted (n = 15, σ1 = 690 MPa; p. 221–222). No oxygen transport, no composition dependence, no evolution of oxide composition. Alloy/GB composition is not an input; RIS is mentioned once as the reason GBs are weak: cracks "develop further in the bulk following the oxidized and weakened grain boundaries (e.g. due to irradiation induced segregation)" (p. 221).

**Irradiation.** Present only through the data: SCK·CEN O-ring constant-load tests on a thimble tube "with the dose levels of 40 and 80 dpa" (p. 221) and Westinghouse data "between 21 dpa and 76 dpa" (p. 223). Interpretation (p. 224): "no significant difference between the time-to-failures of 40 dpa and 80 dpa samples [6] occurs because the oxidation has reached quasi-steady state thickness".

**Predictions and data.** Time-to-failure vs applied stress with 90 % Weibull envelope vs SCK·CEN data (Fig. 1, p. 222; parameters m = 10, n = 15, t1 = 14 h, σ1 = 690 MPa); Westinghouse polished samples falling within the V/V_i = 0.1 envelope (Fig. 4, p. 223); grain-size effect consistent with Takakura et al. (p. 224).

**Authors' limitations.** "The cracking susceptibility parameter (n) for the chromium/spinel ceramic oxide formed on ss316 material [14] is not known, so the relevance of n = 15 value obtained from the fit requires the experimental verification" (p. 221); "the inert strength of chromium/spinel oxide is not known" (p. 222); "a remaining crucial issue is the correlation between laboratory and NPP IASCC data" (p. 224).

**Relation to the manuscript.** This paper needs, as an external input, exactly what the manuscript computes: the GB oxide thickness (V/V_i) and whether GB oxidation saturates with time/dose; its own treatment of oxidation is a bare assumption of "sufficient" oxide with a Cr/spinel identity that is not predicted.

## 4. jivkov2006twodimensional

**Citation (as printed):** A.P. Jivkov, N.P.C. Stevens, T.J. Marrow, "A two-dimensional mesoscale model for intergranular stress corrosion crack propagation", Acta Materialia 54 (2006) 3493–3501.

**Model type and governing equations.** Regular hexagonal-cell microstructure represented as a discrete beam lattice (nodes at grain centres, beams for GBs) solved in ABAQUS; GBs are either susceptible (fail at strain ε_sf, a fraction of ε_y) or resistant (fail at ε_rf, a fraction of ε_u); one boundary fails per step; subsurface boundaries can fail to mimic 3D bypass (bridging). Analytical K_I = F(α)σ∞√(πa), Eqs. (1)–(2); K from FE stresses Eq. (3); bridging K_br = F(α)P√(2/(πr)), Eq. (4). Material: 304 SS, E = 206 GPa, σ_y = 205 MPa, ε_u = 0.4 (p. 3495–3496).

**How oxidation kinetics enter.** They do not. Quote (p. 3494): "At present the model is relevant only to fully sensitised materials and the kinetics of crack propagation are not taken into account. Therefore, the proposed model should be viewed as an advance to the percolation-like models by including mechanical effects. … it is a tool for assessing the relative microstructure resistance to intergranular fracture that is applicable to intergranular SCC, rather than a model for SCC." Quote (p. 3495): "In the present model, the kinetics of the stress corrosion mechanism are not accounted for, so the real time dimension of crack propagation is not determined and crack velocities are not calculated."

**Oxide / alloy composition.** No oxide, no diffusion, no composition; GB chemistry is reduced to the binary susceptible/resistant classification tied to GB character ("special (i.e. low-angle, low-energy) grain boundaries … resistant" vs "random … susceptible", p. 3495). Oxide composition is not predicted.

**Irradiation / RIS.** Not mentioned.

**Predictions and data.** Number of bridges and yielding bridges vs crack extension (Fig. 7, p. 3499); normalised K and shielding K_sh vs crack extension for f = 0.5–0.8 (Fig. 8, p. 3499); shielding vs f for different ε_sf and ε_rf (Fig. 9, p. 3500); bridging-only K (Fig. 10) and bridge contributions (Fig. 11, p. 3500). No quantitative comparison with experiment; motivation from X-ray tomography of a bridging ligament (Fig. 1, p. 3494).

**Authors' limitations.** "Any further refinement of the model requires that it be related to experimental observations of intergranular SCC extensions and distributions to tune the model parameters. This is necessary in order to turn the model into a predictive tool" (p. 3501); "a direct correspondence of the results from the present model and the 3D microstructure for a given f cannot be claimed" (p. 3498).

**Relation to the manuscript.** Purely mechanical/topological; it offers a framework in which the manuscript's time- and dose-dependent GB oxide penetration could define which boundaries become "susceptible" and when, but contains no oxidation, transport or composition physics of its own.

## 5. shoji2010formulating

**Citation (as printed):** Tetsuo Shoji, Zhanpeng Lu, Hiroyoshi Murakami, "Formulating stress corrosion cracking growth rates by combination of crack tip mechanics and crack tip oxidation kinetics", Corrosion Science 52 (2010) 769–779.

**Model type and governing equations.** FRI crack growth rate model: Faradaic CGR da/dt = (M/zρF)(Q_d/t_d), Eq. (1), t_d = ε_d/ε̇_ct, Eq. (2), with the oxidation charge Q_d split into stages I–III, Eqs. (3)–(7). Crack-tip plastic strain from HRR, Gao–Hwang and Gao–Zhang–Hwang asymptotic fields, Eqs. (11)–(14); crack-tip strain rate from the strain-gradient theory dε_ct/dt = (dε_ct/da)(da/dt), Eqs. (15)–(21). Transient oxidation kinetics: power or exponential repassivation laws Eqs. (22)–(23); solid-state oxidation L^{m1} = k1 t, Eqs. (24)–(28) (m1 = 1 linear, 2 parabolic, 3 cubic; m = 1 − 1/m1); general law i(t) = k_m t^-m, Eq. (30), with k_m = i_a0 t_0^m for slip-oxidation, Eqs. (31)–(32), or k_m = (1 − m)k1^(1−m) for solid-state oxidation, Eqs. (33)–(34); da/dt = κ_a(ε̇_ct)^m, Eq. (29); κ_a for the two mechanisms, Eqs. (36)–(39); full CGR equations Eqs. (40)–(45); K exponent n_k = 2n_RO m/((n_RO + 1)(1 − m)), Eq. (50).

**How oxidation kinetics enter.** As a fitted/general kinetic law whose constants are either the Andresen–Ford repassivation parameters or a parabolic constant extracted from bulk film-thickness data. Quote (p. 771): "A general oxidation kinetics law is used in the CGR calculation, emphasizing the role of solid state mass transport in SCC growth." Quote (p. 776): "The value of m is tentatively taken to be 0.5 for solid state oxidation based on the perfect conditions for the parabolic oxide growth law predicted by Wagner's theory of oxidation." Quote (p. 776): "The parabolic constants (for m1 = 2 or m = 0.5) for oxide film growth on bulk surfaces of 304SS [44] and sensitized 304SS are calculated with Eq. (24) from the oxide film thickness data after a certain immersion time in oxygenated pure water at 288 °C." For the slip-oxidation branch, κ_a = 7.8·10^-5 m^3.6, Eq. (49) (p. 776). The fitted k1 values range from 1.5·10^-18 to 3.6·10^-14 m²/s depending on r0 and ε_d (Fig. 10, p. 776).

**Oxide / alloy composition.** Oxide composition does not appear explicitly; the whole oxide is one parabolic constant k1. The only composition-related statements are (i) the film-rupture strain increases with Cr content (Attanasio et al.) "The film rupture strain is about 10^-3 from 4.6 to 23 wt.% Cr, and it increases to 2·10^-3 to 2.5·10^-3 fro 23 to 39 wt.% Cr" (p. 776) and (ii) a dissolved-hydrogen dependence of k1 for Alloy 600 taken from Combrade's compact-oxide thickness data (Fig. 15, p. 778). GB chemistry is explicitly flagged as missing: "One margin is that the crack growth occurs as the results of enhanced oxidation at grain boundaries while the oxidation rate constants in Fig. 11 or 13 are from bulk surface without applied stress. It is expected that the oxidation rate constant at grain boundary under stress would be higher than that at bulk surface without stress" (p. 777). Oxide composition is not predicted.

**Irradiation / RIS.** Not mentioned.

**Predictions and data.** Crack-tip strain rate vs K for sensitised 304SS compared with the PLEDGE equation ε̇_ct = 4.1·10^-14 K^4 and the EPRI 2007 CGR equation da/dt = 1.129·10^-13 K^2.181 (Figs. 7–8, p. 775); CGR vs K for sensitised 304SS in 288 °C oxygenated water vs Andresen et al. 1988 data, slip-oxidation branch (Fig. 9, p. 775) and solid-state-oxidation branch with GZH (Fig. 10, p. 776) and HRR (Fig. 12, p. 777) fields, with r0 selection criteria (Figs. 11, 13); predicted n_k vs m compared with NUREG-0313 (2.161), MRP-115 (1.6) and Andresen–Morra (2.3) (Fig. 14, p. 777); DH peak in k1 for Alloy 600 (Fig. 15, p. 778).

**Authors' limitations.** "Differences still exist between calculated values and experimental data in low K range (<15 MPa m^0.5) and high K range (>30 MPa m^0.5) especially at small r0 values. Such differences could be possibly caused by the selection of appropriate crack tip mechanical field parameters, the estimation of oxidation rate constants at grain boundaries, and the neglecting of the stress effect on oxidation rate constants. There are still uncertainties in choosing the input parameters in the theoretical models, since systematic data for solid state oxidation kinetics parameters especially at grain boundary under stress have not been obtained in high temperature water" (p. 777); "More fundamental data for oxidation kinetic laws and crack tip mechanics are necessary to improve the accuracy of the input parameters in the CGR equations" (p. 779).

**Relation to the manuscript.** Shoji's solid-state-oxidation branch needs a GB oxidation rate law (k1, m) that the authors admit is unavailable and currently borrowed from bulk surfaces; the manuscript's composition- and phase-dependent GB oxide penetration kinetics are a candidate supplier of exactly that GB-specific, RIS-dependent k1(t, dose).

## 6. ford1996quantitative

**Citation (as printed):** F.P. Ford, "Quantitative Prediction of Environmentally Assisted Cracking", Corrosion, Vol. 52, No. 5 (1996) 375–395 (1995 W.R. Whitney Award Lecture).

**Model type and governing equations.** Slip-oxidation (slip-dissolution) crack propagation model for 304/316 SS in 288 °C water: V_t = (M/zρF)(Q_f/ε_f)ε̇_ct, Eq. (1); V_t = A(ε̇_ct)^n, Eq. (2); bare-surface current decay i_t = i_0(t/t_0)^-n, Eq. (3); V_max = (M/zρF)i_0, Eq. (4); expanded form V_t = (M/zρF)(i_0 t_0^n/((1 − n)ε_f^n))ε̇_ct^n, Eq. (5); crack-tip anion activity a_ct = a_b·316^Δφ, Eq. (6); alternative decay i_t = i_0 exp(−Bt^n), Eq. (7); n = f(conductivity κ, corrosion potential φ_c, EPR), Eq. (8); ε̇_ct formulations for constant load, slow strain rate and cyclic load, Eqs. (9)–(11) and Table 1 (6·10^-14 K^4 /s for 304 SS constant load); simplification A = 7.8·10^-3 n^3.6, Eq. (12); irradiation: EPR = EPR_0 + 3.36·10^-24(fluence)^1.17, Eq. (13).

**How oxidation kinetics enter.** As an empirical, measured repassivation transient on a surrogate crack-tip alloy. Quote (p. 379–380): "The current transients generally have an initially high bare surface dissolution current density (i_0) for a short time (t_0). Thereafter, oxide formation or precipitation leads to a decay in the oxidation current density that often follows a general power-law relationship: i_t = i_0(t/t_0)^-n (3)". Quote (p. 382): "As indicated in Figure 6, the bare-surface kinetics for a simulated chromium-depleted, grain-boundary material (in this case, Fe-12% Cr-9.5% Ni) can be measured in a simulated crack-tip solution containing a relevant anionic concentration. The measured values of i_O, t_O, and n, which are fundamental to the crack propagation mechanism (Equation [5]), are functions of the simulated crack-tip environmental and material compositions." n ranges from ~0.33 (low-Cr, high anion) to ~1 (p. 382–383); i_0 ≈ 1 A/cm², ε_f = 10^-3, t_0 = 10^-2–10^-1 s (p. 384).

**Oxide / alloy composition.** No explicit oxide phases; the oxidation product is left generic: "slip-oxidation is more accurate since crack advance may be by a combination of M/M+ and M/MO oxidation reactions" (p. 379). GB composition is an explicit input through Cr depletion: "the n relationships are specific to a SS with a minimum grain-boundary chromium content of 12% Cr that is represented by an EPR value of 15 coul/cm2" (p. 383); the EPR parameter "has been shown via subsequent electronoptical observations to be directly relatable to the grain-boundary chromium depletion volume and minimum chromium content" (p. 382). Oxide composition is not predicted.

**Irradiation / RIS.** Included explicitly (section "Application of Prediction Methodology to Irradiated SS Components", pp. 389–391). Quote (p. 389–390): "Fast neutron fluence gives rise to irradiation-induced segregation of, for example, Fe, Ni, Mo, S etc., to the grain boundary, and also chromium denudation, without the need for Cr23C6 precipitation. All of these grain-boundary composition changes in principle may affect the intergranular CPR, but since the existing unirradiated model focused on the chromium denudation (and hence, EPR in Equation [5]), the irradiation modifications to the life prediction algorithms centered on this particular phenomenon. Thus, an effective EPR value was defined as a function of fast neutron fluence. EPR = EPR_O + 3.36 × 10^-24 (fluence)^1.17 (13)". Irradiation also raises corrosion potential via radiolysis, hardens the steel (modifying ε̇_ct) and relaxes residual stress (p. 389–390).

**Predictions and data.** Observed vs theoretical CPR for 304/316 SS over a wide database, "normal distribution with a mean value of 1.12" of calculated:observed (Fig. 9, p. 385); CPR vs corrosion potential including irradiated data from a BWR core and the Harwell cyclotron (Fig. 10, p. 386); CPR vs conductivity for 316L (Fig. 11); CPR vs ε̇_ct under static, SSRT and cyclic loading (Figs. 12–13, p. 387); CPR vs K vs NRC disposition line (Figs. 14–15, p. 388); crack depth vs time for BWR piping at several plants (Figs. 17–21, pp. 388–391); time to failure vs fast-neutron fluence for pre-irradiated 304 SS predicted with Eq. ## 9. cookson1998oxideinduced

**Citation (as printed):** J.M. Cookson, G.S. Was, P.L. Andresen, "Oxide-Induced Initiation of Stress Corrosion Cracking in Irradiated Stainless Steel", Corrosion, Vol. 54, No. 4 (1998) 299–312.

**Model type and governing equations.** Experimental study (five controlled-purity 304L heats, 3.2 MeV proton irradiation to 1 dpa at 400 °C, interrupted CERT at 3.6·10^-7 /s in 288 °C water with 2 ppm O2) with two analytical criteria: a probability that a random crack intersects an oxide particle, Eq. (1); and a crevice-deaeration criterion from Fick's first law, Eqs. (2)–(6), giving the maximum crevice width for deaeration W(µm) ≥ 1.56·10^-3 a², Eq. (7), compared with the opening of a cracked oxide particle W_eff = 0.005a, Eq. (8). Note: the "oxides" here are oxide inclusions in the matrix, not GB oxide films.

**How oxidation kinetics enter.** No oxidation kinetics of the alloy are modelled; the only transport law is oxygen diffusion in the crevice water. Quote (p. 308): "An order of magnitude estimate of the crack width required for crack tip to be deaerated for a given crack depth can be obtained using an approximated form of Fick's first law to O2 diffusion in a crevice, with parameters calculated by Andresen, et al.40" with D_O2 = 2·10^-4 cm²/s, ΔC_O2 = 2 ppm, I_red ≥ 75 µA/cm² (p. 309).

**Oxide / alloy composition.** Oxide-particle composition is not analysed in the model; the controlling variables are particle number density and size (Table 2, p. 302). GB composition enters the argument qualitatively through the width of the RIS Cr-depleted zone: "Given the very narrow width of Cr depletion at the grain boundary (on the order of 5 nm), it is unlikely that the crack in the particle will be aligned with the grain-boundary plane. For a particle 2 µm in diam … the probability of the crack lining up with the grain boundary would be only 0.005 µm/1 µm or 0.5%" (p. 310). Introduction: "Cr depletion from a bulk concentration of 20 wt% to a grain-boundary concentration of 17.5 wt% led to severe intergranular cracking" (p. 300). No oxide composition is predicted.

**Irradiation / RIS.** Yes. "All samples were irradiated with 3.2 MeV protons to a total dose of 1 dpa at a rate of ≈ 7 × 10^-6 dpa/s … at 400°C ± 10°C … produced an irradiated microstructure and microchemistry similar to that generated by neutron irradiation at 288°C and to doses between 1 dpa and 10 dpa" (p. 301). Central finding: "intergranular cracking was found only when the samples were both irradiated and strained in 288°C water" (p. 303).

**Predictions and data.** Crack density vs alloy oxygen content and vs number of oxides > 1 µm (Figs. 5–6, pp. 304–305); ratio of IG cracks to cracked oxides 0.0021–0.0036 (Table 3, p. 304); 8 of 10 short cracks contained an oxide vs 19–20 % expected by chance (p. 304); critical oxide size ≈ 3 µm from the deaeration criterion "in good agreement with the observed size of the oxides particles which initiated intergranular cracks, which were all > 2 µm in diam" (p. 310).

**Authors' limitations.** "application of these models to the deaeration that can occur in crevices at high temperature in high-purity water is limited by the lack of the data for the kinetics of the cathodic reduction reactions that are critical for quantifying the distance over which deaeration can take place" (p. 309).

**Relation to the manuscript.** Provides the IASCC initiation-side motivation that a RIS Cr-depleted GB must be exposed to an aggressive environment to crack, and uses the ~5 nm RIS width as a geometric input; it contains no GB oxidation model, so the manuscript's prediction of how fast such an exposed, RIS-modified GB oxidises is complementary.

## Cross-cutting observations for the manuscript's positioning

1. None of the nine SCC/IASCC models computes GB oxidation kinetics from the oxide phases that form. Oxidation enters as: a constant diffusivity with no reaction (Pu 2017), a constant diffusivity plus constant second-order rate (Aoyagi 2012), a fitted five-parameter dissolution rate with damage-switched diffusivity (Sedlak 2019), a fitted repassivation power law (Ford 1996) or a fitted parabolic/power oxidation constant k1, m (Shoji 2010), an assumed pre-existing oxide with only a crack-velocity law (Konstantinović 2017), or no kinetics at all (Jivkov 2006, Cookson 1998, Yavas 2018).
2. Dependence of oxygen transport or oxidation rate on oxide composition appears nowhere as a model ingredient; Shoji's k1 and Ford's n are single lumped constants, and Konstantinović names a "chromium/spinel" oxide only to borrow a ceramic cracking-susceptibility exponent. Yavas et al. provide experimental evidence (different alloy/environment) that Si is selectively oxidised into the GB product and that compact vs porous product layers behave differently.
3. RIS-modified GB composition is an input only in Ford 1996 (EPR as a function of fast-neutron fluence, Eq. 13, feeding the repassivation exponent n) and, qualitatively, in Cookson 1998 (5 nm Cr-depleted width). Shoji 2010 explicitly states that GB oxidation rate constants under stress "have not been obtained in high temperature water".
4. The two Kadambi works are RIS-only (Fe–Cr–Ni, no Si, no oxygen) and output exactly the GB Cr/Ni profiles vs dose and temperature that the manuscript's oxidation sub-model consumes; Kadambi 2025 notes Si enrichment at GBs as an unmodelled complication.
