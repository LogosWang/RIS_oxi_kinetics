\begin{abstract}
\emergencystretch=1em
Irradiation accelerates the intergranular oxidation that precedes
irradiation-assisted stress corrosion cracking of austenitic stainless steels
in PWR primary water, but no model connects the radiation-induced segregation
(RIS) at a grain boundary to the kinetics of its oxidation. We develop a model
that couples RIS to grain-boundary oxidation through the oxide itself:
selective interfacial reactions convert the local, segregated alloy
composition into an oxide composition, the oxide retains that composition as
it grows, and the oxygen diffusivities along and across the boundary are
computed from the resulting composition profile, with the porosity left by the
dissolution of oxidized Si included. With one parameter set for all doses, the
model reproduces the intergranular penetration depths measured on
proton-irradiated 304 nuclear-grade stainless steel after \SI{500}{h} at 0,
0.5 and \SI{3}{\dpa} (45, 69 and \SI{106}{nm} calculated against 40, 62 and
\SI{105}{nm} measured), and it predicts the Cr valley observed at the centre
of the oxide of the irradiated specimens. Under the present parameterization
the results support the view that Si enrichment is the dominant factor in the
dose dependence, with Cr depletion contributing a minor part. Applied
without adjustment to concurrent
irradiation and oxidation of a type 316 baffle-former bolt after 40 years of
service, the model gives a penetration depth of \SI{2.6}{\micro\metre}, within
the 2 to \SI{3}{\micro\metre} measured.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\makebox[\textwidth][c]{\includegraphics[width=18.6cm]{physical_picture}}
\caption{Physical picture of intergranular oxidation in primary water without
(a--c) and with (d--f) prior irradiation. (a) Solution-annealed boundary with
uniform chemistry. (b) Oxygen entering at the boundary mouth oxidizes Cr
selectively and forms a Cr-rich intergranular oxide. (c) The Cr-rich oxide
limits oxygen transport along the boundary and the penetration stays shallow.
(d) Irradiation drives point defects to the boundary and the resulting
radiation-induced segregation depletes Cr and enriches Ni and Si. (e) On
exposure the Cr-depleted boundary forms an oxide poor in Cr, and the
segregated Si oxidizes and dissolves, leaving pores. (f) The degraded oxide
transports oxygen faster along the boundary and the oxide penetrates deeper.}
\label{fig:physical-picture}
\end{figure}

% ---- 1. Phenomenon, why it matters, key observations, question ----
Irradiation-assisted stress corrosion cracking (IASCC) is a major
degradation mode of austenitic stainless steel core internals in
light-water reactors \parencite{was2011irradiationassisted}. In irradiated
stainless steels exposed to high-temperature water, intergranular oxidation weakens grain boundaries and precedes intergranular crack initiation
\cite{was2024how}, and grain-boundary oxidation has been identified as
a necessary precursor of IASCC at low doses \cite{kuang2022highresolution}. In 304
stainless steel (304SS) exposed to pressurized water reactor (PWR) primary
water, the extent of this oxidation depends on the chemical changes
introduced by prior irradiation (Fig.~\ref{fig:physical-picture}).
\textcite{deng2017effect} found that increasing the proton irradiation dose
produced deeper intergranular oxidation in 304 nuclear-grade stainless
steel after water exposure, accompanied by a decrease in the Cr content of
the intergranular oxide. Cr depletion is not the only chemical change that
matters: Si enrichment promotes intergranular oxidation and cracking even
when Cr is not depleted \parencite{du2026silicon}. These observations
connect irradiation to both the chemistry and the penetration depth of the
intergranular oxide. Turning them into a predictive relationship requires a
description of how the changing oxide composition and structure affect
oxygen transport and, consequently, the advance of the oxidation front.

% ---- 2. RIS sets the boundary composition; oxide Cr sets transport resistance ----
Radiation-induced segregation (RIS) establishes the local alloy
composition from which the intergranular oxide develops
(Fig.~\ref{fig:physical-picture}d). Coupling between
solute and irradiation-generated point-defect fluxes redistributes
alloying elements near grain boundaries. Measurements on irradiated
304 nuclear-grade stainless steel reveal Cr depletion and Ni and Si enrichment
at the grain boundary \cite{deng2021grain}. Subsequent
oxidation selectively consumes alloying elements
(Fig.~\ref{fig:physical-picture}b,e), changing both the oxide
composition and the adjacent metal, where Ni enrichment has also been
observed \cite{deng2021grain}. The lower Cr content of the oxide
formed on irradiated boundaries has been associated with reduced
resistance to oxygen diffusion \cite{deng2017effect}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:physical-picture}c,f).

% ---- 3. Si oxidation and dissolution add porosity to the same transport quantity ----
Si oxidation and dissolution alter this transport resistance by changing
the structure through which oxygen moves
(Fig.~\ref{fig:physical-picture}e). The role of Si was established by
\textcite{du2026silicon} with model alloys of systematically varied Cr and
Si contents in hydrogenated water, which allowed the contributions of Cr
depletion and Si enrichment to be separated. They
attributed the greater oxide degradation and cracking susceptibility at
higher Si content to dissolution of \ce{SiO2} in the inner oxide and the
resulting porous, defective film, and their STEM analysis of intergranular
oxides indicated faster grain-boundary diffusion at higher Si content. Related microscopy on
proton-irradiated 316L identified Si enrichment at oxide tips and
porosity behind them, consistent with preferential Si oxidation followed
by dissolution \parencite{kuang2022highresolution}. Although that alloy
differs from 304SS in bulk composition, the sequence itself is not
alloy-specific: oxidation and dissolution of segregated Si appears as a
step in the IASCC mechanism summarized by \textcite{was2024how} for
austenitic stainless steels generally. Together, these studies provide
a basis for including dissolution-generated porosity in the transport
description. With the dependence on Cr content, they motivate
representing the evolving oxide composition and porosity through
effective oxygen diffusivities computed from both, which connect the
segregation of multiple elements to the quantities governing oxygen
transport.

% ---- 4. Existing models and the gap ----
Each half of this sequence has been modelled separately. The segregation half is settled enough
that the grain boundary composition can be computed rather than only measured.
Since the inverse-Kirkendall and rate-theory descriptions of RIS in
concentrated alloys \parencite{marwick1978segregation,wiedersich1979theory},
the composition profile at a grain boundary has been obtained from the
coupled fluxes of point defects and alloying elements, with Fe--Cr--Ni
parameterizations that reproduce the measured dose and temperature
dependence of Cr depletion and Ni enrichment
\parencite{allen1998modeling}. Recent models predict the
segregation at grain boundaries and dislocation cell walls in austenitic
Fe--Cr--Ni \parencite{kadambi2025phasefield} and the conditions under which
Cr switches from enrichment to depletion in Fe--Cr
\parencite{oplinger2026radiationinduceda}. All of these models return the segregation profile as their final output;
none includes how the modified boundary composition affects the subsequent
grain-boundary oxidation.

On the oxidation side, the dependence of intergranular penetration depth
on dose and exposure time has been characterized
experimentally \parencite{deng2017effect,deng2020corrosion}, but existing
kinetic models of grain-boundary oxidation do not respond to the grain boundary
chemistry. Models that resolve oxygen transport along the grain boundary treat
the oxygen diffusivity as a single constant: in analytical or
semi-analytical solutions that give the penetration depth in closed form
\parencite{xu2015modeling,evans2013mechanism}, in
finite-element models that couple oxygen diffusion along grain boundaries
with mechanical deformation \parencite{zhao2011modeling,karabela2013oxygen}
or combine it with a constant reaction rate \parencite{aoyagi2012crystal},
and in phase-field simulations of a single
stoichiometric chromia intrusion
\parencite{rancourt2025viscoplasticity}. In none of
these does the diffusivity depend on the composition of the oxide that has
formed. Thermodynamic selection of the
oxide phases from the local alloy composition has been implemented for a
Ni-base superalloy \parencite{fang2022numerical}, but with a constant
oxygen diffusivity, the bulk composition at the boundary, and without the
Cr oxide that limits transport. To our knowledge, no model takes the
irradiation-modified boundary composition as input and lets the resulting
oxide composition set the oxygen transport that governs further penetration.

Establishing this connection requires tracking how the segregated
alloy supplies the interfacial reactions, how those reactions change the
oxide composition, and how the resulting diffusivity affects subsequent oxygen
delivery. Segregation profiles, oxide composition, interfacial Ni
enrichment, and penetration depth then provide complementary observables
for assessing the coupling.

% ---- 5. This work ----
Here, we develop a coupled RIS and grain-boundary oxidation model for
304SS, represented by the Fe--Cr--Ni--Si system. The coupling is made
through the oxide itself: selective interfacial reactions convert the
local, segregated alloy composition into an oxide composition, the oxide
retains that composition as it grows, and the oxygen diffusivities along
and across the boundary are computed from the resulting composition
profile. Within this description, increasing the Cr content of the oxide lowers
the diffusivity, whereas Si oxidation followed by complete dissolution
contributes porosity that enhances transport. The same growth record
gives the diffusivity for oxygen transport along the grain boundary and
that for oxygen delivery across the oxide to the metal interface. The model
therefore connects RIS to oxidation kinetics through the resulting change
in oxygen transport, with metal consumption feeding back on the local
composition. The complete-dissolution treatment is a simplification
motivated by the mechanism discussed by Du and co-workers
\cite{du2026silicon}. We examine the dose and exposure-time dependence of
oxide penetration under sequential irradiation and water exposure, using
separate calibration of the RIS and oxidation components and a common
set of oxidation parameters across doses. The same equations, with
irradiation and oxidation acting together, are then applied without
adjustment to a baffle-former bolt after 40 years of service and compared
with the depth of intergranular oxidation measured on it.

The model consists of two components: a radiation-induced segregation (RIS) model for the evolving grain-boundary chemistry and a reduced-dimensional oxidation model for oxygen transport, consumption, and oxide growth. The RIS-predicted Cr, Ni, Si, and Fe concentrations provide the local alloy composition for the oxidation model. Oxygen transport along the boundary is solved for the cross-section-averaged concentration of the oxidized zone, and the delivery of oxygen to the oxide/metal interface is closed by the fundamental mode of the steady diffusion equation in the cross-section. The oxide composition, recorded across the section as the oxide grows, determines the effective oxygen diffusivities along and across the boundary, which control the local oxygen consumption and oxide growth rate. The formulations of the two components are described below.

Figure~\ref{fig:framework} summarizes the model. The computational domain,
Fig.~\ref{fig:framework-domain}, is a grain boundary that opens onto the surface exposed to the primary water.
Oxygen enters at the mouth through the surface oxide scales and migrates along the
boundary within the oxidized zone, whose half-width grows as the oxide thickens.
Only the half-domain on one side of the boundary is simulated, the other half
following by symmetry; it extends \SI{50}{nm} normal to the boundary and
\SI{300}{nm} along it, on a mesh of \SI{1}{nm} spacing.

The boundary acts as a sink of finite efficiency for the vacancies and
self-interstitials produced by irradiation, Fig.~\ref{fig:framework-ris}. The resulting
redistribution depletes Cr and enriches Ni and Si at the boundary, and the
imbalance between the two defect fluxes gives the segregation contribution
\(\mathbf{v}_{\lat}^{\mathrm{RIS}}=\mathbf{J}_{\Vac}-\mathbf{J}_{\Int}\) to the
lattice velocity; the profiles shown are calculated, mirrored about the boundary
and convolved with a \SI{1}{\nano\metre} FWHM Gaussian. At a given depth,
Fig.~\ref{fig:framework-ox}, the oxygen delivered along the boundary reaches the
advancing oxide/metal interface through the oxide cross-section and is
partitioned there among the three oxide end members; the metal swept by the front is either
oxidized, rejected into the metal, or carried away through the oxide, and the net
removal sets the oxidation contribution to the lattice velocity \(v_{\ox}\). The two components are coupled as shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:framework-coupling}: irradiation controls the boundary chemistry and
part of the lattice velocity, the boundary chemistry controls the interfacial
reaction rates, and the oxide that forms feeds back on the oxygen supply through
its thickness and its effective diffusivity.

\paragraph{Notation.}
Alloying-element indices are \(i,j\in\{\mathrm{Cr},\mathrm{Ni},\mathrm{Si},\mathrm{Fe}\}\);
oxide-phase indices are
\(k\in\{\mathrm{Cr_2O_3},\mathrm{Fe_3O_4},\mathrm{SiO_2}\}\).
Superscripts \(\Vac\) and \(\Int\) denote quantities associated with vacancies and
self-interstitials; the superscript \(\GB\) denotes a value taken on the grain
boundary. \(C\) is a site fraction, \(\mathbf{J}\) a flux in the two-dimensional metal
domain, and \(J\) a flux on the one-dimensional grain-boundary line. The coordinate
\(x\) is normal to the boundary and \(y\) runs along it; \(\mathbf{n}\) is the
unit normal to the boundary plane pointing from the metal towards the boundary,
the outward normal of the metal domain, so that a flux \(\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{n}\)
is counted positive towards the boundary.

\subsection{Radiation-Induced Segregation Model}
\label{sec:methodRIS}

The RIS model is based on solving a set of coupled mass-conservation equations for the
alloying elements (Cr, Ni, Si, and Fe) and point defects (vacancies and
self-interstitials). For each alloying element \(i\), the concentration evolves
according to
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial C_i}{\partial t}=-\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf{J}_i .
\label{eq:mass-metal}
\end{equation}
The site fractions of vacancies and self-interstitials are governed separately by
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial C_{\Vac}}{\partial t}
 &= -\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf{J}_{\Vac}+\epsilon K_0-R\,C_{\Int}C_{\Vac},
\label{eq:mass-vac}\\
\frac{\partial C_{\Int}}{\partial t}
 &= -\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf{J}_{\Int}+\epsilon K_0-R\,C_{\Int}C_{\Vac},
\label{eq:mass-int}
\end{align}
where \(C_{\Vac}\) and \(C_{\Int}\) are the vacancy and self-interstitial site
fractions, \(\epsilon\) is the damage efficiency, \(K_0\) is the radiation dose rate,
and \(R\) is the vacancy--interstitial recombination rate coefficient. The flux of each
alloying element carried by each defect mechanism is
\begin{align}
\mathbf{J}_i^{\Vac} &= -\sum_j L_{ij}^{\Vac}\,
   \nabla\!\left(\mu_j-\mu_{\Vac}\right),
\label{eq:flux-vac}\\
\mathbf{J}_i^{\Int} &= -\sum_j L_{ij}^{\Int}\,
   \nabla\!\left(\mu_j+\mu_{\Int}\right),
\label{eq:flux-int}
\end{align}
where the chemical potentials are those of an ideal solution,
\begin{align}
\mu_j &= \mu_j^{0}+k_BT\ln C_j,
\label{eq:mu-atom}\\
\mu_{\Vac} &= k_BT\ln\frac{C_{\Vac}}{C_{\Vac}^{\mathrm{eq}}},\qquad
\mu_{\Int} = k_BT\ln\frac{C_{\Int}}{C_{\Int}^{\mathrm{eq}}},
\label{eq:mu-defect}
\end{align}
with \(\mu_j^{0}\) the reference chemical potential of element \(j\) and
\(C_{\Vac}^{\mathrm{eq}}\), \(C_{\Int}^{\mathrm{eq}}\) the thermal-equilibrium
defect site fractions, all taken independent of composition. The alloy is thus
treated as an ideal solution: the activity coefficients are unity and no
thermodynamic factor enters the fluxes. The Onsager coefficients are
\begin{align}
L_{ii}^{\Vac} &= \frac{C_iD_i^{\Vac}C_{\Vac}}{k_BT}
  \left(1+\frac{1-f_0^{\Vac}}{f_0^{\Vac}}\,
  \frac{C_iD_i^{\Vac}}{\sum_m C_mD_m^{\Vac}}\right),
\label{eq:onsager-vac-diag}\\
L_{ij}^{\Vac} &= L_{ji}^{\Vac}
  = \frac{1-f_0^{\Vac}}{f_0^{\Vac}}\,
    \frac{C_iD_i^{\Vac}\,C_jD_j^{\Vac}\,C_{\Vac}}
         {k_BT\,\sum_m C_mD_m^{\Vac}},
    \qquad i\neq j,
\label{eq:onsager-vac-off}\\
L_{ii}^{\Int} &= \frac{C_iD_i^{\Int}C_{\Int}}{k_BT}
  \left(1+\frac{1-f_0^{\Int}}{f_0^{\Int}}\,
  \frac{C_iD_i^{\Int}}{\sum_m C_mD_m^{\Int}}\right),
\label{eq:onsager-int-diag}\\
L_{ij}^{\Int} &= L_{ji}^{\Int}
  = \frac{1-f_0^{\Int}}{f_0^{\Int}}\,
    \frac{C_iD_i^{\Int}\,C_jD_j^{\Int}\,C_{\Int}}
         {k_BT\,\sum_m C_mD_m^{\Int}},
    \qquad i\neq j,
\label{eq:onsager-int-off}
\end{align}
where \(D_i^{\Vac}\) and \(D_i^{\Int}\) are the mechanism-specific diffusion
coefficients of element \(i\), and \(f_0^{\Vac}\) and \(f_0^{\Int}\) are the
corresponding correlation factors. The net defect fluxes follow from conservation of
lattice sites,
\begin{align}
\mathbf{J}_{\Vac} &= -\sum_i \mathbf{J}_i^{\Vac},
\label{eq:netflux-vac}\\
\mathbf{J}_{\Int} &= \sum_i \mathbf{J}_i^{\Int},
\label{eq:netflux-int}
\end{align}
and give rise to a lattice velocity
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_{\lat}^{\mathrm{RIS}}=\mathbf{J}_{\Vac}-\mathbf{J}_{\Int} .
\label{eq:vlat-ris}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Grain-boundary sink condition.}
The boundary is treated as a sink of finite efficiency for both point defects. At
the boundary plane the defect fluxes obey the Robin conditions
\begin{align}
\left.\mathbf{J}_{\Vac}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{n}\right|_{x=0}
 &= k_{\Vac}^{\GB}\left(\left.C_{\Vac}\right|_{x=0}-C_{\Vac}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right),
\label{eq:sink-vac}\\
\left.\mathbf{J}_{\Int}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{n}\right|_{x=0}
 &= k_{\Int}^{\GB}\left(\left.C_{\Int}\right|_{x=0}-C_{\Int}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right),
\label{eq:sink-int}
\end{align}
where \(k_{\Vac}^{\GB}\) and \(k_{\Int}^{\GB}\) are the grain-boundary sink
efficiencies of the two defects. The perfect-sink
(Dirichlet) condition \(C_{\Vac}=C_{\Vac}^{\mathrm{eq}}\),
\(C_{\Int}=C_{\Int}^{\mathrm{eq}}\) is recovered for \(k^{\GB}\to\infty\). With the
Dirichlet condition the defect gradients at the boundary, and with them both the
magnitude and the width of the segregation profiles, do not converge under mesh
refinement, a behaviour also reported for other Onsager-type RIS models
\parencite{oplinger2026radiationinduceda}; the finite-efficiency condition removes
this dependence (Supplementary Information, Sec.~S1). The sink efficiencies
are calibrated together with the defect-mediated transport coefficients,
Sec.~\ref{sec:methodRISCalibration}.

\subsection{Grain-Boundary Oxidation Model}
\label{sec:methodGBOxidation}

The oxidized zone is treated as a channel of half-width \(L(y,t)\), the
distance from the boundary mid-plane to the oxide/metal interface. The
unoxidized boundary is an open channel of half-width \(\delta_{\GB}\), and the
oxide of total thickness \(S=\sum_k L_k\) grows from the interface into the
metal, so that
\begin{equation}
L=\max\left(\delta_{\GB},\,S\right);
\label{eq:halfwidth}
\end{equation}
the part of the section not yet filled, \(L-S\), remains open channel with the
oxygen diffusivity \(D_{\Ox}^{0}\) of the unoxidized boundary.

The intergranular oxide formed on 304NG in PWR primary water gives an
amorphous-like fast Fourier transform in high-resolution TEM
\parencite{deng2017effect}, and the oxide core along the boundary plane of
proton-irradiated 316L is amorphous and porous
\parencite{kuang2022highresolution}; the oxide is therefore not treated as an
assembly of crystalline phases. The three oxides \ce{Cr2O3}, \ce{Fe3O4} and
\ce{SiO2} are the end-members of a locally mixed oxide: they carry the
stoichiometry of the Cr, Fe and Si consumption and the oxygen diffusivity
assigned to each component, and their volume fractions \(f_k\) describe the
local composition of the mixed oxide rather than the amounts of three distinct
phases.

The composition of the oxide is not uniform across the section. New oxide
forms at the interface \(x=S\) with the composition of the metal consumed
there at that time, the fractions \(f_k^{\mathrm{new}}=\dot{L}_k/\dot{S}\) of
the thickness rates of the three end-members, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:thick-cr2o3}--\eqref{eq:thick-sio2},
and is not modified once formed. The position \(x\) across the section is
therefore a record of the time of formation, with the oldest oxide at the
mid-plane and the newest at the interface, on both sides of the boundary.
The local diffusivity at position \(x\) is
\begin{equation}
D_{\Ox}(x)=\sum_{k\in\{\mathrm{Cr_2O_3},\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}\}} f_k(x)\,D_{\Ox}^{k}
          +D_{\Ox}^{\mathrm{por}}\,\bigl[f_{\mathrm{SiO_2}}(x)\bigr]^{m},
\label{eq:Dlocal}
\end{equation}
where \(D_{\Ox}^{k}\) is the oxygen diffusivity of end-member \(k\) and
\(D_{\Ox}^{\mathrm{por}}\) that of the porosity left by the dissolution of
\ce{SiO2}, whose volume fraction is \(f_{\mathrm{SiO_2}}\). The two oxides
enter as a volume-fraction average. The porosity enters through Archie's
law, in which the transport coefficient of a porous medium scales with the
porosity raised to a power \(m\)
\parencite{hunt2004continuum,balberg1986excludedvolume}: the pores left
by the dissolution connect to one another only in proportion to their volume
fraction, and \(m=2\), within the range 1.9--2.0 derived for three-dimensional
porous media from continuum percolation
\parencite{hunt2004continuum,balberg1986excludedvolume}, is held fixed. For
\(m=1\) the porosity would be a parallel path and Eq.~\eqref{eq:Dlocal}
would reduce to the plain volume-fraction average.

Oxygen moves along the boundary through the whole section in parallel and
reaches the interface across it in series, so an oxide whose diffusivity
varies across the section has two effective diffusivities,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
D_{\Ox}^{\eff,y}&=\frac{1}{L}\left[\int_{0}^{S} D_{\Ox}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
                     +D_{\Ox}^{0}\,(L-S)\right],\\
D_{\Ox}^{\eff,x}&=L\left[\int_{0}^{S}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{D_{\Ox}(x)}
                     +\frac{L-S}{D_{\Ox}^{0}}\right]^{-1},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:Deff}
\end{equation}
the arithmetic and harmonic means that are the exact transport coefficients
of a medium whose diffusivity varies in one direction, for flux parallel and
normal to that direction, and that form the upper and lower Wiener bounds of
effective-medium theory
\parencite{hashin1962variational,bruggeman2006berechnung}. The two integrals
are advanced in time together with the oxide,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{0}^{S} D_{\Ox}\,\mathrm{d}x
 = D_{\Ox}^{\mathrm{new}}\,\dot{S},
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{0}^{S}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{D_{\Ox}}
 = \frac{\dot{S}}{D_{\Ox}^{\mathrm{new}}},
\label{eq:Dint}
\end{equation}
where \(D_{\Ox}^{\mathrm{new}}\) is Eq.~\eqref{eq:Dlocal} evaluated for the
oxide forming at the interface. The transport therefore requires only these
two integrals and not the profile \(f_k(x)\) itself; the composition profiles
across the oxide reported in Sec.~\ref{sec:results} are reconstructed from
the growth record, that is, from the composition \(f_k^{\mathrm{new}}\) of the
oxide formed at each thickness \(S\).

Integrating the two-dimensional oxygen balance over the cross-section gives,
for the section-averaged concentration \(\bar{C}_{\Ox}(y,t)\),
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bigl(L\,\bar{C}_{\Ox}\bigr)
 &= -\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\bigl(L\,J_{\Ox}\bigr)-J_{\mathrm{r}},
\label{eq:O-mass}\\
J_{\Ox} &= -D_{\Ox}^{\eff,y}\,\frac{\partial \bar{C}_{\Ox}}{\partial y},
\label{eq:O-flux}
\end{align}
where \(J_{\Ox}\) is the oxygen flux along the boundary and
\(J_{\mathrm{r}}=\sum_k J_{\mathrm{r}}^{k}\) is the rate at which oxygen is
consumed by the interfacial reactions,
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:rate-cr2o3}--\eqref{eq:rate-sio2}, per unit area of interface.
The half-width \(L\) multiplies both the stored oxygen and the flux along the
boundary because the oxide section is at once the volume that holds the
dissolved oxygen and the channel that carries it; it stays inside the
derivatives because the section widens as the oxide grows.
Equation~\eqref{eq:O-mass} neglects terms of order \(\partial L/\partial y\)
and therefore applies to a slender oxidized zone; its derivation is given in
the Supplementary Information, Sec.~S3. The consumption \(J_{\mathrm{r}}\)
equals the diffusive flux reaching the interface only when the concentration
profile across the section is quasi-steady, which is the assumption used next
to relate it to \(\bar{C}_{\Ox}\).

The relation between \(J_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\bar{C}_{\Ox}\) is obtained from
the fundamental separable mode of the steady diffusion equation in the
cross-section, \(C_{\Ox}\propto\cos(\mu x/L)\), which satisfies the symmetry
condition at the mid-plane and the reaction condition at the interface, with
the across-section diffusivity \(D_{\Ox}^{\eff,x}\) of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Deff}. It
gives
\begin{equation}
J_{\mathrm{r}}=\frac{D_{\Ox}^{\eff,x}\,\mu^{2}\,\bar{C}_{\Ox}}{L},
\qquad
C_{\Ox}^{\intf}=\bar{C}_{\Ox}\,\mu\cot\mu ,
\label{eq:film-transport}
\end{equation}
where \(C_{\Ox}^{\intf}\) is the oxygen concentration at the interface and the
dimensionless eigenvalue \(\mu\in(0,\pi/2)\) is fixed at every position by the
interfacial balance between oxygen supply and consumption,
\begin{equation}
\frac{D_{\Ox}^{\eff,x}\,\mu^{2}\,\bar{C}_{\Ox}}{L}
=\sum_k J_{\mathrm{r}}^{k}\!\left(\bar{C}_{\Ox}\,\mu\cot\mu\right).
\label{eq:qss-closure}
\end{equation}
The interfacial reaction rates of the three end-members are
\begin{align}
J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}}
 &= k_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}}\,
    C_{\Ox}^{\intf}\,C_{\mathrm{Cr}}^{\GB},
\label{eq:rate-cr2o3}\\
J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}}
 &= k_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}}\,
    C_{\Ox}^{\intf}\,C_{\mathrm{Fe}}^{\GB},
\label{eq:rate-fe3o4}\\
J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{SiO_2}}
 &= k_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{SiO_2}}\,
    C_{\Ox}^{\intf}\,C_{\mathrm{Si}}^{\GB},
\label{eq:rate-sio2}
\end{align}
where \(k_{\mathrm{r}}^{k}\) is the interfacial rate constant of end-member
\(k\).
The left-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:qss-closure} increases and the right-hand
side decreases monotonically with \(\mu\), so the root is unique; it is found by
Newton's method. In the reaction-limited limit,
\(\mu^{2}\simeq k L/D_{\Ox}^{\eff,x}\) with
\(k=\mathrm{d}J_{\mathrm{r}}/\mathrm{d}C_{\Ox}^{\intf}\), the interface sees the
average concentration, \(C_{\Ox}^{\intf}\simeq\bar{C}_{\Ox}\); in the
diffusion-limited limit \(\mu\to\pi/2\), \(C_{\Ox}^{\intf}\to0\) and
\(J_{\mathrm{r}}\to\pi^{2}D_{\Ox}^{\eff,x}\bar{C}_{\Ox}/4L\). The closure thus
spans both regimes without a prescribed transport resistance; its derivation,
the neglected higher modes and its range of validity are given in the
Supplementary Information, Sec.~S3.

The oxygen concentration is normalized by the reference oxygen density
\(C_{\Ox}^{\mathrm{ref}}\) of the water-filled channel, so that the fluxes
\(J_{\mathrm{r}}^{k}\) have the dimension of a velocity, whereas the metal
concentrations are site fractions on a lattice of site density \(N\). The
rates of metal removal, in the same units as the metal fluxes, follow from
the stoichiometry of each end-member,
\begin{align}
J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Cr}}
 &= \tfrac{2}{3}\,\frac{C_{\Ox}^{\mathrm{ref}}}{N}\,J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}},
\label{eq:sink-cr}\\
J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Fe}}
 &= \tfrac{3}{4}\,\frac{C_{\Ox}^{\mathrm{ref}}}{N}\,J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}},
\label{eq:sink-fe}\\
J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Si}}
 &= \tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{C_{\Ox}^{\mathrm{ref}}}{N}\,J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{SiO_2}} ,
\label{eq:sink-si}
\end{align}
and the thickness of each end-member grows at the rate at which its formula
units are produced,
\begin{align}
\dot{L}_{\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}}
 &= \tfrac{1}{3}\,\Omega_{\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}}\,C_{\Ox}^{\mathrm{ref}}\,
    J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}},
\label{eq:thick-cr2o3}\\
\dot{L}_{\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}}
 &= \tfrac{1}{4}\,\Omega_{\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}}\,C_{\Ox}^{\mathrm{ref}}\,
    J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}},
\label{eq:thick-fe3o4}\\
\dot{L}_{\mathrm{SiO_2}}
 &= \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Omega_{\mathrm{SiO_2}}\,C_{\Ox}^{\mathrm{ref}}\,
    J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{SiO_2}},
\label{eq:thick-sio2}
\end{align}
where \(\Omega_k=M_k/(N_{\mathrm{A}}\rho_k)\) is the volume per formula unit
of end-member \(k\), with \(M_k\) its molar mass and \(\rho_k\) its density,
and the prefactors are the reciprocals of the number of oxygen atoms per
formula unit. \ce{SiO2} is treated as dissolved on formation, so
\(L_{\mathrm{SiO_2}}\) is the thickness its volume would occupy, which is
retained as porosity in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Dlocal}. The total thickness
\(S=\sum_k L_k\) and the composition \(f_k^{\mathrm{new}}\) of the oxide
forming at the interface follow from
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:thick-cr2o3}--\eqref{eq:thick-sio2}.

\paragraph{Partition of the unoxidized metal at the oxidation front.}
The element with the largest ratio of consumption to availability sets the rate
at which the oxidation front advances into the metal,
\begin{equation}
v_{\lat}^{\ox,\max}=\max_{i}\frac{J_{\mathrm{r}}^{i}}{C_i^{\GB}} ,
\label{eq:sweep-velocity}
\end{equation}
with \(J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Ni}}=0\) since Ni is not oxidized. The front
sweeps element \(i\) at the rate \(v_{\lat}^{\ox,\max}C_i^{\GB}\), of which only
\(J_{\mathrm{r}}^{i}\) is oxidized; the excess
\begin{equation}
E_i=\max\!\left(v_{\lat}^{\ox,\max}C_i^{\GB}-J_{\mathrm{r}}^{i},\,0\right)
\label{eq:excess}
\end{equation}
is either rejected into the metal ahead of the front or carried into the oxide,
where it is retained as unoxidized solute or transported along the oxide grain
boundaries to the surface \parencite{deng2020corrosion}. The partition between
the two paths is described by a function whose form is taken from the
continuous-growth model of solute trapping
\parencite{aziz1982model,aziz1988continuous}, which has also been applied to
the oxide/metal interface
\parencite{yu2018nonequilibrium,sherman2019thermodynamics}. In that model an
atom escapes the advancing interface only by diffusive exchange across it, and
the partition coefficient of the growing phase is
\(k(v)=(v/v_D+k_e)/(v/v_D+1)\), with \(v\) the interface velocity, \(v_D\) the
diffusive velocity of the interface and \(k_e\) the equilibrium partition
coefficient. The same form is used here for the unoxidized excess \(E_i\):
with \(v=v_{\lat}^{\ox,\max}\) and \(k_e=0\), so that the excess is rejected
completely when the front is slow, the fraction of the excess that passes to
the oxide side of the interface is
\begin{equation}
\lambda_i=\frac{v_{\lat}^{\ox,\max}}{v_{\lat}^{\ox,\max}+v_i^{\mathrm{r}}} ,
\label{eq:capture-fraction}
\end{equation}
where the rejection velocity \(v_i^{\mathrm{r}}\) takes the place of the
diffusive velocity: it is the characteristic velocity at which element \(i\)
can be rejected into the metal ahead of the front. Complete rejection
(\(\lambda_i=0\)), the local-equilibrium limit of classical oxidation theory
\parencite{sherman2019thermodynamics}, is recovered for
\(v_i^{\mathrm{r}}\to\infty\); for \(v_i^{\mathrm{r}}\ll v_{\lat}^{\ox,\max}\)
the front outruns the rejection and the whole excess passes to the oxide side
(\(\lambda_i\to1\)). In solute trapping the atoms that fail to escape are
frozen into the growing phase, and complete trapping gives that phase the
composition of the parent phase. Here Fe and Ni, which make up nearly all of
the excess, are both soluble
and mobile in the intergranular oxide \parencite{deng2020corrosion}, so the
atoms that pass to the oxide side are not frozen into it but are transported
along its grain boundaries towards the surface. The limit
\(\lambda_{\mathrm{Fe}}\to1\) adopted for Fe
(Sec.~\ref{sec:methodGBOXidationCalibration}) therefore does not return the
atomic fractions of the oxide to the composition of the metal; these remain
set by the reaction rates through
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:thick-cr2o3}--\eqref{eq:thick-sio2}. The net removal of element
\(i\) from the metal is then
\begin{equation}
\tilde{J}_{\mathrm{r}}^{i}=J_{\mathrm{r}}^{i}+\lambda_i E_i ,
\label{eq:sink-capture}
\end{equation}
which replaces \(J_{\mathrm{r}}^{i}\) in the interfacial conditions of
Sec.~\ref{sec:methodLattice}. The motivation for this treatment and its limiting
behaviour are given in the Supplementary Information, Sec.~S2.

\subsection{Coupling Strategy and Simulation Protocol}
\label{sec:methodCoupling}

\subsubsection{Lattice Velocity and Metal Consumption}
\label{sec:methodLattice}
Metal atoms leave the metal domain only at the grain-boundary plane, either by
oxidation or by removal through the oxide, Eq.~\eqref{eq:sink-capture}. Because
the metal lattice is incompressible, this removal creates no source in the
interior: the entire lattice translates towards the interface at the velocity set
by the net interfacial removal. Denoting by
\begin{equation}
v_{\ox}(y,t)=\sum_i \tilde{J}_{\mathrm{r}}^{i}
 =\sum_i\left(J_{\mathrm{r}}^{i}+\lambda_i E_i\right)
\label{eq:vlat-ox}
\end{equation}
the recession rate of the metal/oxide interface, which lies between the
oxidation rate \(\sum_i J_{\mathrm{r}}^{i}\) (complete rejection) and the sweep
velocity \(v_{\lat}^{\ox,\max}\) (complete removal), the total lattice velocity in
the metal domain is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_{\lat}(x,y,t)
 =\mathbf{v}_{\lat}^{\mathrm{RIS}}(x,y,t)
  +v_{\ox}(y,t)\,\mathbf{n} .
\label{eq:vlat-total}
\end{equation}
The two contributions differ in character. The segregation term is local: it is
determined pointwise by the defect fluxes at \((x,y)\). The oxidation term is
not; it is evaluated at the boundary and is independent of \(x\), so a single
value applies across the whole domain at a given \(y\). Segregation therefore
redistributes the lattice internally, whereas oxidation translates it rigidly, so that the total flux of alloying element \(i\) entering
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mass-metal} is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{J}_i
 = \mathbf{J}_i^{\Vac}+\mathbf{J}_i^{\Int}
  +C_i\mathbf{v}_{\lat} ,
\label{eq:flux-total}
\end{equation}
subject to the interfacial condition
\begin{equation}
\left.\mathbf{J}_i\!\cdot\!\mathbf{n}\right|_{x=0}
 = \tilde{J}_{\mathrm{r}}^{i} ,
\label{eq:flux-bc}
\end{equation}
which removes from the boundary plane the atoms incorporated into the growing
oxide or carried away through it.

Equations~\eqref{eq:flux-total} and \eqref{eq:flux-bc} collect the three ways in which
an alloying element can move. The first two terms are the diffusive fluxes carried by
the two defect mechanisms, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:flux-vac} and \eqref{eq:flux-int}; the third
is the drift of the entire lattice relative to the laboratory frame; the interfacial
consumption enters not as a bulk term but as an outgoing flux on the boundary plane.

The two contributions to \(\mathbf{v}_{\lat}\) have different origins but act
identically on every species. \(\mathbf{v}_{\lat}^{\mathrm{RIS}}\) arises because the
vacancy and interstitial fluxes are unequal, so lattice sites are created and destroyed
at different rates; \(v_{\ox}\) arises because oxidation consumes metal atoms from the
interfacial layer, so the lattice is displaced towards the advancing front. Because the
drift term \(C_i\mathbf{v}_{\lat}\) is proportional to the local concentration and
carries no species selectivity, it does not by itself alter the relative composition;
selectivity enters only through the mechanism-specific diffusivities in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:onsager-vac-diag}--\eqref{eq:onsager-int-off}, through the
stoichiometric sinks in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:sink-cr}--\eqref{eq:sink-si}, and through
the element-specific partition fractions \(\lambda_i\) of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:capture-fraction}.

\subsubsection{Sequential Irradiation--Oxidation Protocol}
\label{sec:methodSequential}



The reference experiments irradiate the specimen first and expose it to the primary
water environment afterwards. The model reproduces this protocol as two consecutive
stages that share the same governing equations and differ only in two switches: the
dose rate \(K_0\) and the oxygen boundary condition at the grain-boundary mouth.

In the irradiation stage the specimen is irradiated to the target dose with no oxygen
present, \(K_0>0\) and \(C_{\Ox}\equiv 0\), so that
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:mass-metal}--\eqref{eq:vlat-ris} govern the evolution alone and all
interfacial reaction rates vanish identically. The stage is integrated to
\(t_{\mathrm{irr}}=\Phi/K_0\), where \(\Phi\) is the nominal dose in dpa, and yields the
segregated composition profiles \(C_i(x,y,t_{\mathrm{irr}})\).

The oxidation stage continues from the final state of the irradiation stage: all
concentration fields, including the point-defect populations, are inherited unchanged,
\begin{equation}
\left.\left(C_i,\,C_{\Vac},\,C_{\Int}\right)\right|_{t=0^{+}}
=\left.\left(C_i,\,C_{\Vac},\,C_{\Int}\right)\right|_{t=t_{\mathrm{irr}}},
\qquad
\left.L_k\right|_{t=0^{+}}=0 ,
\label{eq:handoff}
\end{equation}
and the exposure clock is restarted from zero. The dose rate is then set to zero,
\(K_0=0\), so that the inherited defect supersaturation decays by recombination
according to Eqs.~\eqref{eq:mass-vac} and \eqref{eq:mass-int} without further
production.

Simultaneously, the oxygen boundary condition at the mouth of the boundary is switched
on. Oxygen ingress is limited by the surface film that grows on the free surface, whose
resistance increases as the film thickens; this is imposed as a Robin condition with a
time-decaying transfer coefficient,
\begin{equation}
\left.J_{\Ox}\right|_{y=0}
= h(t)\left(C_{\Ox}^{\mathrm{w}}-\left.\bar{C}_{\Ox}\right|_{y=0}\right),
\qquad
h(t)=\frac{k_{\mathrm{s}}}{\sqrt{t}+\sqrt{t_0}} ,
\label{eq:robin}
\end{equation}
where \(C_{\Ox}^{\mathrm{w}}\) is the oxygen concentration of the bulk water,
\(k_{\mathrm{s}}\) is the surface transfer coefficient, and \(t_0\) regularizes the
initial instant. The \(t^{-1/2}\) decay reproduces the parabolic thickening of the
surface film, so that the cumulative oxygen admitted through the mouth grows as
\(t^{1/2}\). The deep end of the boundary is closed,
\(\left.J_{\Ox}\right|_{y=L_{\GB}}=0\), where \(L_{\GB}\) is the length of
the boundary in the computational domain.

Because no point defects are produced once \(K_0=0\), the inherited supersaturation
decays on the recombination timescale, which is short compared with the exposure time;
segregation therefore effectively ceases early in the exposure stage, and the
composition field that the oxidation model sees is the one established during
irradiation. The sequential protocol thus isolates the compositional pathway: any dose
dependence of the predicted oxidation depth must be carried by the radiation-induced
segregation profile inherited through Eq.~\eqref{eq:handoff}, since neither radiolysis
nor sustained irradiation-assisted transport is present.

\subsubsection{Fully Coupled Irradiation--Oxidation Framework}
\label{sec:methodFullyCoupled}

The sequential protocol of Sec.~\ref{sec:methodSequential} is a special case of the
governing equations rather than a separate model: it is obtained by switching \(K_0\)
and the mouth boundary condition on and off in turn. Leaving both switches on
throughout, \(K_0>0\) together with the Robin condition Eq.~\eqref{eq:robin}, gives the
fully coupled problem in which segregation and oxidation proceed simultaneously, as
they do in reactor service. No additional equations or parameters are required;
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:mass-metal}--\eqref{eq:vlat-ris} are integrated as a single stage.

In this mode the two processes feed back on each other. Segregation continues to
modify the boundary composition while the oxidation front advances, so the composition
that the interfacial reactions see evolves in time rather than being fixed at its
end-of-irradiation value; conversely, the metal consumed at the front and the
associated lattice drift, Eq.~\eqref{eq:vlat-ox}, alter the concentration field on
which the segregation fluxes act. This mode takes no part in the calibration of
Sec.~\ref{sec:methodCalibration} and is used only for prediction.

\paragraph{In-service calculation.}
The fully coupled mode is applied to a component that was irradiated and
oxidized together in service: a type 316 stainless steel baffle-former bolt
removed from a Westinghouse two-loop PWR after about 40 years, with a dose of
about \SI{41}{\dpa} at the bolt head
\parencite{gussev2023microstructure,lach2024inservice,lach2025inservice}. On
the water-exposed head-to-shank transition of this bolt,
\textcite{gussev2023microstructure} distinguished two kinds of intergranular
damage: shallow, wedge-shaped oxidized grain boundaries, mostly 2 to
\SI{3}{\micro\metre} deep, and deeper short cracks of \SI{4}{\micro\metre} or
more. The shallow damage is the quantity compared with the calculated
penetration depth; the cracks involve stress and are outside the model.

The calculation differs from the calibrated one only in its inputs. The bulk
site fractions are those of the bolt, taken from the atom-probe composition
reported by \textcite{lach2024inservice}: 0.1932 Cr, 0.1155 Ni and 0.0124 Si,
with Fe as the balance, 0.6789, which includes Mo, Mn and the minor elements
as in Table~\ref{tab:composition}. The flux history of the bolt is not
available \parencite{gussev2023microstructure}, so the dose rate is taken as
constant in time, \(K_0=\SI{3.17e-8}{\dpa\per\second}\), that is
\SI{1}{\dpa} per year and \SI{40}{\dpa} after the 40 years
(\SI{350400}{h}) of the calculation. Irradiation and oxygen ingress
start together at \(t=0\) from the unsegregated alloy and continue to the end.
All other parameters, those of
Tables~\ref{tab:params-ris}--\ref{tab:params-transport}, keep their
calibrated values; none is adjusted to the in-service data. The domain is
extended to \SI{5}{\micro\metre} along the boundary, with a mesh spacing of
\SI{50}{nm} in that direction and \SI{1}{nm} normal to the boundary, and the
penetration depth is defined as in
Sec.~\ref{sec:methodTransportCalibration}.

\subsubsection{RIS Parameterization}
\label{sec:methodRISCalibration}

The RIS model requires the mechanism-specific diffusion coefficients
\(D_i^{\Vac}\) and \(D_i^{\Int}\) of the four alloying elements, the correlation
factors \(f_0^{\Vac}\) and \(f_0^{\Int}\), the recombination coefficient \(R\),
the damage efficiency \(\epsilon\), the dose rate \(K_0\), and the grain-boundary
sink efficiencies \(k_{\Vac}^{\GB}\) and \(k_{\Int}^{\GB}\) of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:sink-vac} and \eqref{eq:sink-int}. The dose rate and
irradiation temperature are those of the reference experiment. The correlation
factors are taken from their crystallographic values for the fcc lattice. The
damage efficiency accounts for the fraction of displacements that survive
in-cascade recombination and escape as freely migrating defects; its value is
that reported for proton irradiation. The mechanism-specific diffusion
coefficients and the grain-boundary sink efficiencies are calibrated against the measured grain-boundary composition
profiles of the reference experiment \cite{deng2021grain}, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:profiles}. The interstitial-mediated diffusion coefficients \(D_i^{\Int}\) are effective
values. In the multicomponent rate-theory model of
\textcite{fukuya2009multicomponent}, the preferential association of
undersized solutes with self-interstitials is represented by a coupling
parameter
\(\xi_k=\exp(E^{\mathrm{b}}_{k\Int}/k_BT)\big/\sum_m C_m\exp(E^{\mathrm{b}}_{m\Int}/k_BT)\),
the fraction of self-interstitials that form a mixed dumbbell with solute
\(k\), set by the solute--interstitial binding energy
\(E^{\mathrm{b}}_{k\Int}\), which is taken as nonzero for Si alone. In the
rate equations this parameter appears only as the product
\(\xi_k d_k^{\Int}\) with the interstitial partial diffusion coefficient, so
it is absorbed here into \(D_i^{\Int}\): the strong binding of Si to
self-interstitials appears as a larger effective \(D_{\mathrm{Si}}^{\Int}\),
and the weak dependence of \(\xi_k\) on the local composition through its
normalization is neglected. Related parameters are shown in Table~\ref{tab:params-ris}.


\subsubsection{Interfacial Reaction and Partition Parameterization}
\label{sec:methodGBOXidationCalibration}
The interfacial rate constants \(k_{\mathrm{r}}^{k}\) of the three end-members
and the rejection velocities \(v_i^{\mathrm{r}}\) of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:capture-fraction} are fixed by the EDX line scan across the
intergranular oxide of the unirradiated specimen of \textcite{deng2017effect}:
the metal-basis atomic fractions of the oxide and the Ni content of the metal
at the oxide/metal interface (Fig.~\ref{fig:edx-compare}a--c). The scans of the irradiated specimens take no part in the
calibration. Fe is soluble in the oxide and
diffuses through the intergranular oxide along its grain boundaries to the
surface, where it feeds the growth of the outer spinel particles
\parencite{deng2020corrosion}; it is therefore assumed not to be rejected
into the metal, and \(v_{\mathrm{Fe}}^{\mathrm{r}}\) is set to a value small
compared with the front velocity, the limit \(\lambda_{\mathrm{Fe}}\to1\) in
which the Fe removed is the amount swept at the metal-side composition,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:excess}. The same value is used for Cr and Si, which set or
nearly set the front velocity in Eq.~\eqref{eq:sweep-velocity}, so that their
excess is small. Ni is not oxidized and its rejection builds up the enrichment
of the metal ahead of the interface; \(v_{\mathrm{Ni}}^{\mathrm{r}}\) is
calibrated against the Ni content of the metal at the interface of the
unirradiated specimen after oxidation. The rate
constants \(k_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}}\) and
\(k_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}}\) are calibrated against the Cr and Fe
fractions of the unirradiated oxide. The oxide composition does not constrain
\(k_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{SiO_2}}\), since the \ce{SiO2} formed is treated as
dissolved; it is set above \(k_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}}\) and held
fixed, under the constraint
\(k_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{SiO_2}}>k_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}}
\gg k_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}}\) imposed by the order of
thermodynamic stability of the three oxides, \ce{SiO2} being the most and
\ce{Fe3O4} the least stable \parencite{young2016high}. The reference oxygen
density and lattice site density of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:sink-cr}--\eqref{eq:sink-si}
and the oxide densities of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:thick-cr2o3}--\eqref{eq:thick-sio2}
are fixed. The values are given in Table~\ref{tab:params-ox}; they are held
fixed in the calibration of the transport parameters that follows.

\subsubsection{Oxygen Transport Parameterization}
\label{sec:methodTransportCalibration}
With the interfacial parameters fixed, the oxygen diffusivities
\(D_{\Ox}^{\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}}\) and \(D_{\Ox}^{\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}}\) of the two
oxide end-members and the porosity diffusivity \(D_{\Ox}^{\mathrm{por}}\) of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Dlocal} are calibrated against the dose-dependent
grain-boundary oxidation depths of \textcite{deng2017effect}, measured after
exposure in simulated PWR primary water at 0, 0.5, and \SI{3.0}{\dpa}. The
predicted oxidation depth is defined as the distance from the boundary mouth
to the deepest position at which the total oxide thickness \(S\) exceeds the
channel half-width \(\delta_{\GB}\), applied identically at every dose. The
three diffusivities are adjusted to reproduce the measured depths at all
three doses simultaneously, using a single parameter set; no parameter is
allowed to take different values at different doses. The remaining transport
parameters are not adjusted: the Archie exponent is held at \(m=2\), the
open-channel diffusivity \(D_{\Ox}^{0}\) is set equal to the porosity
diffusivity, the channel half-width \(\delta_{\GB}\) is assigned, and the
surface transfer coefficient \(k_{\mathrm{s}}\) and regularization time
\(t_0\) of Eq.~\eqref{eq:robin} are assigned fixed values, to which the
predicted depths are insensitive. The values are given in
Table~\ref{tab:params-transport}.

The two levels of calibration are not fully independent. The oxide composition
fixes the ratios of the rate constants, whereas the total oxide thickness
depends on the rate constants and the diffusivities together through the
closure of Eq.~\eqref{eq:qss-closure}, which partitions the oxygen supply
between interfacial reaction and transport across the oxide. The absolute
level of the rate constants is therefore adjusted, together with the
diffusivities, so that the calculated total thickness of the intergranular
oxide is close to the measured one; it is not fitted more precisely.

record of Sec.~\ref{sec:methodGBOxidation}: the oxide formed first lies at the

(Eq.~\eqref{eq:capture-fraction}), so the calculated Ni content of the oxide is
zero, against measured values of 1 to \SI{4}{\atpct}. On the metal side the
calculated Ni shows a slight additional enrichment at the interface,
consistent with the measurements; neither Fe nor Ni is strongly enriched
there.

calculated normalized depths, 0.28 and 0.38, exceed the measured 0.12 and
0.18; this difference depends on the unknown onset and is not interpreted.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Oxygen Transport across and along the Intergranular Oxide}
\label{sec:discussionTransport}

The penetration depth is set by the rate at which oxygen reaches the front,
and the model resolves this rate into two steps: transport along the boundary, with \(D_{\Ox}^{\eff,y}\), and
delivery across the oxide to the interface, with \(D_{\Ox}^{\eff,x}\)
combined with the interfacial rates through the eigenvalue \(\mu\) of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:qss-closure}. The two diffusivities weight the same profile
\(D_{\Ox}(x)\) differently. The arithmetic mean is dominated by the most
permeable part of the section, the harmonic mean by the least permeable. In
the irradiated boundaries the porous oxide formed from the segregated plane
therefore carries the transport along the boundary, whereas the delivery to
the interface has to cross the dense Cr-rich oxide formed later from metal of
matrix composition and is limited by it. The closure also fixes the regime
of the interfacial reaction. With the calibrated values of
Tables~\ref{tab:params-ox} and \ref{tab:params-transport}, the ratio
\(kL/D_{\Ox}^{\eff,x}\) of the interfacial rate to the rate of transport
across the oxide (Sec.~\ref{sec:methodGBOxidation}) passes
unity when the oxide reaches a thickness of the order of \SI{10}{nm},
comparable to the oxide width at half the penetration depth
(Fig.~\ref{fig:poro-across}). The two regimes therefore coexist along the
oxidized length: the interface is reaction-limited near the front, where the
oxide is thin, and the consumption of oxygen in the thicker oxide behind it
is increasingly limited by delivery across the oxide. The interfacial rate
constants thus enter the kinetics both directly, near the front, and through
the composition of the oxide they produce.

The dose dependence of the penetration depth is, in the model, a transport
effect. In the sequential protocol the only quantity that irradiation hands
to the oxidation stage is the composition of the boundary,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:handoff}; the rate constants and the oxygen supply at the mouth
are the same at every dose, so the increase of depth with dose
(Fig.~\ref{fig:front}) arises through \(D_{\Ox}(x)\) alone, by the two terms
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Dlocal}: a lower \ce{Cr2O3} fraction raises the
volume-fraction average, and the dissolution of \ce{SiO2} adds porosity.

\subsection{Composition and Porosity across the Intergranular Oxide}
\label{sec:discussionTranscription}

Because the oxide keeps the composition it had on formation and grows from
the boundary plane outward, the position across the section records the
time of formation, and the segregation profile is transcribed into the oxide
in the order in which the front passes through it. Two features of the
measured oxide follow from this transcription without being targets of the
calibration, which uses only the composition of the unirradiated oxide, the
Ni content at its interface and the penetration depths
(Secs.~\ref{sec:methodGBOXidationCalibration} and
\ref{sec:methodTransportCalibration}); the composition profiles of the
irradiated specimens are predictions. The first is the Cr valley at the
centre of the oxide in the irradiated specimens, whose depth increases with
dose (Figs.~\ref{fig:edx-compare} and \ref{fig:Cr-min}): it is the Cr minimum
of the segregation profile, oxidized first at the boundary plane and then
overgrown by oxide formed from the less depleted metal on either side. The
second is the location of the porosity. The Si peak of the segregation
profile is transcribed to the same position, where its oxidation and the
dissolution of \ce{SiO2} convert it into the porosity concentrated within a
few nanometres of the mid-plane (Fig.~\ref{fig:poro-across}). The porous
interface that \textcite{kuang2022highresolution} observed along the centre
of the intergranular oxide of proton-irradiated 316L, up to the oxide tip,
and attributed to the same sequence of Si oxidation and dissolution, is thus
the Si counterpart of the Cr valley measured by \textcite{deng2017effect}: in
the model both are images of the segregation profile, and their position at
the centre is the position of the original boundary plane.

The transcription requires that the elements not consumed at the front do
not accumulate strongly ahead of it. In the local-equilibrium limit of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:capture-fraction}, \(v_i^{\mathrm{r}}\to\infty\), the ordering
of the oxygen affinities, Si \(>\) Cr \(\gg\) Fe \(\gg\) Ni, would deplete Cr
and Si and enrich Fe and Ni strongly in the metal ahead of the front, and the
composition at the interface would no longer be that of the segregation
profile. The scans show otherwise: on the metal side there is neither Cr
depletion nor Fe enrichment, and only a slight additional Ni enrichment at
the interface (Fig.~\ref{fig:edx-compare}). The partition of the unoxidized
metal, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:sweep-velocity}--\eqref{eq:sink-capture}, accounts for
this. The Fe swept by the front and part of the Ni pass into the oxide and
leave along its grain boundaries to the surface, where they feed the outer
oxide particles that \textcite{deng2020corrosion} observed to grow faster on
irradiated specimens; the rest of the Ni is rejected into the metal and gives
the slight additional Ni enrichment at the interface. Consistent with this
path, \textcite{boisson2018effects} observed larger outer oxide crystallites
on top of the preferentially oxidized grain boundaries of 316L exposed to
simulated PWR primary water. \textcite{lach2025inservice} found that the
large Fe-rich outer oxides on neutron-irradiated 316 baffle-former bolts lie
primarily over the oxidized grain boundaries, and proposed that the grain
boundary feeds them.

The measured Ni content of the oxide, 1 to \SI{4}{\atpct} against
zero in the model, indicates that part of the Ni passing to the oxide side is
retained in the oxide rather than transported out of it. Atom probe
tomography of the intergranular oxide detected Ni but no NiO\(_x\) among the
evaporated species \parencite{deng2021grain}, so the retained Ni is
unoxidized, as expected from the low oxygen affinity of Ni, and is most
likely dissolved in the oxide without yet having been transported out.

\subsection{Contributions of Cr Depletion and Si Enrichment}
\label{sec:discussionCrSi}

Irradiation depletes Cr and enriches Si at the boundary together. In the
model the two can be examined separately by resetting one segregation
profile at a time (Fig.~\ref{fig:front-decoupled}). Under the present
parameterization the results support the view that the Si enrichment is the
dominant factor: the Si
enrichment alone produces \SI{43}{nm} of the \SI{60}{nm} increase of
penetration depth between 0 and \SI{3.0}{\dpa}, reproducing the measured
depth at \SI{0.5}{\dpa} and falling \SI{17}{nm} short of it at
\SI{3.0}{\dpa}, whereas the Cr depletion alone adds \SI{7}{nm}. The difference lies in how each element enters
\(D_{\Ox}(x)\). The Cr depletion lowers the Cr content of the central oxide
by about 12 percentage points over the dose range (Fig.~\ref{fig:Cr-min})
and moves the volume-fraction average of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Dlocal} part of the
way from the diffusivity of one oxide toward that of the other, in a part of
the section that is a few nanometres wide. The Si enrichment converts a
boundary Si content that rises to \SI{3.0}{\atpct} (Fig.~\ref{fig:profile-si})
into a porosity of up to \SI{22}{vol\percent} at the same position
(Fig.~\ref{fig:poro-across}); even reduced by the Archie exponent, this
porosity has a diffusivity orders of magnitude above that of either oxide,
and the central oxide becomes the path along which oxygen reaches the front.

This result depends on how Si is treated in the model: all \ce{SiO2} is
assumed to dissolve, the oxygen diffusivity
of the porosity is calibrated, and the Archie exponent is taken from the
literature. If part of the \ce{SiO2} were retained, or the pores were less
connected, the contribution of the Si enrichment would be smaller. The
experimental assessments also place Si above Cr.
\textcite{kuang2022highresolution} found that segregated Si is oxidized
preferentially at the front and dissolves, leaving the porous interface
discussed above, while the oxide tip was not depleted in Cr, and judged the
Si effect rather than Cr depletion to be a key element of the IASCC
mechanism in PWR primary water. \textcite{du2026silicon}, with unirradiated
model alloys in which Si and Cr were varied independently, found that the
susceptibility to crack initiation rose strongly with Si content whereas a
low Cr content added little, with deeper intergranular oxidation in the
high-Si alloys.

\subsection{Concurrent Irradiation and Oxidation in Service}
\label{sec:discussionInService}

The in-service calculation is a prediction without an adjusted parameter.
The parameters were calibrated on proton-irradiated 304NG exposed for
\SI{500}{h}, with penetration depths of 40 to \SI{105}{nm}; the bolt is a
neutron-irradiated type 316 steel exposed for \SI{350400}{h}, at a dose rate
about 190 times lower and with irradiation and oxidation acting together.
The calculation extends the calibration by a factor of about 700 in time and
25 in depth, and the calculated depth of \SI{2.61}{\micro\metre} falls within
the 2 to \SI{3}{\micro\metre} measured for the shallow damage
(Fig.~\ref{fig:front-inservice}). The measured oxide also shows the
processes on which the model rests. The original grain boundary inside the
in-service oxide is depleted in Cr and in Si \parencite{lach2025inservice}
and, as noted in Sec.~\ref{sec:discussionTranscription}, appears to carry Fe
and Ni toward the outer oxide; in the model these correspond to the Cr valley
at the centre of the oxide, the dissolution of the oxidized Si, and the
removal of the unoxidized Fe and Ni through the oxide.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A model that couples radiation-induced segregation to grain-boundary
oxidation through the composition-dependent oxygen transport of the
intergranular oxide has been developed for 304 stainless steel in PWR primary
water. The main conclusions are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
\item The segregation model is calibrated on the measured grain-boundary
profiles, and the oxidation model, with a single parameter set, on the
composition of the unirradiated oxide and on the penetration depths after
\SI{500}{h} at 0, 0.5 and \SI{3.0}{\dpa}. Without further adjustment the
model reproduces the composition profiles across the oxide of the irradiated
specimens, including the Cr valley at its centre, and the ratio of the
penetration depths between doses in an independent exposure series. The
absolute depths of that series after \SI{1500}{h} are underestimated by
about a factor of two.

\item The segregation profile is transcribed into the oxide as it grows
outward from the boundary plane: the Cr depletion becomes the Cr valley at
the centre of the oxide, which deepens with dose, and the Si enrichment
becomes porosity concentrated along the centreline.

\item In the model, the dose dependence of the penetration depth is carried
by the segregation profile inherited from the irradiation stage and acts
through the oxygen transport of the oxide. Under the present
parameterization the results support the view that the Si enrichment is the
dominant factor: alone it produces \SI{43}{nm} of the \SI{60}{nm} increase
of penetration depth between 0 and \SI{3.0}{\dpa}, against \SI{7}{nm} for
the Cr depletion. This result is conditional on the complete dissolution of
\ce{SiO2} and on the porosity transport law.

\item Applied without adjustment to concurrent irradiation and oxidation of
a type 316 baffle-former bolt over 40 years of service, the model gives a
penetration depth of \SI{2.61}{\micro\metre} at \SI{40}{\dpa}, within the 2
to \SI{3}{\micro\metre} reported for the shallow intergranular oxidation
damage of the bolt.
\end{enumerate}
