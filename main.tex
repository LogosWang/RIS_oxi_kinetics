The model consists of two components: a radiation-induced segregation (RIS) model for the evolving GB chemistry and a reduced-dimensional oxidation model for oxygen transport, consumption, and oxide growth. The RIS-predicted Cr, Ni, Si, and Fe concentrations provide the local alloy composition for the oxidation model. Oxygen transport along the boundary is solved for the cross-section-averaged concentration of the oxidized zone, and the delivery of oxygen to the oxide/metal interface is closed by the fundamental mode of the steady diffusion equation in the cross-section. The oxide composition determines the effective oxygen diffusivity through an effective-medium treatment, which controls the local oxygen consumption and oxide growth rate. The formulations of the two components are described below.

Figure~\ref{fig:framework} summarises the model. The computational domain,
Fig.~\ref{fig:framework-domain}, is a grain boundary that opens onto the surface exposed to the primary water.
Oxygen enters at the mouth through the surface film and migrates along the
boundary within the oxidized zone, whose half-width grows as the oxide thickens.
Only the half-domain on one side of the boundary is simulated, the other half
following by symmetry.

The boundary acts as a sink of finite efficiency for the vacancies and
self-interstitials produced by irradiation, Fig.~\ref{fig:framework-ris}. The resulting
redistribution depletes Cr and enriches Ni and Si at the boundary, and the
imbalance between the two defect fluxes gives the segregation contribution
\(\mathbf{v}_{\lat}^{\mathrm{RIS}}=\mathbf{J}_{\Vac}-\mathbf{J}_{\Int}\) to the
lattice velocity; the profiles shown are calculated, mirrored about the boundary
and convolved with a \SI{1}{\nano\metre} FWHM Gaussian. At a given depth,
Fig.~\ref{fig:framework-ox}, the oxygen delivered along the boundary reaches the
advancing oxide/metal interface through the oxide cross-section and is
partitioned there among the four oxide phases; the metal swept by the front is either
oxidized, rejected into the metal, or carried away through the oxide, and the net
removal sets the oxidation contribution \(v_{\ox}\). The two components are coupled as shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:framework-coupling}: irradiation fixes the boundary chemistry and
part of the lattice velocity, the boundary chemistry controls the interfacial
reaction rates, and the oxide that forms feeds back on the oxygen supply through
its thickness and its effective diffusivity.

\paragraph{Notation.}
Alloying-element indices are \(i,j\in\{\mathrm{Cr},\mathrm{Ni},\mathrm{Si},\mathrm{Fe}\}\);
oxide-phase indices are
\(k\in\{\mathrm{Cr_2O_3},\mathrm{Fe_3O_4},\mathrm{FeCr_2O_4},\mathrm{SiO_2}\}\).
Superscripts \(\Vac\) and \(\Int\) denote quantities associated with vacancies and
self-interstitials; the superscript \(\GB\) denotes a value taken on the grain
boundary. \(C\) is a site fraction, \(\mathbf{J}\) a flux in the two-dimensional metal
domain, and \(J\) a flux on the one-dimensional grain-boundary line. The coordinate
\(x\) is normal to the boundary and \(y\) runs along it.

\subsection{Radiation-Induced Segregation Model}
\label{sec:methodRIS}

The RIS model is based on solving a set of coupled mass-conservation equations for the
alloying elements (Cr, Ni, Si, and Fe) and point defects (vacancies and
self-interstitials). For each alloying element \(i\), the concentration evolves
according to
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial C_i}{\partial t}=-\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf{J}_i .
\label{eq:mass-metal}
\end{equation}
The site fractions of vacancies and self-interstitials are governed separately by
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial C_{\Vac}}{\partial t}
 &= -\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf{J}_{\Vac}+\epsilon K_0-R\,C_{\Int}C_{\Vac},
\label{eq:mass-vac}\\
\frac{\partial C_{\Int}}{\partial t}
 &= -\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf{J}_{\Int}+\epsilon K_0-R\,C_{\Int}C_{\Vac},
\label{eq:mass-int}
\end{align}
where \(C_{\Vac}\) and \(C_{\Int}\) are the vacancy and self-interstitial site
fractions, \(\epsilon\) is the damage efficiency, \(K_0\) is the radiation dose rate,
and \(R\) is the vacancy--interstitial recombination rate coefficient. The flux of each
alloying element carried by each defect mechanism is
\begin{align}
\mathbf{J}_i^{\Vac} &= -\sum_j L_{ij}^{\Vac}\,
   \nabla\!\left(\mu_j-\mu_{\Vac}\right),
\label{eq:flux-vac}\\
\mathbf{J}_i^{\Int} &= -\sum_j L_{ij}^{\Int}\,
   \nabla\!\left(\mu_j+\mu_{\Int}\right),
\label{eq:flux-int}
\end{align}
with the Onsager coefficients
\begin{align}
L_{ii}^{\Vac} &= \frac{C_iD_i^{\Vac}C_{\Vac}}{k_BT}
  \left(1+\frac{1-f_0^{\Vac}}{f_0^{\Vac}}\,
  \frac{C_iD_i^{\Vac}}{\sum_m C_mD_m^{\Vac}}\right),
\label{eq:onsager-vac-diag}\\
L_{ij}^{\Vac} &= L_{ji}^{\Vac}
  = \frac{1-f_0^{\Vac}}{f_0^{\Vac}}\,
    \frac{C_iD_i^{\Vac}\,C_jD_j^{\Vac}\,C_{\Vac}}
         {k_BT\,\sum_m C_mD_m^{\Vac}},
    \qquad i\neq j,
\label{eq:onsager-vac-off}\\
L_{ii}^{\Int} &= \frac{C_iD_i^{\Int}C_{\Int}}{k_BT}
  \left(1+\frac{1-f_0^{\Int}}{f_0^{\Int}}\,
  \frac{C_iD_i^{\Int}}{\sum_m C_mD_m^{\Int}}\right),
\label{eq:onsager-int-diag}\\
L_{ij}^{\Int} &= L_{ji}^{\Int}
  = \frac{1-f_0^{\Int}}{f_0^{\Int}}\,
    \frac{C_iD_i^{\Int}\,C_jD_j^{\Int}\,C_{\Int}}
         {k_BT\,\sum_m C_mD_m^{\Int}},
    \qquad i\neq j,
\label{eq:onsager-int-off}
\end{align}
where \(D_i^{\Vac}\) and \(D_i^{\Int}\) are the mechanism-specific diffusion
coefficients of element \(i\), and \(f_0^{\Vac}\) and \(f_0^{\Int}\) are the
corresponding correlation factors. The net defect fluxes follow from conservation of
lattice sites,
\begin{align}
\mathbf{J}_{\Vac} &= -\sum_i \mathbf{J}_i^{\Vac},
\label{eq:netflux-vac}\\
\mathbf{J}_{\Int} &= \sum_i \mathbf{J}_i^{\Int},
\label{eq:netflux-int}
\end{align}
and give rise to a lattice velocity
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_{\lat}^{\mathrm{RIS}}=\mathbf{J}_{\Vac}-\mathbf{J}_{\Int} .
\label{eq:vlat-ris}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Grain-boundary sink condition.}
The boundary is treated as a sink of finite efficiency for both point defects. At
the boundary plane the defect fluxes obey the Robin conditions
\begin{align}
\left.\mathbf{J}_{\Vac}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x}\right|_{x=0}
 &= k_{\Vac}^{\GB}\left(\left.C_{\Vac}\right|_{x=0}-C_{\Vac}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right),
\label{eq:sink-vac}\\
\left.\mathbf{J}_{\Int}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x}\right|_{x=0}
 &= k_{\Int}^{\GB}\left(\left.C_{\Int}\right|_{x=0}-C_{\Int}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right),
\label{eq:sink-int}
\end{align}
where \(C_{\Vac}^{\mathrm{eq}}\) and \(C_{\Int}^{\mathrm{eq}}\) are the
thermal-equilibrium site fractions and \(k_{\Vac}^{\GB}\), \(k_{\Int}^{\GB}\) are
the boundary transfer coefficients of the two defects. The perfect-sink
(Dirichlet) condition \(C_{\Vac}=C_{\Vac}^{\mathrm{eq}}\),
\(C_{\Int}=C_{\Int}^{\mathrm{eq}}\) is recovered for \(k^{\GB}\to\infty\). With the
Dirichlet condition the defect gradients at the boundary, and with them both the
magnitude and the width of the segregation profiles, do not converge under mesh
refinement, a behaviour also reported for other Onsager-type RIS models
\parencite{oplinger2026radiationinduced}; the finite-efficiency condition removes
this dependence (Supplementary Information, Sec.~S1). The transfer coefficients
are calibrated together with the defect-mediated transport coefficients,
Sec.~\ref{sec:methodRISCalibration}.

\subsection{Grain-Boundary Oxidation Model}
\label{sec:methodGBOxidation}

The oxidized zone is treated as a channel of half-width \(L(y,t)\), the
distance from the boundary mid-plane to the oxide/metal interface, which the
oxide phases of thickness \(L_k\) fill, \(L=\sum_k L_k\).
%% CHECK: relation between L and the phase thicknesses L_k must match the
%% filling rule used in the code.
The oxide is mixed locally rather than layered, so a single effective
diffusivity applies across and along the boundary,
\begin{equation}
D_{\Ox}^{\eff}=\sum_k f_k\,D_{\Ox}^{k},
\label{eq:Deff}
\end{equation}
with \(f_k\) the volume fraction and \(D_{\Ox}^{k}\) the oxygen diffusivity of
phase \(k\). Integrating the two-dimensional oxygen balance over the
cross-section gives, for the section-averaged concentration
\(\bar{C}_{\Ox}(y,t)\),
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bigl(L\,\bar{C}_{\Ox}\bigr)
 &= -\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\bigl(L\,J_{\Ox}\bigr)-J_{\mathrm{r}},
\label{eq:O-mass}\\
J_{\Ox} &= -D_{\Ox}^{\eff}\,\frac{\partial \bar{C}_{\Ox}}{\partial y},
\label{eq:O-flux}
\end{align}
where \(J_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the oxygen flux reaching the interface.
Equation~\eqref{eq:O-mass} is exact up to terms of order \(\partial L/\partial y\)
and makes no assumption on the lateral concentration profile; \(L\) inside the
time derivative accounts for the dilution of dissolved oxygen as the section
widens (Supplementary Information, Sec.~S3).

The relation between \(J_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\bar{C}_{\Ox}\) is obtained from
the fundamental separable mode of the steady diffusion equation in the
cross-section, \(C_{\Ox}\propto\cos(\mu x/L)\), which satisfies the symmetry
condition at the mid-plane and the reaction condition at the interface. It gives
\begin{equation}
J_{\mathrm{r}}=\frac{D_{\Ox}^{\eff}\,\mu^{2}\,\bar{C}_{\Ox}}{L},
\qquad
C_{\Ox}^{\intf}=\bar{C}_{\Ox}\,\mu\cot\mu ,
\label{eq:film-transport}
\end{equation}
where \(C_{\Ox}^{\intf}\) is the oxygen concentration at the interface and the
dimensionless eigenvalue \(\mu\in(0,\pi/2)\) is fixed at every position by the
interfacial balance between oxygen supply and consumption,
\begin{equation}
\frac{D_{\Ox}^{\eff}\,\mu^{2}\,\bar{C}_{\Ox}}{L}
=\sum_k J_{\mathrm{r}}^{k}\!\left(\bar{C}_{\Ox}\,\mu\cot\mu\right).
\label{eq:qss-closure}
\end{equation}
The interfacial reaction rates of the four oxide phases are
\begin{align}
J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}}
 &= k_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}}\,
    C_{\Ox}^{\intf}\,C_{\mathrm{Cr}}^{\GB},
\label{eq:rate-cr2o3}\\
J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}}
 &= k_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}}
    \left(C_{\Ox}^{\intf}-C_{\Ox}^{\thr}\right)
    C_{\mathrm{Fe}}^{\GB},
\label{eq:rate-fe3o4}\\
J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{FeCr_2O_4}}
 &= k_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{FeCr_2O_4}}\,
    C_{\Ox}^{\intf}
    \left(\frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{Fe}}^{\GB}}
         +\frac{2}{C_{\mathrm{Cr}}^{\GB}}\right)^{-1},
\label{eq:rate-fecr2o4}\\
J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{SiO_2}}
 &= k_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{SiO_2}}\,
    C_{\Ox}^{\intf}\,C_{\mathrm{Si}}^{\GB},
\label{eq:rate-sio2}
\end{align}
where \(k_{\mathrm{r}}^{k}\) is the interfacial rate constant of phase \(k\) and
\(C_{\Ox}^{\thr}\) is the threshold oxygen concentration for magnetite formation;
their sum is the total interfacial consumption,
\begin{equation}
J_{\mathrm{r}}
 = J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}}+J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}}
  +J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{FeCr_2O_4}}+J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{SiO_2}} .
\label{eq:rate-total}
\end{equation}
The left-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:qss-closure} increases and the right-hand
side decreases monotonically with \(\mu\), so the root is unique; it is found by
Newton's method. In the reaction-limited limit, \(\mu^{2}\simeq k L/D_{\Ox}^{\eff}\)
with \(k=\mathrm{d}J_{\mathrm{r}}/\mathrm{d}C_{\Ox}^{\intf}\), the interface sees the
average concentration, \(C_{\Ox}^{\intf}\simeq\bar{C}_{\Ox}\); in the
diffusion-limited limit \(\mu\to\pi/2\), \(C_{\Ox}^{\intf}\to0\) and
\(J_{\mathrm{r}}\to\pi^{2}D_{\Ox}^{\eff}\bar{C}_{\Ox}/4L\). The closure thus spans
both regimes without a prescribed transport resistance; its derivation, the
neglected higher modes and its range of validity are given in the Supplementary
Information, Sec.~S3. The corresponding rates of metal removal follow from the
stoichiometry of each phase,
\begin{align}
J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Cr}}
 &= \tfrac{2}{3}J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}}
   +\tfrac{2}{4}J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{FeCr_2O_4}},
\label{eq:sink-cr}\\
J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Fe}}
 &= \tfrac{3}{4}J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}}
   +\tfrac{1}{4}J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{FeCr_2O_4}},
\label{eq:sink-fe}\\
J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Si}}
 &= \tfrac{1}{2}J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{SiO_2}} .
\label{eq:sink-si}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Interfacial mass redistribution.}
The element with the largest ratio of consumption to availability sets the rate
at which the oxidation front advances into the metal,
\begin{equation}
v_{\lat}^{\ox,\max}=\max_{i}\frac{J_{\mathrm{r}}^{i}}{C_i^{\GB}} ,
\label{eq:sweep-velocity}
\end{equation}
with \(J_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{Ni}}=0\) since Ni is not oxidized. The front
sweeps element \(i\) at the rate \(v_{\lat}^{\ox,\max}C_i^{\GB}\), of which only
\(J_{\mathrm{r}}^{i}\) is oxidized; the excess
\begin{equation}
E_i=\max\!\left(v_{\lat}^{\ox,\max}C_i^{\GB}-J_{\mathrm{r}}^{i},\,0\right)
\label{eq:excess}
\end{equation}
is either rejected into the metal ahead of the front or removed through the
oxide, where it is retained as unoxidized solute or transported along the oxide
grain boundaries to the surface \parencite{deng2020corrosion}. The fraction
removed through the oxide is
\begin{equation}
\lambda_i=\frac{v_{\lat}^{\ox,\max}}{v_{\lat}^{\ox,\max}+v_i^{\mathrm{r}}} ,
\label{eq:capture-fraction}
\end{equation}
where \(v_i^{\mathrm{r}}\) is the redistribution velocity of element \(i\), the
characteristic velocity at which it can be rejected into the metal. Complete
rejection (\(\lambda_i=0\)) is recovered for \(v_i^{\mathrm{r}}\to\infty\); for
\(v_i^{\mathrm{r}}\ll v_{\lat}^{\ox,\max}\) the front outruns the rejection and
the excess is carried into the oxide (\(\lambda_i\to1\)). The net removal of
element \(i\) from the metal is then
\begin{equation}
\tilde{J}_{\mathrm{r}}^{i}=J_{\mathrm{r}}^{i}+\lambda_i E_i ,
\label{eq:sink-capture}
\end{equation}
which replaces \(J_{\mathrm{r}}^{i}\) in the interfacial conditions of
Sec.~\ref{sec:methodLattice}. The motivation for this treatment and its limiting
behaviour are given in the Supplementary Information, Sec.~S2.

\subsection{Coupling Strategy and Simulation Protocol}
\label{sec:methodCoupling}

\subsubsection{Lattice Velocity and Metal Consumption}
\label{sec:methodLattice}
Metal atoms leave the metal domain only at the grain-boundary plane, either by
oxidation or by removal through the oxide, Eq.~\eqref{eq:sink-capture}. Because
the metal lattice is incompressible, this removal creates no source in the
interior: the entire lattice translates towards the interface at the velocity set
by the net interfacial removal. Denoting by
\begin{equation}
v_{\ox}(y,t)=\sum_i \tilde{J}_{\mathrm{r}}^{i}
 =\sum_i\left(J_{\mathrm{r}}^{i}+\lambda_i E_i\right)
\label{eq:vlat-ox}
\end{equation}
the recession rate of the metal/oxide interface, which lies between the
oxidation rate \(\sum_i J_{\mathrm{r}}^{i}\) (complete rejection) and the sweep
velocity \(v_{\lat}^{\ox,\max}\) (complete removal), the total lattice velocity in
the metal domain is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_{\lat}(x,y,t)
 =\mathbf{v}_{\lat}^{\mathrm{RIS}}(x,y,t)
  +v_{\ox}(y,t)\,\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x} .
\label{eq:vlat-total}
\end{equation}
The two contributions differ in character. The segregation term is local: it is
determined pointwise by the defect fluxes at \((x,y)\). The oxidation term is
not; it is evaluated at the boundary and is independent of \(x\), so a single
value applies across the whole domain at a given \(y\). Segregation therefore
redistributes the lattice internally, whereas oxidation translates it rigidly, so that the total flux of alloying element \(i\) entering
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mass-metal} is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{J}_i
 = \mathbf{J}_i^{\Vac}+\mathbf{J}_i^{\Int}
  +C_i\mathbf{v}_{\lat} ,
\label{eq:flux-total}
\end{equation}
subject to the interfacial condition
\begin{equation}
\left.\mathbf{J}_i\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x}\right|_{x=0}
 = \tilde{J}_{\mathrm{r}}^{i} ,
\label{eq:flux-bc}
\end{equation}
which removes from the boundary plane the atoms incorporated into the growing
oxide or carried away through it.

Equations~\eqref{eq:flux-total} and \eqref{eq:flux-bc} collect the three ways in which
an alloying element can move. The first two terms are the diffusive fluxes carried by
the two defect mechanisms, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:flux-vac} and \eqref{eq:flux-int}; the third
is the drift of the entire lattice relative to the laboratory frame; the interfacial
consumption enters not as a bulk term but as an outgoing flux on the boundary plane.

The two contributions to \(\mathbf{v}_{\lat}\) have different origins but act
identically on every species. \(\mathbf{v}_{\lat}^{\mathrm{RIS}}\) arises because the
vacancy and interstitial fluxes are unequal, so lattice sites are created and destroyed
at different rates; \(v_{\ox}\) arises because oxidation consumes metal atoms from the
interfacial layer, so the lattice is displaced towards the advancing front. Because the
drift term \(C_i\mathbf{v}_{\lat}\) is proportional to the local concentration and
carries no species selectivity, it does not by itself alter the relative composition;
selectivity enters only through the mechanism-specific diffusivities in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:onsager-vac-diag}--\eqref{eq:onsager-int-off}, through the
stoichiometric sinks in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:sink-cr}--\eqref{eq:sink-si}, and through
the element-specific redistribution fractions \(\lambda_i\) of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:capture-fraction}.

\subsubsection{Sequential Irradiation--Oxidation Protocol}
\label{sec:methodSequential}



The reference experiments irradiate the specimen first and expose it to the primary
water environment afterwards. The model reproduces this protocol as two consecutive
stages that share the same governing equations and differ only in two switches: the
dose rate \(K_0\) and the oxygen boundary condition at the grain-boundary mouth.

In the irradiation stage the specimen is irradiated to the target dose with no oxygen
present, \(K_0>0\) and \(C_{\Ox}\equiv 0\), so that
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:mass-metal}--\eqref{eq:vlat-ris} govern the evolution alone and all
interfacial reaction rates vanish identically. The stage is integrated to
\(t_{\mathrm{irr}}=\Phi/K_0\), where \(\Phi\) is the nominal dose in dpa, and yields the
segregated composition profiles \(C_i(x,y,t_{\mathrm{irr}})\).

The oxidation stage continues from the final state of the irradiation stage: all
concentration fields, including the point-defect populations, are inherited unchanged,
\begin{equation}
\left.\left(C_i,\,C_{\Vac},\,C_{\Int}\right)\right|_{t=0^{+}}
=\left.\left(C_i,\,C_{\Vac},\,C_{\Int}\right)\right|_{t=t_{\mathrm{irr}}},
\qquad
\left.L_k\right|_{t=0^{+}}=0 ,
\label{eq:handoff}
\end{equation}
and the exposure clock is restarted from zero. The dose rate is then set to zero,
\(K_0=0\), so that the inherited defect supersaturation decays by recombination
according to Eqs.~\eqref{eq:mass-vac} and \eqref{eq:mass-int} without further
production.

Simultaneously, the oxygen boundary condition at the mouth of the boundary is switched
on. Oxygen ingress is limited by the surface film that grows on the free surface, whose
resistance increases as the film thickens; this is imposed as a Robin condition with a
time-decaying transfer coefficient,
\begin{equation}
\left.J_{\Ox}\right|_{y=0}
= h(t)\left(C_{\Ox}^{\mathrm{w}}-\left.\bar{C}_{\Ox}\right|_{y=0}\right),
\qquad
h(t)=\frac{k_{\mathrm{s}}}{\sqrt{t}+\sqrt{t_0}} ,
\label{eq:robin}
\end{equation}
where \(C_{\Ox}^{\mathrm{w}}\) is the oxygen concentration of the bulk water,
\(k_{\mathrm{s}}\) is the surface transfer coefficient, and \(t_0\) regularizes the
initial instant. The \(t^{-1/2}\) decay reproduces the parabolic thickening of the
surface film, so that the cumulative oxygen admitted through the mouth grows as
\(t^{1/2}\). The deep end of the boundary is closed,
\(\left.J_{\Ox}\right|_{y=L_{\GB}}=0\).

Because no point defects are produced once \(K_0=0\), the inherited supersaturation
decays on the recombination timescale, which is short compared with the exposure time;
segregation therefore effectively ceases early in the exposure stage, and the
composition field that the oxidation model sees is the one established during
irradiation. The sequential protocol thus isolates the compositional pathway: any dose
dependence of the predicted oxidation depth must be carried by the radiation-induced
segregation profile inherited through Eq.~\eqref{eq:handoff}, since neither radiolysis
nor sustained irradiation-assisted transport is present.

\subsubsection{Fully Coupled Irradiation--Oxidation Framework}
\label{sec:methodFullyCoupled}

The sequential protocol of Sec.~\ref{sec:methodSequential} is a special case of the
governing equations rather than a separate model: it is obtained by switching \(K_0\)
and the mouth boundary condition on and off in turn. Leaving both switches on
throughout, \(K_0>0\) together with the Robin condition Eq.~\eqref{eq:robin}, gives the
fully coupled problem in which segregation and oxidation proceed simultaneously, as
they do in reactor service. No additional equations or parameters are required;
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:mass-metal}--\eqref{eq:vlat-ris} are integrated as a single stage.

In this mode the two processes feed back on each other. Segregation continues to
modify the boundary composition while the oxidation front advances, so the composition
that the interfacial reactions see evolves in time rather than being fixed at its
end-of-irradiation value; conversely, the metal consumed at the front and the
associated lattice drift, Eq.~\eqref{eq:vlat-ox}, alter the concentration field on
which the segregation fluxes act. Predictions obtained in this mode are reported in
Sec.~\ref{sec:resultsFullyCoupled}; because no experiment in the present dataset
imposes concurrent irradiation and exposure, this mode is used only for prediction and
takes no part in the calibration of Sec.~\ref{sec:methodCalibration}.

\subsection{Model Parameterization and Calibration}
\label{sec:methodCalibration}
The two components are parameterized and calibrated separately and against separate data. The RIS model is calibrated first; the oxidation model is then calibrated with the RIS model related parameters fixed. 

\subsubsection{RIS Parameterization}
\label{sec:methodRISCalibration}

The RIS model requires the mechanism-specific diffusion coefficients
\(D_i^{\Vac}\) and \(D_i^{\Int}\) of the four alloying elements, the correlation
factors \(f_0^{\Vac}\) and \(f_0^{\Int}\), the recombination coefficient \(R\),
the damage efficiency \(\epsilon\), the dose rate \(K_0\), and the grain-boundary
transfer coefficients \(k_{\Vac}^{\GB}\) and \(k_{\Int}^{\GB}\) of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:sink-vac} and \eqref{eq:sink-int}. The dose rate and
irradiation temperature are those of the reference experiment. The correlation
factors are taken from their crystallographic values for the fcc lattice. The
damage efficiency accounts for the fraction of displacements that survive
in-cascade recombination and escape as freely migrating defects; its value is
that reported for proton irradiation. The vacancy and interstitial migration
energies and pre-exponential factors are taken from the literature and adjusted
within their reported ranges, jointly with the transfer coefficients, to reproduce
both the magnitude and the width of the measured grain-boundary composition
profiles across the dose series. Because these profiles are measured
before exposure, the RIS calibration is independent of the oxidation model. The
resulting values are given in Table~\ref{tab:params-ris}.


\subsubsection{Oxidation Kinetics Parameterization}
\label{sec:methodGBOXidationCalibration}
The oxidation model requires the interfacial rate constants \(k_{\mathrm{r}}^{k}\)
of the four oxide phases, their oxygen diffusivities \(D_{\Ox}^{k}\), the surface
transfer coefficient \(k_{\mathrm{s}}\) of the Robin condition
Eq.~\eqref{eq:robin}, the magnetite threshold \(C_{\Ox}^{\thr}\), and the
redistribution velocities \(v_i^{\mathrm{r}}\) of Eq.~\eqref{eq:capture-fraction}. The oxide
molar volumes and densities used in the thickness update are taken from
crystallographic data and held fixed.

The model is calibrated against the dose-dependent grain-boundary oxidation
depths of \textcite{deng2017effect}, measured after exposure in simulated PWR primary water
at 0, 0.5, and 3 dpa. The predicted oxidation depth is defined as the
distance from the boundary mouth to the deepest position at which the total oxide
thickness \(\sum_k L_k\) exceeds a fixed threshold \(L_{\thr}\), applied
identically at every dose. The free parameters are adjusted to reproduce the
measured depths at all three doses simultaneously, using a single parameter set;
no parameter is allowed to take different values at different doses. The
resulting values are given in Table~\ref{tab:params-ox}.

boundary values at \SI{3.0}{\dpa} are reproduced for all three elements; at
\SI{0.5}{\dpa} the model underestimates the Cr depletion and the Si enrichment.

The widths of the profiles differ between the two doses. The measured profiles
return to their matrix values within roughly \SI{5}{nm} of the boundary at
\SI{0.5}{\dpa} and within roughly \SI{8}{nm} at \SI{3.0}{\dpa}. The calculated
\SI{0.5}{\dpa} profiles reproduce this width, whereas the calculated
\SI{3.0}{\dpa} profiles are broader than measured: \SI{15}{nm} from the boundary
the calculated Cr and Ni have not yet returned to their matrix values. The dose
dependence of the segregation magnitude is therefore captured, while the width
of the segregated zone at the higher dose is overestimated.

Figure~\ref{fig:edx-compare} compares the calculated composition across the
intergranular oxide with the EDX line scans of \textcite{deng2017effect}. The
measurements show the signature of selective oxidation: Cr is enriched and Fe
depleted in the oxide relative to the matrix. On the metal side of the interface,
however, the scans show no significant Cr depletion or Fe enrichment, and only a
slight Ni enrichment. In the irradiated specimens the Cr profile exhibits a
minimum at the centre of the oxide that deepens with increasing dose. This
indicates that the Cr concentration at the advancing interface is set by the
radiation-induced segregation profile and is not modulated by the selective
oxidation: the shape of the segregation profile is retained as the front moves
into the metal.

A model in which all unoxidized metal is rejected to the metal side cannot
reproduce this behaviour. Because Cr reacts much faster than Fe, such a model
depletes Cr and enriches Fe strongly at the interface, in conflict with the
scans. With the interfacial mass redistribution of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:sweep-velocity}--\eqref{eq:sink-capture}, the excess Fe and Ni
swept by the front are carried away through the oxide instead, as proposed by
\textcite{deng2020corrosion}, and the calculated profiles reproduce both the
retained segregation shape and the central Cr minimum, whose depth increases
with dose in the measurements and in the model. The calculated Cr and Fe contents
of the oxide follow the measured dose dependence: less Cr is available at the
front after irradiation, so the oxide that forms there is poorer in Cr and richer
in Fe. No Ni oxide is observed experimentally \parencite{deng2021grain}; the small
amount of Ni detected in the oxide is treated as unoxidized solute retained in
the oxide, which the model represents through the Ni fraction
\(\lambda_{\mathrm{Ni}}\). The calculated profiles are plotted with the oxide
formed first located at the centre of the intergranular oxide, consistent with
symmetric growth into both grains.

Silica formed at the boundary is not retained: the segregated Si is oxidized
preferentially and the resulting \ce{SiO2} dissolves into the water, leaving
porosity in the intergranular oxide \parencite{du2026silicon}. The model adopts
this behaviour explicitly. All \ce{SiO2} produced is treated as dissolved and the
volume it occupied is retained as free volume available for oxygen transport, so
the calculated \ce{SiO2} volume fraction is a measure of the porosity generated
within the intergranular oxide rather than of a phase present in it.

The oxidation parameters are calibrated against the dose dependence of the

\label{sec:resultsPredictiveValidation}

\begin{subfigure}[t]{0.48\textwidth}\centering

  \caption{}\label{fig:front-time-abs}
\end{subfigure}\hfill
\begin{subfigure}[t]{0.48\textwidth}\centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{front_vs_time_normalized}
  \caption{}\label{fig:front-time-norm}
\end{subfigure}
\caption{Predicted and measured time dependence of the intergranular oxide
penetration depth at 0.5 and \SI{1.5}{\dpa}, obtained with the calibrated
parameters held fixed; neither the \SI{1.5}{\dpa} dose nor the exposure times
shown enter the calibration. (a) Penetration depth versus exposure time.
(b) Penetration depth normalized by the maximum depth over both doses,
\(L/L_{\max}\), taken separately for the calculations (\(L_{\max}=\SI{110}{nm}\))
and the measurements (\(L_{\max}=\SI{231}{nm}\)). Measurements from
\textcite{deng2020corrosion}.}
\label{fig:front-time}
\end{figure}

With the parameters fixed by the calibration of Fig.~\ref{fig:front-dose}, the
model is used to predict the time dependence of the penetration depth, which is
compared in Fig.~\ref{fig:front-time} with the measurements of
\textcite{deng2020corrosion} at 0.5 and \SI{1.5}{\dpa} after 100 and
\SI{1500}{h}. At \SI{100}{h} the calculated and measured depths are of the same
order, 20--\SI{40}{nm}. At \SI{1500}{h} the model gives about 95 and
\SI{110}{nm} against measured values of about 194 and \SI{231}{nm}, roughly half
(Fig.~\ref{fig:front-time-abs}). The two experimental series are, however, not
mutually consistent: although the authors report the same material, irradiation
and exposure conditions, the apparent effective diffusivity of the oxidation
front, \(\bar{D}_{\mathrm{eff}}=L^{2}/2t\), is larger for the \SI{0.5}{\dpa}
specimen of \textcite{deng2020corrosion} than for the \SI{3.0}{\dpa} specimen of
\textcite{deng2017effect} (Supplementary Information, Table~S1). A model
calibrated on the 2017 series is therefore expected to underestimate the depths
of the 2020 series. When the depths are normalized by the maximum over both
doses, Fig.~\ref{fig:front-time-norm}, the calculated \SI{0.5}{\dpa} value at
\SI{1500}{h}, 0.85, matches the measured 0.84, so the ratio between the two doses
at long times is reproduced; at \SI{100}{h} the calculated normalized depths,
about 0.20 and 0.25, exceed the measured 0.12 and 0.18, i.e.\ the calculated
kinetics are relatively faster at short times. The model thus predicts the
relative effect of dose at long exposure, while the absolute rate is tied to the
series used for calibration.
